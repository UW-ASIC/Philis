Manufacturability, density and final geometry

Close grid, spacing, enclosure, fill, redundancy and pattern-dependent process requirements.

Manufacturability is the verified ability to fabricate the intended electrical structure. This chapter explains geometric legality, device recognition, final fill and the evidence needed to distinguish a plausible candidate from a checked deliverable.

Manufacturable is a necessary condition, not a performance score

Manufacturability begins with a complete legal structure: correct device layers, widths, spaces, overlaps, enclosures, connectivity, pattern density and process options. These constraints are not rewards that may be traded away for lower area. A minimum-space violation does not become acceptable because a common-centroid score improved. However, passing the geometric checks only establishes the checks that were actually run, using their actual coverage and rule-deck version.

Hastings explains design rules in terms of process bias, linewidth control, mask alignment, outdiffusion and depletion. These ingredients justify why rules vary by layer pair, device type and voltage. Modern rule decks encode much richer interactions than the simplified teaching examples. The automation should consume the authoritative deck and preserve its semantics rather than infer an all-purpose spacing from feature size. PDF pp. 130–150

Where the engine supports only rectangles, document which polygon operations, angles and derived layers are represented and delegate the remainder to signoff.

Worked example: a legal line that cannot accept a via

Suppose a hypothetical metal layer permits 100 nm line width and 100 nm spacing. A via cut is 80 nm wide, and the connected metal must extend 30 nm beyond it on the two relevant sides. The minimum landing width is therefore 80 + 2×30 = 140 nm. A router using a 200 nm pitch from line width plus spacing will discover that a 140 nm landing beside an unchanged neighboring wire violates the original spacing reservation.

The necessary response is not a late arbitrary jog. First compute legal landing templates, including asymmetric enclosures if the PDK permits them. Next choose track/via sites that allow those templates. Reserve any enlarged footprints in congestion estimation. Finally check actual emitted polygons and via connectivity. These numbers are illustrative; the book's argument about lead-to-lead, via-to-via and via-to-lead pitches establishes the mechanism. PDF pp. 812–815

A manufacturing grid adds another condition. A path of odd width centered on an integer grid can put its edges on half-grid positions. Either choose a supported centerline grid, emit legal rectangles directly, or select a width whose edges are representable. Do not silently round each edge independently: that can shrink enclosure or break the equality of supposedly matched wires.

Validate this example with the exact shape generator, not with a test of the algebra used to choose the pitch. Include a neighboring via, a channel intersection and both legal orientations of an asymmetric landing. Those cases expose errors a straight isolated route cannot reveal.

Final material changes the device and the verification result

Fill insertion is often deferred until late in a flow, but it adds material near devices and wires. That material affects capacitance, stress, hydrogen access and RF losses. A successful pre-fill mismatch estimate therefore cannot automatically be promoted to a final post-fill guarantee. Hastings discusses metal-fill exclusions for matched MOS devices, controlled surroundings for passive arrays and removal or careful design of dummy material near inductors. PDF pp. 703–704, 716, 363

Worked tradeoff. A capacitor array may benefit from an electrostatic shield that fixes its field boundary. That shield increases local metal density and may need slotting; slots alter the shield and its resistance. Meanwhile, a nearby critical MOS array may prefer an over-gate metal exclusion to preserve passivation uniformity. A universal instruction to “fill empty area” or “shield all precision devices” cannot satisfy both. The floorplanner should reserve separate environmental regions and the finalizer should solve density with those constraints present.

Use an iterative finalization loop: generate candidate material; run density and geometry checks; extract or update the relevant parasitics; evaluate device environment and circuit budgets; revise the fill or the macro plan when a budget fails. Cache checks whose dependency regions did not change, but invalidate a result whenever its actual physical inputs change. A final layout checksum should identify the geometry to which the evidence applies.

A manufacturability evidence bundle

  1. Freeze the inputs. Retain PDK/deck/model versions, device options, package choice, layer mapping, grid, netlist and scenario specifications. This makes later comparison meaningful.
  2. Validate primitives before composition. Qualified device, dummy, tap, guard, pad and via templates reduce the chance that every optimization move invents a new structure.
  3. Check assembled geometry and extraction. Abutment, rotation, routing and merging can invalidate a previously legal primitive or change the extracted device network.
  4. Run electrical and reliability checks with their required inputs. DRC does not substitute for antenna history, EM current distribution, voltage-aware checks, ESD path verification or latchup qualification.
  5. Finalize fill, slots, seals and pad structures. The manufactured database is the object that must pass; do not attach a pre-finalization certificate to it.
  6. Record exceptions and unknowns explicitly. A foundry-approved waiver has different status from an engine feature that has not been implemented.
  7. Archive the actual deliverables and reports. Include libraries and layer maps needed to reconstruct the design, not merely a screenshot or optimizer seed.

The source's final checklist supplies the basis for this evidence-oriented completion criterion. It includes pad/bonding requirements, Kelvin locations, high-current paths, antenna, noise separation, latchup precautions, fill exclusions and archive completeness. PDF pp. 823–825

Philis priority. Preserve the distinction between a cheap optimization surrogate and the authoritative acceptance check. A legal bbox placement is useful progress; a post-route, post-fill checked design is a stronger result. Surface these distinctions in reports so that the engine can improve incrementally without overstating what any stage guarantees.

Voltage-aware legality and the limits of a waiver

A voltage-aware spacing rule needs the maximum differential voltage that can occur between the relevant neighboring shapes. Merely recording that one belongs to a “high-voltage net” loses information. Consider hypothetical nets A and B that are both 5 V during steady operation. If startup allows A = 5 V while B remains at 0 V, their spacing cannot be justified by the steady-state zero difference. Conversely, two nets that track by an explicit circuit guarantee may have a smaller differential than a naive combination of unrelated extremes. The checker must evaluate allowed joint scenarios, retain the guarantee and include its failure conditions. PDF p. 821

The same discipline applies to device recognition and exceptions. A DRC exclusion marker does not physically repair a violation, and a waived check is not an ordinary pass. Record the exact geometry, violated rule, justification, approving qualification and scope. A foundry-qualified special device may legitimately rely on a dedicated recognition layer and special rules; a generic rectangle copied from a drawing does not inherit that qualification. When the geometry or device version changes, re-evaluate whether the exception still applies.

For an automated engine, distinguish hard failure, verified pass, qualified exception and missing evidence. These states prevent an optimizer from exploiting a missing model as though it were permission. They also make development practical: an early candidate can be useful while still reporting that final density, antenna or special-device recognition awaits the designated signoff check. The final “manufacturable” claim should refer to the exact final geometry and declared rule domain, not to a list of checks performed on earlier versions.
