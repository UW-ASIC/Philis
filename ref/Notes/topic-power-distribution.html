Power, ground, IR drop and current paths

Size complete supply and return networks, contacts and vias from electrical scenarios.

A supply network delivers voltage differences through finite impedance

“Connected to VDD” or “connected to ground” is a topological statement. An analog block requires a sufficiently accurate voltage difference at its actual device terminals over current, frequency, process, temperature, and operating mode. An optimal distribution network meets that electrical requirement while respecting interconnect reliability, noise isolation, matching, and area. It includes the forward path, return path, contacts, vias, package impedance, and any circuit-defined sensing or decoupling elements.

This chapter combines the EM book’s branch-current and routing methodology with the substrate book’s analysis of reference impedance and noise conversion. The worked supply calculations, network equations, and proposed engine architecture are instructional synthesis grounded in those mechanisms. They are not numerical design rules from a PDK. Current topology; Ground/reference connections and isolation.

1. Classify nets by circuit function, not only by their names

A power rail normally supplies sustained current and may also carry large transients. A bias-current line may be a signal net in the netlist yet require substantial ampacity and low resistance. A voltage-bias line feeding gates may have negligible DC load but be highly sensitive to leakage, capacitive feedthrough, startup charge, or settling time. A reference rail in an ADC can carry short charge-transfer pulses even when its average current is small. An output driver return may be geometrically near a precision reference while having an incompatible noise spectrum.

The engine therefore needs functional annotations: allowed DC drop, AC impedance or noise, branch-current scenarios, sensitivity to coupling, settling requirements, and valid reference nodes. A blanket rule that supplies are wide and all other nets are minimum width misses current-heavy analog signals and noise-sensitive low-current references. Conversely, widening a high-impedance gate-bias net without evaluating capacitance can worsen startup or interference pickup.

Specify the observation points. The voltage at a supply pad, at a distribution trunk, at a local source contact, and at a sense terminal are different quantities once current flows. A regulation loop can maintain one of them while another moves. This distinction gives the router a concrete error budget rather than a vague preference for “strong power metal.”

2. Branch currents follow conservation

In a lumped tree, an upstream branch carries the algebraic sum of all downstream currents. Include distributed interconnect shunt/displacement currents as loads when analyzing transients; the DC example has no such contribution. The figure’s 3.5 mA trunk cannot be sized from the largest individual 2 mA load alone. Each branch can use a different width or layer because its demand, length, and allowable drop differ. In a mesh, current divides according to the network resistances; altering one segment changes currents elsewhere.

For time-dependent loads, simultaneity matters. If three current pulses occur together, their peaks add on the trunk; if they occur at separate times, the maximum can be lower. Preserve valid switching scenarios and compute the branch waveform before its average, RMS, and peak metrics. A single net-current scalar may be a conservative screen, but cannot characterize every branch accurately. This is the EM book’s central motivation for signed terminal-current and topology-aware modeling. Terminal and branch-current modeling, PDF 83–90.

3. Derive DC voltage-drop requirements from the actual path

R = ρL/(wt) = RsheetL/w; ΔVpath = Σ IeRe.

Sheet resistance is convenient for uniform layers, but contacts, vias, bends, local width changes, and pin access add resistance. Temperature and process corners change these values. The load current may also depend on its delivered voltage, so a strongly nonlinear analog network needs a circuit solution rather than a one-way drop calculation.

Illustrative example. A trunk 200 µm long and 2 µm wide with sheet resistance 0.08 Ω/square has 100 squares and 8 Ω resistance. At 3.5 mA it drops 28 mV. Increasing width to 8 µm reduces the uniform-metal resistance to 2 Ω and the drop to 7 mV, before via and contact contributions. This fourfold width consumes routing area and increases some capacitances; it is justified only if the resulting electrical and geometric tradeoff is acceptable.

Suppose load A’s 2 mA branch adds 2 Ω, giving another 4 mV. Its forward-path drop is then 32 mV with the original trunk. If its return shares a 1.5 Ω trunk carrying 3.5 mA and has a 1 Ω local branch carrying 2 mA, the return rises by 5.25 + 2 = 7.25 mV. The local supply difference is reduced by 39.25 mV. Checking only the forward rail would miss nearly one fifth of this example’s total loss.

Supply headroom, source degeneration, reference accuracy, and output swing can each impose a different budget. Allocate drop by path and sensitivity instead of distributing an equal millivolt allowance to every segment. Reducing a high-current shared trunk often helps many loads at once; fixing a local branch may be necessary for a particularly sensitive load even when the trunk passes.

4. A small reference error can be a large analog error

For a MOS device, a local linear perturbation can be written ΔI ≈ gmΔVGS + gmbΔVBS + gdsΔVDS. Unequal source, body, or drain access resistances can therefore spoil matching even if the intrinsic device geometries are identical. Equal metal lengths are only a proxy: different layer resistance, width, contacts, and loading can produce unequal voltage drops at equal length.

Illustrative sensitivity. A device carrying 100 µA with gm = 2 mS experiences approximately 2 µA current change from a 1 mV change in gate-to-source voltage under a local fixed-other-bias approximation. That is 2% of its current. A millivolt that is negligible on a 1.8 V supply may be unacceptable at a matched source connection. This is why a supply or return network should be scored by circuit error as well as percentage rail drop.

The mismatch source emphasizes bias-dependent sensitivities and propagation of physical variation; the same principle applies to deterministic routing-induced voltage errors. Extract the network, update device biases, and evaluate the circuit quantity that is specified. Do not combine a deterministic mean error with a random standard deviation without a stated statistical model. Sensitivity propagation; Bias dependence.

5. Kelvin connections remove a particular error path

A sense path carrying negligible load current develops little load-induced IR drop. By attaching it at the intended device or resistor terminal, a circuit can observe the local voltage beyond the force-path resistance. Attaching it upstream of the contact or narrow access section leaves that omitted drop unobserved. Thus pin access and the exact physical sense junction matter.

Kelvin routing is not simply duplicating a wire on one undifferentiated net. The circuit must provide an appropriate high-impedance sense function, and extraction must preserve the physical force/sense topology. Input bias currents, leakage, coupling, thermoelectric effects in applicable structures, and finite sense-path impedance can still matter. A placer/router can honor an existing force/sense contract and select its access geometry; it should not silently invent a feedback connection or change the netlist to create one.

For differential sensing, both sense paths need compatible coupling and reference behavior. A perfectly low-resistance sense wire can still pick up interference through capacitance. The correct objective is the sensed error, not simply the minimum wire resistance.

6. AC power integrity requires impedance, not only resistance

ΔV(ω) = Zdistribution(ω)ΔI(ω).

The impedance includes metal and contact resistance, loop inductance, device and decoupling capacitance, package parasitics, and any regulator or reference dynamics. A wide rail may reduce resistance yet leave inductive ground bounce or a resonant impedance peak. The relevant current contains harmonics set by edge slew and activity, not just the fundamental clock frequency.

A target impedance |Z| ≤ ΔVallowed/|ΔI| is a useful initial frequency-dependent budget for a specified excitation and observation point. It is not a universal scalar limit when multiple sources have phase relationships or the victim is periodically time varying. Evaluate the network’s transfer impedance between each important injection and observation pair.

For an ideal capacitor supplying a constant current for a short interval, ΔV = IΔt/C, so C ≥ IΔt/ΔV is a first estimate. A real capacitor adds ESR-related step ΔI·ESR and ESL-related L·dI/dt, and its connection route contributes additional impedance. Capacitance that is physically distant or attached through a narrow neck may not serve the intended fast transient. Multiple capacitances and inductances can form resonances or antiresonances; adding capacitance is not automatically a monotonic improvement over the entire band. These are circuit-network consequences and should be verified with the actual allowed decoupling structures and models.

Decoupling uses area and may couple through its bottom plate or substrate. The substrate source’s capacitor and passive analysis explains why terminal reference and parasitic paths matter. Adding or resizing a capacitor changes the circuit; routing an already specified capacitor should minimize the relevant connection impedance without inventing a new electrical element. §7.4.1, capacitor terminal choice and model context, PDF 113–115.

7. Keep forward and return paths together where the circuit requires a small loop

Current completes a loop. The magnetic field and loop inductance depend on both paths and the available conducting planes or rails. A short forward wire with a long displaced return can have an unfavorable loop even when its individual length is small. Close appropriate forward/return routing can reduce loop area and magnetic coupling, but it also changes capacitance and consumes routing resources.

“Return” is frequency dependent in a distributed network: displacement current can flow through capacitance and nearby conductors. A nominal ground rail is not automatically the dominant high-frequency return. Use an extracted model over the operating band when loop effects matter. For sensitive differential paths, preserve their intended mutual environment and avoid forcing unrelated switching return current through shared impedance with the receiving reference.

Separate analog and digital labels do not guarantee isolation. If their currents share a bondwire, package segment, local metal neck, or substrate contact network, the shared impedance produces a voltage that both blocks see. The substrate book demonstrates that guard rings and backplates can become noise injectors through their return connections. Improving the return network can therefore improve both power integrity and substrate isolation. Ring and return-reference effects; §8.9, finite-impedance shields, PDF 152–156.

8. Width, layer, via, and topology must be selected jointly

Widening a branch reduces uniform resistance and average current density, but can increase capacitance to nearby signals or the substrate, block other routes, and alter metal-density obligations. Moving to a thicker upper layer may improve resistance and ampacity while requiring additional vias and changing inductive or capacitive coupling. Parallel paths reduce resistance only according to their actual access network; poorly placed via arrays can leave local bottlenecks.

A current-aware objective can minimize metal area ΣLw, or another calibrated cost, subject to voltage, temperature, reliability, and analog performance constraints. The EM book explains why a minimum-length tree is not necessarily optimal once branch currents determine required widths. A mesh offers alternate paths and potentially lower impedance but requires electrical current-sharing analysis and can change coupling and reference topology. It should be chosen for a demonstrated requirement, not as a universal upgrade. §3.3.3, current-aware wire planning, PDF 89–90; §4.6.4, via-array current sharing, PDF 137–140.

Device contacts and local access are part of this optimization. A wide global supply on an upper layer still fails its purpose if only a narrow lower-metal finger connects to the device. The capacitance and geometry of the local supply can also affect matching. Reserve sufficient access and via area early so that late widening does not force asymmetric rerouting of critical analog nets.

9. A concrete analysis and construction loop

  1. Define electrical requirements at actual terminals. Record rail/reference identities, voltage ranges, allowable DC and AC error, sensitivities, and circuit-defined force/sense relationships.
  2. Collect correlated current scenarios. Include nominal bias, load extremes, startup, switching, and other specified modes. Preserve signed terminal currents and uncertainty.
  3. Construct the distribution and return graph. Include pins, contacts, vias, layer resistance, package interfaces, and existing decoupling or regulation models.
  4. Solve DC and relevant frequency/transient cases. Determine delivered voltages, branch currents, reference motion, and dominant shared-impedance paths.
  5. Size and route under combined constraints. Choose topology, layers, widths, and via templates to meet IR, impedance, EM, coupling, and geometry requirements.
  6. Update temperature and device operating points. Resistance and load currents change with temperature and voltage; iterate where the feedback matters.
  7. Re-extract final geometry. Verify local pin access, sense landing, return continuity, actual sharing, and parasitic coupling rather than trusting abstract route widths.
  8. Report a path-based explanation. Name the limiting segment, load/mode, forward and return contributions, noise transfer, required versus actual margin, and missing model coverage.

10. Required data and Philis integration

The process inputs include resistance per layer and via over temperature/corners, thickness and geometry rules, current capabilities, legal access/array templates, coupling models, and permitted decoupling structures. Application inputs include supply and reference tolerances, current waveforms, package/interconnect boundary conditions, operating modes, and sensitive observation points. A scalar current limit or a list of supply-net names is insufficient to derive all these requirements.

The frontend already passes supply_nets near line 138. DetailedCfg contains scalar net_current_ua, and em_width performs current-based width selection with a fixed current-per-width constant. The next useful representation is a branch-annotated electrical graph linked to actual geometry and terminal access. It can serve IR, EM, thermal, and reference-noise analysis consistently instead of letting each rule invent a different current estimate.

Keep construction heuristics and final evidence separate. A supply-width preference can guide routing before full extraction, while an extracted voltage/impedance check determines whether the allocated circuit budget is met. If package impedance or current scenarios are missing, the report should identify the resulting uncertainty rather than claim full power integrity from geometric connectivity.

Validation and limits

Validate a three-load tree with analytically known branch currents and drops; a mesh whose currents change after one branch is widened; simultaneous versus staggered pulses; equal device geometry with unequal source resistance; a wide trunk with a narrow access throat; a force/sense landing before versus after a resistive contact; and a quiet body ring sharing a switching return. Compare DC network results with an independent solver and AC/transient results with extracted circuit analysis. Check current conservation and explicit units, and inspect both forward and return contributions. This chapter derives a power-distribution workflow from the supplied EM and substrate sources plus elementary circuit relations; it does not claim that a single textbook provides a universal analog power-grid template.
