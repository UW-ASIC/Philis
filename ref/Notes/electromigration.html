Fundamentals of Electromigration-Aware Integrated Circuit Design

Jens Lienig and Matthias Thiele · 2018

Current density, thermal effects, vias, stress evolution and reliability-aware routing.

Open supplied PDF · 171 physical pages · File identity and coverage

What this source changes about “optimal” routing

A route is a manufactured, heated conductor carrying a time-dependent current through a particular geometry. Its reliability depends on every narrow section, bend, pin access, via, material boundary, and operating condition. A connected route with legal minimum widths can still fail. The central lesson of Jens Lienig and Matthias Thiele’s Fundamentals of Electromigration-Aware Integrated Circuit Design (2018) is to make these electrical and reliability requirements inputs to construction, not merely checks after routing. An optimal route minimizes its area, parasitics, and routing obstruction subject to a stated service life and failure probability under its mission profile. Chapter 3; constraint-driven design, chapter 5.

This book supplies mechanisms and an architecture for reliability-aware routing. Its historical roadmap projections, example activation energies, current-density limits, frequency boundaries, and proposed future materials are not a foundry reliability deck. They must not be imported as universal constants.

1. Why conductors fail even when connectivity is correct

Electron momentum transfer drives metal atoms along available diffusion paths. Uniform material flow through an ideal uniform conductor does not alone identify the point of damage: accumulation and depletion arise where the flux diverges. Geometric necks, interfaces, grain boundaries, temperature gradients, and blocked ends change that flow. Depletion produces voids and resistance increase; accumulation can produce extrusions and shorts. The first electrical consequence can therefore be degraded gain, bias, settling, or supply regulation before an open circuit appears. A progressively smaller conducting cross-section raises current density and heating, creating positive feedback. Physical mechanisms, PDF 25–33.

Three coupled transport mechanisms matter. Electromigration follows the electrical driving force; thermomigration follows a temperature gradient; stress migration responds to mechanical stress gradients. Stress induced by atom accumulation can oppose EM and help a confined wire, but thermal-expansion mismatch, manufacturing stress, and an unfavorable package or neighboring heater can introduce different stress gradients. A router must distinguish helpful back stress from externally imposed stress rather than assign a generic “stress bonus.” Thermal and stress migration, PDF 45–57.

Diffusion paths depend on the manufactured stack. Grain boundaries are particularly relevant in aluminum; copper surface and interface diffusion can dominate despite copper’s better conductivity. Liners, dielectric caps, dielectric stiffness, CMP damage, and grain structure alter the governing mechanism. A nominal metal name is consequently insufficient to transfer an activation energy between processes. Process development can change materials; a layout engine generally chooses among the stack and primitives the foundry actually supplies. Diffusion mechanisms; materials and boundaries.

2. Current density, geometry, and lifetime have different roles

j = I / Aconducting; for a uniform rectangular wire, Aconducting ≈ w t.

t50 = C j−n exp(Ea / kT).

The first expression converts branch current into average cross-sectional density. It is not a local crowding solution. Thickness, actual conductive width, barriers, etch loss, and manufacturing variation determine the physical section. A deck expressed in mA/µm already incorporates an effective thickness and its characterization assumptions; it must not be treated as A/cm² or divided by thickness twice.

The second expression is the generalized Black relation. The book presents a median failure time, not a guarantee that every wire survives. Its coefficient, activation energy, and current exponent must describe the process, geometry, failure criterion, and dominant failure mechanism. A fitted exponent near one for growth-limited failure and near two for nucleation-limited failure is an observation discussed by the book, not permission to select either value without calibration. Extrapolating accelerated testing across a mechanism change is particularly risky. Black’s relation and limitations, PDF 30–33.

A circuit contains many vulnerable locations. A median lifetime for one test structure is not the allowed failure probability for the entire product. The analysis needs the distribution of lifetimes, characterization confidence, target percentile, total exposure, and allocation of reliability among features. The book’s mission-profile treatment connects manufacturing, storage, and operating modes with time, temperature, and current. Reusing a route under a new operating profile invalidates the old reliability claim unless the profile remains inside the characterized envelope. Mission profiles and failure probability, PDF 91–98.

Proposed reliability contract

Store a capability per metal and via type with units, reference temperature, lifetime, failure criterion, applicable width range, duty-cycle model, geometry restrictions, and provenance. Store the application profile separately. An absent capability produces an explicit unverified reliability status. It must not silently inherit a global “safe current” constant.

3. Preserve DC, RMS, peak, direction, and waveform information

A single operating-point current is inadequate for a switched bias, output stage, clock buffer, or charging capacitor. Mean signed current describes directional transport only within an appropriate recovery model. RMS current controls resistive heating for a fixed resistance. Peak current constrains short-pulse and electrical-overstress behavior under separate pulse rules. All three may determine dimensions, and the largest applicable requirement governs. Current reversals can partially undo directional transport, but zero signed average does not imply zero heating or infinite life. Unequal positive and negative pulses, material boundaries, nonlinear diffusion, and thermal time constants can defeat simplistic cancellation. Alternating-current effects; current classification, PDF 82–86.

The book uses frequency thresholds to organize example flows. Those thresholds are not universal material constants. Whether a pulse behaves as DC stress, recoverable alternating stress, or a separate transient event depends on the process and waveform. Its minimum-feature digital-wire skin-effect discussion also does not eliminate RF loss analysis for wider top metal, inductors, or high-frequency analog structures. Frequency effects, PDF 140–142 and 154.

Illustrative calculation. A 10 mA unipolar pulse at 10% duty has 1 mA average, 3.16 mA RMS, and 10 mA peak. Sizing from 1 mA alone can overlook heating; sizing from 10 mA as continuous DC can waste area if a characterized pulse rule permits less. The required action is to evaluate every relevant rule against the same waveform and operating mode, retaining the reason for the selected width. These values teach the distinction; they are not a process recommendation.

4. The current belongs to a route branch, not to a net name

At a terminal, current has direction as well as magnitude. A multi-terminal net may contain sources and sinks that change roles during operation. A scalar maximum absolute current at each terminal loses phase and cancellation information and cannot, in general, determine the worst current in a Steiner branch. The book constructs signed lower and upper terminal-current bounds and derives branch demands from topology. For a tree, cutting an edge separates the loads into two sets; the branch current equals the signed sum on either side, with consistent orientation. This is a lumped-network KCL result: distributed wire capacitance and other shunt currents must either be negligible in the analysis or be included as loads on the corresponding side of the cut. For a resistive mesh, current sharing requires a network solution. Terminal currents and topology, PDF 83–90.

Iedge(t, mode) = Σterminals on one side of the cut Iterminal(t, mode).

Compute mean, RMS, and peak after this signed summation. Adding independent RMS values is a conservative bound in some circumstances, not the exact RMS of the sum; correlated and anti-correlated switching give different answers. The best available input is a set of physically consistent current-waveform scenarios satisfying Kirchhoff’s current law. If only intervals exist, preserve their conservative interpretation and identify the lost correlation.

Topology and wire dimensions are coupled. The shortest-length tree can leave a long branch carrying the sum of many loads, requiring a wide trunk and more capacitance. A current-aware objective can minimize Σ length × required width, subject to voltage drop, parasitic, and congestion limits. Reconnecting one load changes upstream currents; therefore branch annotations must be invalidated and recomputed after topology changes. §3.3.3, current-aware wire planning, PDF 89–90.

5. Width, pin, and via capacity must agree

The book’s sizing flow takes the maximum dimension required by equivalent-current, peak-current, and manufacturing rules, then accounts for process-dependent cross-section loss. The same reasoning must include bends, necks, contacts, and device-access metal. A wide global wire connected through a narrow pin throat does not have the capacity of its widest segment. Landing location on a complex pin changes the internal current path; pin accessibility is therefore an electrical property as well as a geometrical one. Dimensioning and pin accessibility, PDF 99–105.

Via redundancy improves manufacturing connectivity and can improve reliability, but dividing total current by the number of cuts assumes equal sharing. At an orthogonal layer transition, inner cuts can have shorter, lower-resistance access paths and carry more current than outer cuts. Simply adding distant cuts may barely relieve the hottest one. The book illustrates this with spatial current-density solutions and arrangements that equalize access path lengths. Adjacent metal geometry and allowed directional routing determine whether such an arrangement is useful in a real deck. Multiple vias, PDF 134–140.

A practical router should generate several legal landing templates, estimate or solve the current in each cut, and select a template whose maximum cut utilization passes. Vias in parallel also alter local reservoirs and mechanical stress. A generic “more vias always yields proportionate lifetime improvement” rule is unsupported. More cuts can still be useful for open-defect tolerance even when the EM benefit is smaller than the ideal arithmetic suggests.

Apparent temperature-factor inconsistency in the supplied book

In PDF page 101, printed equations 3.25–3.26 multiply the required via count by a factor that decreases when the application temperature rises above the reference temperature. The page was visually inspected; this is not merely text-extraction corruption. Under fixed-lifetime Black scaling with positive activation energy, that decreasing factor describes allowable current, whose inverse is needed when determining the number of equally loaded vias. Before implementing those printed equations, resolve the convention against the foundry model. The physically consistent fixed-lifetime relation derived from Black’s expression is jallow(T)/jallow(Tref) = exp[(Ea/nk)(1/T − 1/Tref)]. Higher temperature decreases allowable current. A required-width/count implementation needs a monotonicity test that catches the opposite result. The supplied equations.

6. Short wires, reservoirs, and material effects are conditional techniques

In a sufficiently confined conductor, EM creates a stress gradient that drives atoms back. Balancing the two driving forces gives a critical current-density–length product of the form |jL|crit = ΩΔσ/(e|Z*|ρ). The symbols represent atomic volume Ω, allowed stress difference Δσ, elementary charge e, dimensionless effective charge number Z*, and resistivity ρ. The length is a physical diffusion domain under the model’s boundary conditions. It is not the distance between arbitrary routing vertices. Inserting a software node does not create a diffusion barrier. Blech effect, PDF 117–127.

A short-segment exemption needs technology-qualified blocking boundaries, surrounding mechanical confinement, initial stress assumptions, and an appropriate failure criterion. A nucleation-free segment and a segment whose void growth eventually saturates are different claims; a saturated void can still violate resistance and analog performance budgets. Branched trees exchange stress and material through junctions, so independently checking every drawn edge against a straight-wire threshold can be wrong. The book itself notes that existence of a safe steady state does not prove the transient reaches it without damage.

Via-above and via-below configurations in dual-Damascene stacks can tolerate different void volumes before losing electrical connection. The direction and landing layer consequently matter. Reservoir metal can supply atoms at one boundary yet weaken helpful stress buildup at another; current reversal can exchange those roles. Bamboo grains can suppress one diffusion path while narrowing the wire raises resistance and heating, and copper interface diffusion may remain dominant. These are candidate techniques enabled by a qualified process model, not independent boolean optimizations. Bamboo limitations; via orientation and reservoirs, PDF 127–133.

The material chapters explain why dielectric stiffness, caps, liners, and copper interfaces affect back stress and activation energy. Carbon nanotubes and composites are research directions discussed in the 2018 source, with explicit contact, integration, and thermal limitations. Their theoretical current densities cannot be substituted into a conventional CMOS routing deck. The useful architectural lesson is to keep material and primitive capabilities extensible. Material stack and alternatives, PDF 142–154.

7. Numerical analysis and reusable routing patterns

Current density follows the electrical potential solution and conductivity. Local finite-element or resistance-mesh models expose crowding that average width rules miss. Electrothermal analysis updates resistivity and temperature together; stress and atom-transport analysis adds further state for reliability prediction. The mesh and geometric representation require convergence checks: a mathematical sharp corner can create a singular result, but indiscriminately smoothing it can erase a real manufactured neck. Use actual process geometry and a characterized local criterion. Numerical modeling, PDF 58–69; current-density verification.

A full multiphysics solve during every routing move is impractical. The book proposes precharacterized reliable routing elements, such as straight sections, bends, transitions, and via arrays. Such a library is especially applicable to Philis: annotate a legal template with current, temperature, direction, and boundary ranges; reject use outside those ranges; and verify the interactions when templates are joined. Individually valid blocks do not automatically compose if adjoining blocks change thermal conditions, stress reservoirs, current direction, or access geometry. Routing-element library, PDF 165–167.

8. Concrete integration with the inspected engine

The detailed router currently declares EM_UA_PER_UM = 1000 near line 38 and uses DetailedCfg::net_current_ua and em_width near lines 54 and 105. It takes an absolute DC net current, converts it using one current-per-width constant, and rounds the width to the manufacturing grid. That is a useful initial sizing heuristic. It does not encode per-layer thickness, branch current, waveform, temperature, lifetime, via sharing, or pin-neck capacity. Supply-net information is already wired from the frontend near line 138; the missing work is richer electrical information, not merely passing that existing list.

Implementation sequence, with reasons

  1. Define the reliability input schema. Layer/via capabilities and mission-profile provenance come first because unsupported numerical assumptions cannot be repaired by a better optimizer.
  2. Attach signed current scenarios to terminals. Terminal information survives topology changes; a net-wide scalar does not identify branch demand.
  3. Annotate the generated routing graph. Compute branch waveforms and voltage drops, then invalidate them whenever geometry or topology changes.
  4. Select layer, width, via template, and pin access jointly. Their capacities form a series path whose weakest element governs.
  5. Recompute electrical and thermal behavior. Wider metal changes parasitics and resistance; local heating changes resistance and reliability limits.
  6. Apply qualified local analysis to critical features. Use calibrated patterns for routine geometry and field analysis for cases outside their domain.
  7. Report margins and unresolved coverage. A route with unknown current or missing via capability is not an EM pass.

9. Validation that would justify the resulting rules

Useful regression cases are physical counterexamples: a tree with opposing terminal currents; a unipolar pulsed load with distinct average/RMS/peak demands; identical total via count with unequal access geometry; a wide wire feeding a narrow pin; a hot segment adjacent to a power device; and a branched diffusion domain that invalidates naive per-edge Blech checks. For every case, compare the fast model with an independent electrical or field solution and record its error over process corners.

Basic invariants catch severe implementation mistakes: conserve current at every junction; maintain units through µm-to-cm conversion; require no smaller width when current or temperature increases under unchanged rules; distinguish unknown current from zero current; avoid increasing allowable current when the required lifetime grows; and ensure rounding never reduces a required dimension. After a repair, repeat DRC, extraction, analog performance checks, and reliability evaluation because electrical and geometric objectives are coupled. A useful final artifact names the limiting segment or cut, its current metric, temperature, rule, margin, and model applicability.

All main chapters were read: PDF 13–23, 25–69, 73–108, 111–154, and 161–168, with front matter and the contents used to establish the map.
