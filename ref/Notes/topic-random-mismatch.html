Random mismatch and statistical matching

Understand area laws, covariance, operating-point sensitivity and characterization limits.

The design objective is a distribution of circuit error

Two nominally identical transistors do not have identical electrical behavior. An optimal analog layout controls the systematic differences that it can influence and supplies enough statistically useful device area for the remaining local variation, while preserving the required bias, speed, headroom, noise, and routing behavior. The object being optimized is the circuit’s offset, current ratio, gain error, settling error, or another specified quantity over process and operating conditions. “Equal rectangles,” “large area,” and “close together” are techniques whose value must be demonstrated against that quantity.

The foundations here are the supplied four-page Pelgrom–Duinmaijer ESSCIRC 1988 paper and Drennan–McAndrew’s 2003 paper. The former develops useful area and distance laws; the latter explains why electrical mismatch cannot generally be reduced to an area law independent of geometry and bias. Pelgrom, model; Drennan, physical variation.

1. Separate three kinds of variation before assigning a remedy

Global process variation moves many devices together: a die may have a systematically different oxide thickness, mobility, or threshold from the nominal model. Matching two nearby devices does not necessarily protect the circuit from this shared change. Process corners and global statistical models address it.

Local random mismatch makes nominally equivalent devices different because their microscopic populations and boundaries differ. Dopant statistics, local oxide fluctuations, edge variation, and other physical causes contribute. Averaging over more independent material can reduce this component. It remains after geometrical symmetry and proximity have removed much of the systematic difference.

Systematic spatial or contextual difference arises when devices sample different gradients, neighboring structures, well boundaries, stress, temperatures, routing voltage drops, or lithographic environments. The corresponding error can have a nonzero mean for a particular layout. Equalizing the environment, centroid, bias, and temperature is more appropriate than pretending that this error is another independent random threshold sample.

The categories describe a model and its scale, rather than an absolute property of nature. An unknown slowly varying wafer field can be represented statistically across dies but is approximately a deterministic gradient within one matched array. A spatial covariance model can connect these views. Record which effects the foundry’s statistical compact model already includes before adding a separate layout-dependent correction; otherwise the same oxide or geometry variation may be counted twice. Local and long-range components; Physical parameter dependencies and global/local distinction.

2. Why the area law works, and what its coefficient means

Suppose a transistor averages many small, independent fluctuations with finite variance. Increasing its gate area increases the number of samples. Variance of the average falls inversely with that number; standard deviation therefore falls approximately with inverse square root of area. This is the statistical reason behind an area law. It depends on independence at the sampled scale, a sufficiently uniform sensitivity across the device, and the absence of a dominant edge or long-range contribution.

σ²(ΔP) ≈ AP,pair²/(WL) + SP²D².

Here ΔP is the difference between a matched pair, W and L describe the characterized geometry, D is their separation under the fitted model, and A and S are fitted coefficients. The first term models local mismatch; the second models a distance-dependent contribution from long-range variation. This is the paper’s approximation for the studied processes, not a complete model for arbitrary modern devices. Its coefficients have units: a threshold coefficient might be mV·µm and a spatial coefficient mV/µm. Unit mistakes directly corrupt the allowed distance.

A critical convention is whether A describes a single device or the difference of two devices. For two independent devices each with variance σ², the variance of their difference is 2σ². Thus the pair coefficient is √2 times the single-device coefficient under those assumptions. A PDK coefficient must be interpreted from its definition; multiplying every published value by √2 is equally wrong. The engine should store the convention explicitly. Area and distance expression.

Geometry-specific physical variation explains departures. Drennan models length variance as proportional to 1/W, width variance as proportional to 1/L, and area-distributed physical variance as proportional to 1/(WL). Electrical parameters combine these terms with different sensitivities. Two equal-area rectangles with very different aspect ratios therefore need not have the same electrical mismatch. The original Pelgrom paper itself retains distinct width and length contributions before approximating current-factor mismatch with a simpler area expression. Current-factor contributions; Physical scaling laws.

3. Splitting a device does not manufacture extra statistical area

For n equal independent units, the relative standard deviation of their average decreases as 1/√n compared with one of those units. However, each unit is smaller and individually noisier than a single device with the combined area. Under the ideal area law the two effects cancel. Comparing four small units with one small unit demonstrates the benefit of four times the area, not a free benefit from partitioning.

Partitioning can be extremely valuable for common-centroid organization, interdigitation, aspect ratio, gate resistance, access symmetry, and manufacturability. It also adds perimeter, contacts, routing, and possible narrow-width or stress effects. Shared diffusion may change parasitic capacitance and source resistance; the compact-model semantics of fingers and multiplicity may differ. The engine must preserve both the logical device mapping and the physical unit construction so that electrical equivalence is checked instead of assumed. Drennan’s multi-unit examples explicitly distinguish the statistical averaging argument from bias and circuit-role effects. Multiple units and mirror examples.

4. Correlation determines which errors average away

Var(P1 − P2) = Var(P1) + Var(P2) − 2Cov(P1,P2).

For equal variances: Var(ΔP) = 2σ²(1 − ρ).

A perfectly shared fluctuation cancels in an ideal difference but still shifts common-mode behavior. Independent fluctuations do not cancel; positively correlated fluctuations partly cancel; anticorrelation can worsen the difference. In a multi-unit device, variance of a weighted sum is wTCw. Averaging n units by dividing the single-unit variance by n is justified only if the covariance and weights support it.

The drawing shows why common-centroid organization and larger area solve different problems. If unit error contains a linear field g·r, equal electrical weights and equal group centroids cancel the first spatial moment. Curvature, field discontinuities, unequal temperatures, unequal unit currents, and orientation effects require additional constraints or a better model. Merely making the geometric centroids equal is insufficient when units carry different electrical weights.

A useful implementation separates a short-range covariance model, a long-range field model, and deterministic layout-context corrections. A fitted distance coefficient can be a conservative approximation when these details are unavailable. Its provenance and spatial range must travel with it. The engine must never label an uncalibrated fixed ratio of gradient coefficient to area coefficient as a physical constant.

5. Convert device variation into the electrical quantity that matters

Let the circuit error be e and the underlying physical parameters form vector p. For small perturbations, Δe ≈ JΔp, where J contains the derivatives of the circuit output with respect to those parameters at the actual operating condition. The corresponding variance is approximately J C JT. This formulation reveals two independent ways to improve mismatch: reduce physical variation and reduce circuit sensitivity to it.

Drennan computes sensitivities using a compact transistor model and fits physical parameter variances from measured electrical mismatch. This avoids treating derived threshold and current-factor parameters as independent when both share an oxide-thickness cause. The same method also explains why weak inversion, strong inversion, linear operation, body bias, drain bias, series resistance, and short/narrow-channel behavior change the result. A model calibrated only on square devices or one bias point can mis-rank the geometries most useful in analog circuits. Parameter choice; Propagation and back propagation of variance.

For an input-referred current perturbation under a local linear approximation, ΔVin ≈ ΔI/gm. This is not generally identical to threshold mismatch. The relevant gm depends on current and geometry; the current mismatch includes more than threshold. The output of a differential pair further depends on load mismatch, common-mode conditions, source degeneration, finite output resistance, and subsequent stages. A placement rule should therefore carry its circuit-role sensitivity rather than assign the same offset interpretation to every matched MOS pair.

6. A worked geometry tradeoff: increasing width may give little benefit

Consider an explicitly simplified long-channel saturation model, I = ½μCox(W/L)Vov², with current held fixed and threshold mismatch as the only random mechanism. Small threshold error gives relative current error approximately −2ΔVT/Vov. If σ²(ΔVT) = A²/(WL), the threshold contribution to relative current variance is 4A²/(WL Vov²). At fixed current, Vov² = 2IL/(μCoxW). Substitution makes that contribution proportional to 1/L² and independent of W.

The cancellation is instructive: widening averages more material but reduces overdrive, increasing threshold-to-current sensitivity by the compensating amount in this toy model. Lengthening can improve the contribution while increasing overdrive and consuming voltage headroom. This derivation does not claim that width never helps. Mobility variation, edge effects, short-channel behavior, finite output resistance, series resistance, bias changes, and the actual statistical model can change the result. It explains why a blind area-only objective is insufficient and why the paper’s measured/model results depend on bias and aspect ratio. Current-mirror geometry reasoning.

The engine’s action is to enumerate approved geometry variants, recompute the operating point and sensitivity for each, and reject candidates that violate headroom, speed, power, or model validity. If the placement contract fixes W/L, it should optimize the allowed physical realization and report a mismatch infeasibility rather than silently resize the circuit.

7. A worked distance and area budget

Illustrative assumptions, not foundry values: use a pair threshold coefficient A = 4 mV·µm and spatial coefficient S = 0.001 mV/µm. With gate area 100 µm², local standard deviation is 0.4 mV. With separation 100 µm, the distance contribution is 0.1 mV. If these fitted components are combined as independent variance terms, total standard deviation is √(0.4² + 0.1²) = 0.412 mV.

Suppose the design allocates a gradient standard-deviation contribution no larger than 25% of the local component. The condition SD ≤ 0.25 A/√area gives D ≤ 100 µm for this example. Quadrupling area reduces the local component to 0.2 mV. The same 100 µm separation then gives total 0.224 mV and a gradient/local ratio of 50%; meeting the original 25% allocation requires separation at most 50 µm. Larger devices make systematic matching relatively more important, even though absolute total mismatch improved.

This relationship is useful for a distance budget, but the allocation is a design choice and the coefficients require characterization. A deterministic known gradient should normally be propagated as a mean error, not automatically combined in quadrature with random variation. If the gradient’s direction is known, the projected separation matters; a scalar radial distance may be a deliberate worst-case approximation.

8. Static mismatch is not temporal noise

Mismatch describes device-to-device differences across a population of fabricated circuits or sampled physical states. Thermal noise and flicker noise describe fluctuations of a given circuit over time and require spectral or time-domain analysis. Increasing area may help both some mismatch contributions and some low-frequency noise contributions, but their coefficients, correlations, bandwidths, and circuit transfer functions differ. A mismatch standard deviation in millivolts cannot be added directly to a voltage-noise density in V/√Hz.

For a circuit specification, integrate temporal noise through the actual transfer function and bandwidth, model static offset and drift separately, and combine quantities only under a stated statistical and operational definition. Chopping, autozeroing, calibration, or trimming can change which error matters, but these are architectural features that must already exist in the circuit contract. A placement engine cannot assume that an unmodeled calibration will remove its layout-induced error. Substrate-coupled switching can also be deterministic interference or cyclostationary disturbance rather than independent thermal noise. The substrate source illustrates its phase-dependent effects. §5.3, phase-dependent switching interference, PDF 75–76 and 81.

10. An implementable mismatch evaluation loop

  1. Identify the circuit metric and device group. Record intended ratios, biases, allowed transformations, and the logical-to-physical unit mapping. This establishes what equality means.
  2. Obtain a valid statistical model. Prefer the foundry compact-model statistics; otherwise use a characterized local approximation with explicit units, covariance convention, geometry, bias, and temperature range.
  3. Evaluate nominal behavior and sensitivities. Do this across relevant operating modes because a mirror in saturation and the same mirror near compliance can have different sensitivities.
  4. Evaluate local covariance and spatial/context corrections. Keep their provenance separate and prevent double counting.
  5. Generate placement and routing candidates. Compare approved unit arrays, weighted centroids, thermal positions, and access symmetry using electrical error budgets.
  6. Extract and re-evaluate. Source resistance, body connections, drain voltage, and capacitance change after routing. Preserve the actual unit mapping in the extracted representation.
  7. Validate promising candidates statistically. Compare linear sensitivity estimates with Monte Carlo or another trusted model when nonlinear tails or tight yield targets matter.
  8. Report error contribution and confidence. Explain which component limits the result, which physical action reduced it, and which missing characterization prevents a stronger claim.

11. Applying this to Philis

MatchingPair currently evaluates a distance/area relationship using GRADIENT_PER_AVT_UM2 and gradient_over_random near lines 16–19 and 79. The algebra expresses a useful normalized gradient allocation if its coefficients are valid. The hard-coded ratio is not a universal physical property. Replace that assumption with a process- and device-specific model reference and separate the fast placement surrogate from the final electrical metric. The Matching distinction between mirror and cross/differential roles can support different sensitivities, but a label alone does not supply their bias dependence.

A practical first improvement is to retain the existing cheap distance score while making its calibration and applicability explicit, then add electrical re-evaluation for shortlisted candidates. This preserves optimization speed and prevents a convenient surrogate from being presented as a complete yield prediction. Unknown model input should be visible as an unresolved budget, not disguised as a precise score.

Acceptance evidence and chapter scope

Validate with equal-area square and extreme-aspect-ratio devices; fixed-current width and length sweeps; single versus equal-total-area split devices; correlated versus independent unit errors; known linear gradients; unequal source access resistance; and thermal asymmetry. The expected answers should come from independent model calculations or measured data, not from reproducing the implementation’s own formula. For statistical targets report sample uncertainty, mean error, spread, and model range. This chapter synthesizes the complete supplied Pelgrom and Drennan papers, supplemented by the substrate source’s circuit-sensitivity reasoning. Its illustrative values and proposed Philis algorithm are explicitly distinct from characterized process rules.
