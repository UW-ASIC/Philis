Source companion

The Art of Analog Layout

Alan Hastings · third edition · 2023 catalog / ©2024

Physical mechanisms, device construction, matching, protection and die assembly.

Open supplied PDF · 1,568 physical pages · File identity and coverage

Hastings treats an analog layout as a physical implementation of a circuit in a particular fabrication process and package. Its quality depends on electrical accuracy, parasitics, temperature, mechanical stress, noise coupling, manufacturing variation, and survival over its intended life. For Philis, the most useful lesson is to turn layout advice into conditional, explainable rules tied to mechanisms and measurable budgets. A compact, symmetric drawing is only one contributor to that result.

Applicability boundary. The book explicitly retains older processes to teach physical principles. Its illustrative dimensions, voltage limits, lifetime assumptions, matching grades, doping profiles, packaging conventions, and material comparisons do not become foundry rules merely by appearing in a textbook. These notes identify what to characterize or verify before a rule can support a production claim. This source is particularly valuable for planar analog CMOS, bipolar, BiCMOS, and power processes; it is not a complete treatment of FinFET/GAA layout, modern multi-patterning, or every foundry's reliability qualification.

What “optimal” should mean to the engine

Source finding. The text repeatedly trades area against precision, compactness against local interactions, shielding against other physical effects, and device size against parasitic load. Its chapter 8 discussion explicitly explains that adding more sections to a fixed-area array can reduce gradient error while increasing random mismatch because spacing consumes active area. It also states that a CC arrangement offers no matching benefit if the relevant gradients are absent. PDF pp. 392–394

Philis interpretation. Define the optimization problem from the circuit's allowed error, operating scenarios, process, package, manufacturing variation, and lifetime. First require electrical connectivity, legal devices, manufacturable geometry, and relevant reliability limits. Then compare feasible candidates on extracted circuit performance, mismatch/yield, area, power loss, and routing resources. Record unresolved checks as unknown rather than silently assuming they passed. “Optimal” should identify the objective, evaluated scenarios, model fidelity, and alternatives considered.

For example, minimizing transistor separation helps with a smooth temperature gradient, but placing a hot transistor beside a precision pair can overwhelm that benefit. A grounded shield can reduce capacitive interference while adding load and changing hydrogenation or mechanical surroundings. A small mirror may route beautifully yet fail the current-ratio target. Each objective therefore needs an error mechanism and an operating context; a weighted sum of geometric preferences cannot by itself certify analog performance.

Use two levels of explanation for each accepted or rejected move: the local reason—for example, this via arrangement lowers the maximum current density—and the circuit consequence—for example, it preserves the specified current under the hot operating scenario without increasing resistance beyond its voltage-drop budget. Preserve both because a rule can be physically valid yet irrelevant to the circuit being optimized.

Fabrication knowledge must travel with the geometry

Chapters 1–4 explain why shapes on different layers are not interchangeable obstacles. Diffusion extends beyond the drawn opening; depletion extends beyond the metallurgical junction; implanted dopants have tails; oxide steps alter topography; local pattern density alters printing, etching, or polishing; contacts and vias depend on the actual metal process. Minimum dimensions include manufacturing tolerances and electrical requirements. Consequently, a device footprint alone cannot express all interactions. §§2.3–2.6, PDF pp. 79–95, §2.7, PDF pp. 99–110, §3.2.3, PDF pp. 138–144

Matching requires the right quantity, the right centroid, and the right environment

Source finding. Random variation, systematic dimensional bias, neighboring-pattern effects, contact/interconnect resistance, temperature, stress and electric fields are distinct mechanisms. Increasing active area averages some random fluctuations; it does not automatically remove systematic error. The simpler inverse-square-root-of-area relation is an approximation when areal variation dominates peripheral variation. The book also distinguishes absolute accuracy from ratio matching and from resolution. PDF pp. 368–375, 418

For equal, comparable MOS devices in the regime where the fitted model applies, Hastings uses the familiar relation σ(ΔVTH) = AVT/√(WL). The coefficient is obtained from measurements. The text explicitly cautions against overtrusting its oxide-thickness rule of thumb, discusses backgate-bias dependence, and gives exceptions involving small geometries and pocket implants. That is evidence for storing a mismatch model per device family and operating regime, not treating one dimensionless ratio of gradient to random error as universal. PDF pp. 687–690

Centroids are electrical, not simply centers of occupied rectangles

For a conventional MOS device, the active area is the intersection of gate and active silicon. Its centroid can differ from the cell bounding-box center because contacts, guard rings, dummies and routing occupy additional space. For arbitrary resistor networks, equal-sized drawn segments need not have equal effects on the resulting resistance. Hastings gives sensitivity-weighted coordinates:

XR = Σ[(∂R/∂Ri) Xi] / Σ(∂R/∂Ri), with the analogous expression for YR.

This transcription was checked visually against equations 8.24–8.25. A series element and an element in a parallel branch can contribute different weights even when their shapes are identical. PDF p. 390, 704–705

Generalization for Philis. Weight geometry by the sensitivity to the physical field actually being modeled. If a field creates equal absolute changes in segment resistance, the source's ∂R/∂Ri weights apply. If it instead produces equal fractional changes, the weights also contain Ri; for other parameters use the corresponding local derivative. Normalize the weights, define the covered operating point, and preserve the same normalization on both matched sides. This avoids a false “zero centroid error” obtained by averaging quantities with different electrical influence.

Common centroid is a first-order tool

Coincident centroids cancel a linear spatial contribution under the assumptions behind that averaging. They do not cancel all higher-order variation, local discontinuities, arbitrary coupling, or different terminal voltages. Hastings' four passive-device rules are coincidence, symmetry, subdivision into smaller common-centroid groups, and compactness; MOS devices add orientation. The revised third-edition notion of dispersion specifically favors repeatable small balanced subarrays over merely spreading labels uniformly. PDF pp. 392–394, 709–711

Evaluate a candidate using normalized active first moments, second moments and local environment descriptors, followed by extracted electrical checks. First moments test a linear field; differences in x², xy and y² moments reveal possible quadratic sensitivity. These additional moments are our proposed diagnostic, not a claim that Hastings supplies a complete polynomial optimizer. Verify them against synthetic fields before using them to rank layouts. Compactness is a useful heuristic when the field is unknown; it is not a proof that one arrangement dominates every possible gradient.

Counterexample to compulsory segmentation: if device area is fixed, more contacts, dummies and spacing reduce usable active area and increase resistance/capacitance. If random mismatch dominates and local gradients are weak, a simpler arrangement may perform better. Generate a small Pareto set of segment counts and aspect ratios, then compare their estimated and extracted error contributions.

Resistors: geometry, connectivity and electric fields are inseparable

Chapter 6 shows why a resistor is not just sheet resistance multiplied by drawn length/width. It has dimension biases, contact crowding, end effects, corner effects, nonlinear voltage and temperature dependence, distributed capacitance, and sometimes parasitic junctions. For a right-angle equal-width corner, the book gives 0.5587 squares rather than the approximate half-square mnemonic. This small difference matters if the algorithm uses many corners to obtain a precision ratio. Dogbone heads do not inherently make two devices match better: identical systematic head contributions can cancel, while unmatched heads cannot. PDF pp. 279–295

Thermoelectrics are a connectivity test, not a centroid test

Equation 8.34 is ET = S ΔTC, where S is the contact Seebeck coefficient and ΔTC is the temperature difference between a segment's contacts. Figure 8.22, visually checked here, contrasts connections that add these voltages with connections that largely cancel them. The engine should trace the ordered path through each series chain and accumulate signed thermoelectric sources. This is separate from matching the average resistance under a temperature field. Close placement of serpentine heads reduces endpoint temperature difference, but their orientation must also address mask-alignment sensitivity. PDF pp. 401–402

Material and trim decisions. Thin-film, poly, diffusion, well, metal and pinched resistors occupy different places in the accuracy/area/power/parasitic trade space. Diffused resistors can dissipate heat more effectively through silicon, while deposited resistors avoid the same junction parasitics. Trimming can compensate a value at its calibration condition, but cannot generally remove thermal gradients, material-dependent temperature behavior, hysteresis or later drift. Preserve trim accessibility, programming-current paths and exclusions when trim structures are present; component substitution or adding trim is a circuit-design choice, not a permissible placement repair. PDF pp. 295–323, 418–422

Capacitors: match the whole charge-transfer network

The book distinguishes junction, MOS, poly-poly, metal-metal, stacked, lateral-flux and trench capacitors. Their voltage dependence, leakage, plate resistance, substrate parasitics, dielectric absorption, breakdown and density differ. Two terminals on a schematic do not imply interchangeable plates in layout. Even when the intended capacitance matches perfectly, bottom-plate or lead coupling can change charge redistribution. MOS: distinguish voltage matching, current matching, orientation and LDE

Hastings begins MOS matching by distinguishing equal-current voltage matching from equal-gate-voltage current matching. Threshold and current-factor variation enter these cases differently. In the long-channel, strong-inversion, saturation approximation at fixed drain current, increasing width both averages threshold variation and raises transconductance; these effects can cancel in the relative current-error expression. Increasing length can still help. This is the basis of the source's current-matching versus voltage-matching sizing guidance—not a universal theorem for arbitrary compact models, inversion levels or pocket-implanted devices. PDF pp. 686–690, 712–714

Propagate mismatch through the actual operating-point sensitivities. A useful first-order decomposition is δI ≈ (∂I/∂VTH)δVTH + (∂I/∂β)δβ + (∂I/∂VDS)δVDS + (∂I/∂VBS)δVBS, including covariance where characterized. This is an implementation proposal, not a replacement compact model. Use derivatives from supported device models and simulation; distinguish fixed-current, fixed-voltage and ratioed operation.

Orientation is a terminal-direction property

Equation 13.61 defines Φ = (1/N)ΣΦi for identical units, with Φi = +1 or −1 according to drain-current direction. Equal signed orientation is required; equal absolute magnitude is insufficient. Three right-oriented and one left-oriented unit give +1/2, as do nine right-oriented and three left-oriented units. Zero is convenient but is not compulsory. In two dimensions, track both directional components. The formula and tilted-implant diagram were visually checked. PDF pp. 708–711

During pattern construction, retain source/drain labels and legal sharing boundaries. During hierarchy transforms, rotate or reflect the orientation descriptor consistently. Add a test in which two macros have identical outlines and coincident centroids but opposite normalized source/drain directions: the engine should detect the mismatch. For asymmetric drain-extended devices, use explicitly qualified transformations; apparent geometric mirroring can change a non-self-aligned channel length under mask misalignment. PDF p. 691

Bipolar transistors and diodes need their own matching and interaction rules

Vertical bipolar devices. Match emitter geometry and use identical unit emitters for ratios. Active emitter area and perimeter determine injection, while base resistance, base/emitter contacts, collector resistance, Early effect and saturation alter the terminal currents. Compact clusters and common-centroid arrays help with temperature and stress fields, but contact and base distributions also need balancing. Placing collector contacts outside an array can bring its critical emitters closer together. When emitters share a base, do not blindly exploit the smaller “connected-emitter” spacing: overlapping depletion regions can alter carrier flow even when the terminals share a net. PDF pp. 506–526

Lateral bipolar devices. Both emitter and collector geometry matter because current flows laterally between them. A larger single emitter can reduce current gain, so the source often favors arrays of small emitters rather than simply enlarging one device. Match the collector's inner perimeter, base/contact environment and applicable field plates. A saturated collector can reinject carriers into neighboring collectors; equal nominal base nets do not make shared tanks harmless. Separate isolation regions can be worth their area where collector efficiency or saturation threatens matching. PDF pp. 527–529

Operating-point limits. Keep precision bipolar devices in their characterized current-density range, below problematic high-level injection and away from low-current leakage/recombination dominance. Equalize collector voltages when the circuit requires suppression of Early-effect error. Emitter degeneration transfers sensitivity from a transistor to a resistor and costs voltage headroom; it is an architectural option, not something the placer can add. Guard against reverse emitter-base stress, which can degrade current gain before an obvious catastrophic failure. A ratioed bandgap pair also relies on accurate temperature tracking; a common centroid does not remove all nonlinear thermal behavior. PDF pp. 509–523, 526

PN diodes. Diode-connected transistors inherit their parent family’s matching limitations. Control the actual regulated current: emitter-current regulation includes base current, so it is not identical to collector-current regulation. Match junction shape, contact arrangement, guard structures, material and surrounding diffusions. Distinguish series resistance from junction-voltage error and include leakage over temperature. PDF pp. 565–571

Zeners. Localized avalanche can let one region with the lowest breakdown voltage dominate the entire junction. Consequently, increasing area does not generally deliver inverse-square-root matching at low current. Surface walkout/walkback and unequal accumulated charge can produce later divergence. Use a characterized structure and lifecycle model; neither a circular outline nor CC placement can promise precise Zener matching. PDF pp. 566–567

Schottky diodes. Barrier material, inhomogeneity, contact perimeter, field relief and series resistance matter. A field-relief guard junction can improve field uniformity but can also inject minority carriers when it forward biases (§11.2, PDF p. 563). The source's comparison of aluminum and noble-silicide Schottkies demonstrates why material-specific primitives are necessary; it is not a license to synthesize an unqualified barrier. PDF pp. 568–571

For Philis, a bipolar/diode matching rule should name the matched output—VBE, current ratio, forward drop or reverse breakdown—and the controlled terminal conditions. Reject the tempting abstraction “all matched devices get the MOS area formula.” Validate geometry through extraction and the circuit's operating scenarios, including potential saturation and transient injection.

Inductors, power devices, JFETs and high-voltage structures

Integrated inductors. More turns do not monotonically improve performance. Winding resistance, skin/proximity effects, substrate eddy currents and parasitic capacitance compete with inductance and mutual coupling. High frequency makes conductor shape, neighboring metal and return paths part of the device. The source favors high-level, low-resistance metal, short leads, a controlled empty center and limited unrelated material nearby. A solid shield can create eddy-current loss; a patterned shield must interrupt the relevant current loops. Stacking metal in parallel is different from adding serial winding layers, which can greatly increase interwinding capacitance. PDF pp. 355–363

Treat RF passives as characterized electromagnetic cells with frequency-dependent port models, transform restrictions and exclusion regions. Evaluate Q, self-resonant behavior, loss and coupling across the specified band. Preserve equal terminal environments for differential inductors. Automatic fill and routing must honor these regions or trigger fresh EM extraction. Minimized DC resistance and a correct nominal inductance are insufficient acceptance criteria.

Power bipolar devices. Emitter debiasing, thermal runaway and turnoff current focusing distinguish continuous, switching and pulsed operation. Base contact placement and ballast must be designed for those conditions. A structure good for a linear pass element can have poor reverse-bias safe operating area during turnoff. Repeating a small-signal transistor until its total area is large enough does not establish power capability. PDF pp. 485–505

Power MOS devices. Include silicon, metal and package contributions to on-resistance. Limit distributed gate delay, keep source/body resistance under control, and check electrical as well as electrothermal safe operating area. A positive temperature coefficient in some operating regions does not imply universal immunity to current localization: the text discusses impact-ionization feedback and the Spirito effect. SenseFET units must sample comparable temperature, terminal voltage and edge environments; a small sense element at the edge can see first-finger effects absent from the average main transistor. PDF pp. 644–668

The rule of one-third: useful approximation, with a source check

For a uniformly distributed, singly terminated metal finger, the source derives an effective added resistance approximately one third of its disconnected end-to-end resistance. That differs from a lead delivering all its current to a load at the far end: current is collected or delivered continuously along the finger. Unequal material coverage, tapering or strong debiasing requires a fuller distributed model. PDF pp. 650–664, 870–876

Using the visually checked definitions on pages 871–872, let r = Rsheet/Wmetal be resistance per length, g = 1/(L RSi) be silicon conductance per length, and u = L√(rg). The exact one-finger model gives Rtotal = RSi u coth(u), so Rmetal ≈ rL/3 for small u. This notation renames the source's finger length W to L to avoid confusion with metal width.

Apparent source inconsistency. Equation E.14 as displayed omits the square root implied by E.1, E.2 and E.7 when expressing γW. In the same derivation, the displayed E.6 has a plus sign for the second exponential term although E.3 and E.5 require a minus, and E.8 writes the current prefactor γ/G although differentiation and units require G/γ. Equation E.9 gives the consistent total-resistance expression used above. The audit therefore checks the differential equations and boundary conditions, not an uncorrected transcription of every displayed intermediate equation. Its quoted approximation accuracy also needs care: accuracy of total resistance is not automatically the same as relative accuracy of the much smaller added metal resistance. Implement the exact dimensionless model or validate the approximation against it; do not copy an isolated penetration-distance threshold without reconciling the equations. This is our algebraic check of the supplied edition, not a publisher-confirmed erratum.

High-voltage, annular and JFET structures. Drift regions, RESURF, field plates, oxide options, body ties and isolation define their operation. Annular transistors are not ordinary rectangles with an interchangeable source and drain. JFET pinch-off and parasitic junctions depend on a process-specific channel construction. Embedded NVM likewise needs programming/erase voltage, endurance and antenna/protection considerations beyond its static footprint. Restrict automatic transformations and mergers to the characterized variants; offer unsupported structures as explicit capability gaps. PDF pp. 616–639, 669–685

Antenna is fabrication-history dependent. A long conductor may not yet connect to a gate when its layer is etched. Conversely, a small final connected component can have a problematic exposure history. The book distinguishes areal and peripheral ratios and discusses latent connections during incomplete etching. A higher-layer jumper changes the earlier connected component; it is not merely a change in final total wire area. A top-layer violation cannot be repaired by jumping to a nonexistent higher layer. A diode brings junction capacitance, leakage, placement and sometimes illumination requirements, all of which must be checked. PDF pp. 228–230

ESD protection must remain benign during operation. A snapback clamp has trigger and holding behavior, while a rate-triggered device responds to slew. A normal power-up ramp, a failsafe input above an unpowered supply, or an external source able to sustain holding current can therefore alter device choice. Extra rail width alone cannot repair a clamp that is inappropriate for the application. Primary pad protection also does not ensure a nearby thin gate survives fast local discharge; return points and local secondary clamps matter. The book's illustrated HBM/CDM values are examples from specific structures and eras, not current universal qualification requirements. PDF pp. 749–752, 766–776

Guard rings and device sharing require a cross-sectional model

A substrate tap usually reduces majority-carrier voltage drop. A minority-carrier collecting ring intercepts carriers at a suitable reverse-biased junction. A blocking ring uses an effective high-low junction or dielectric barrier to obstruct their path. These are different mechanisms even when their top views look like similar rings. The relevant carrier type, depth, buried layer, bias and contact resistance determine whether carriers pass beneath a ring, over a weak junction, or through an opening. PDF pp. 256–270, 735–742

Engine actions and reasons: identify potential injectors over transient as well as DC scenarios; choose a qualified guard architecture for the actual substrate; ensure its required three-dimensional continuity; connect it to an appropriate low-impedance node; and verify the return path and local debiasing. A nominal enclosure without a sound electrical connection can fail. More width eventually gives diminishing benefit, and a ring that works on a heavily doped substrate may be marginal on a lightly doped one. A blocking ring typically needs complete enclosure; the text also discusses qualified collecting-ring arrangements near die edges that are not closed rectangles. Do not collapse these distinctions into “all rings must look alike.”

Sharing is an electrical transformation. Merging devices in a common well/tank saves repeated isolation area but introduces shared resistance, junction capacitance and minority-carrier paths. Net equivalence is necessary for many mergers but not sufficient for safe operation. Saturating lateral PNPs, noisy isolated MOS devices, shared contacts carrying large current, and resistors whose bodies become forward biased illustrate the failure modes. Whitelist qualified merger templates with terminal/bias and injection conditions, and otherwise retain separate regions. A merge should be re-extracted and rechecked under scenarios, not approved merely because DRC and nominal LVS pass. PDF pp. 723–734, 600

Routing and floorplanning finish the analog circuit

Kelvin and star connections. The physical point at which sense and force conductors join determines which voltage drop the circuit measures. If they merge before the intended device or pad, shared current creates an unwanted offset. Separate net names and schematic resistors can assist LVS but do not prove the junction is in the right physical location. Likewise, matched emitters or sources should join an intended star at the actual location where common-mode rather than differential IR drop is desired. Include finite sense current and lead resistance for high accuracy. PDF p. 818

Noise. Coupling depends on aggressor slew, coupling capacitance, victim impedance and timing, not merely the aggressor's average frequency. A configuration bit that switches infrequently can still upset a sensitive measurement. Use circuit roles to separate incompatible nets, controlled crossing geometry, and shields tied to the relevant quiet reference. Check the shield's impedance; a shared noisy return can defeat its purpose. Slowing a signal is a possible circuit-level remedy only when its timing permits it. PDF pp. 819–820

Voltage and current. High-voltage spacing depends on the maximum voltage difference between adjacent conductors under allowed scenarios, not just their voltage to ground. EM is a local bottleneck condition; resistance is an integrated path quantity. The source's via illustration shows why vias near the inside of a corner carry more current than nominally identical outer vias. Prefer appropriate straight transitions and sufficient distributed access, then check the actual current distribution. PDF pp. 821–822

Capacity and access. Route pitch must account for via footprint, enclosure and output-grid legality, not only minimum line spacing. Reserve space for wide supplies, shields, matched routes and local access before filling channels with ordinary signals. At intersecting channels, available tracks can disappear into turning and via demand; a channel-width scalar alone is an imperfect congestion model. Use blockage/access-aware estimation early enough to revise the floorplan. PDF pp. 811–817

Thermal and stress placement. Conventional molded-package stress often has low gradients near the die center despite large absolute stress there. Heat-source avoidance can favor an off-center location. Bumps, pillars, die attach, board loading and multiple changing power sources create different distributions. Optimize the mismatch-relevant field difference over the active region, not simply distance from a heater or absolute stress. Preserve useful symmetry under unequal power scenarios rather than assuming equal power forever. PDF pp. 399–411, 525, 715

Whole-die feasibility. Core area and pad-ring requirements can limit die size independently. Pads, ESD devices, guard rings, seal rings, scribe interfaces, bumps, bond trajectories and assembly exclusions all consume resources. Put these structures and the actual pin order into the early floorplan. A smaller core may produce no die-area saving on a pad-limited chip; it may instead free area for lower resistance, extra taps, shielding or matching improvements. PDF pp. 782–810

Finalization. The book ends with a checklist covering DRC/LVS, high-current and resistance checks, antenna, ESD, latchup, dummy exclusions, noisy/sensitive routing, pad requirements, Kelvin locations, corner/slot/seal requirements and archiving. Philis can translate this into a reproducible evidence bundle: final layout and libraries, exact process/rule/model versions, extraction, evaluated scenarios, pass/fail/unknown results and constraint provenance. This is stronger than retaining only a final objective score. PDF pp. 823–825

A practical validation program with discriminating examples

The following experiments are proposed for Philis. Their purpose is to distinguish superficially similar layouts whose physical consequences differ, rather than testing that an implementation reproduces its own formula.

  1. Active centroid versus cell center: add a guard ring or asymmetric pin extension to one matched unit without changing its active channel. Active centroid should remain unchanged while bounding-box centroid changes. Then move the active channel within a fixed outline and require the reverse result.
  2. First versus higher moments: compare centroid-equal arrays under linear and quadratic synthetic fields. Confirm first-order cancellation and quantify residual quadratic error; include a compact repeated-subarray alternative and its extra route cost.
  3. Terminal orientation: keep A/B placement identical while reversing the source/drain pattern of one side. Require detection even when all bounding boxes and centroids remain equal.
  4. Resistor connection polarity: apply a temperature gradient along identical strips and compare series paths that add versus cancel Seebeck sources. The CC score alone should not decide the outcome.
  5. LDE environment: give equal-W/L edge and interior MOS fingers different STI/well distances, then add appropriate dummies. Compare extracted model parameters or characterized device currents, not simply geometric regularity.
  6. Capacitor routes: produce equal-length leads with different layers, crossings or fill surroundings. Extract their capacitance matrices and show why equal length can fail the intended ratio.
  7. Shared-region transient: compare isolated and merged device variants under a scenario that forward biases a normally reverse-biased junction. Nominal connectivity equivalence should not suppress injection/debiasing evaluation.
  8. Distributed power metal: compare a one-end finger, two-ended access, a tapered bus and a far-end lumped load. Check the one-third approximation only where its assumptions hold.
  9. Antenna history: compare routes with similar final total metal area but different intermediate-layer connections. Verify that a valid jumper changes the appropriate process-stage ratio.
  10. ESD and Kelvin path location: keep nominal nets identical while moving a clamp return or force/sense junction. Check protected-terminal voltage or sensed IR drop; LVS equality alone should not pass the test.
  11. Post-fill validation: apply fill near a matched MOS group, a capacitor array and an inductor. Recheck their different environment requirements instead of assuming one keepout distance suits all three.
  12. Package/scenario robustness: compare one-heater, unequal two-heater and bumped-package cases. Report performance across scenarios, including the models and uncertainties that remain uncharacterized.

Before assigning numerical guarantees, collect foundry device/mismatch/LDE and aging models; terminal-current and voltage scenarios; interconnect/contact/via properties and extraction technology; antenna and voltage-dependent rule definitions; qualified guard and ESD structures; thermal and package geometry/materials; and assembly/fill requirements. Where these are absent, the correct deliverable is a justified candidate and an explicit unverified budget, not a fictional universal threshold.
