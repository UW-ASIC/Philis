Bipolar devices, diodes and precision structures

Respect device-family geometry, current density, thermal response and special-device limits.

Bipolar and diode layout starts with carrier injection, terminal conditions and temperature. Matching emitter area is useful only when access resistance, bias, isolation and operating history support the same electrical objective.

Choose the electrical quantity before choosing the pattern

A bipolar transistor is an injection device. Collector current depends exponentially on base-emitter voltage in its useful forward-active regime, while base resistance, collector voltage, recombination, leakage and high-level injection modify the relationship. A diode-connected bipolar device adds the question of whether the external circuit controls collector current or emitter current. The optimum layout therefore depends on whether the specification is an absolute forward drop, a difference in base-emitter voltages, a current ratio, a temperature coefficient, power handling or protection behavior.

This distinction is operational. A current mirror can need equal collector voltages and low base-access resistance; a bandgap pair deliberately uses different current densities while requiring very close temperature tracking; a power output transistor needs distributed current collection and safe operating area. They cannot share a single “matched BJT” penalty based only on geometric area. PDF pp. 429–483, 506–529

First input contract. Record device family and qualified variant, temperature and current-density range, which current is forced, which terminal voltage is sensed, permitted saturation and reverse-bias events, and what quantity must match. Add the circuit's allowable error over those conditions. The layout optimizer may choose unit arrangement and access geometry inside that contract; changing emitter degeneration or current bias belongs to circuit design.

Bipolar transistors and diodes need their own matching and interaction rules

Vertical bipolar devices. Match emitter geometry and use identical unit emitters for ratios. Active emitter area and perimeter determine injection, while base resistance, base/emitter contacts, collector resistance, Early effect and saturation alter the terminal currents. Compact clusters and common-centroid arrays help with temperature and stress fields, but contact and base distributions also need balancing. Placing collector contacts outside an array can bring its critical emitters closer together. When emitters share a base, do not blindly exploit the smaller “connected-emitter” spacing: overlapping depletion regions can alter carrier flow even when the terminals share a net. PDF pp. 506–526

Lateral bipolar devices. Both emitter and collector geometry matter because current flows laterally between them. A larger single emitter can reduce current gain, so the source often favors arrays of small emitters rather than simply enlarging one device. Match the collector's inner perimeter, base/contact environment and applicable field plates. A saturated collector can reinject carriers into neighboring collectors; equal nominal base nets do not make shared tanks harmless. Separate isolation regions can be worth their area where collector efficiency or saturation threatens matching. PDF pp. 527–529

Operating-point limits. Keep precision bipolar devices in their characterized current-density range, below problematic high-level injection and away from low-current leakage/recombination dominance. Equalize collector voltages when the circuit requires suppression of Early-effect error. Emitter degeneration transfers sensitivity from a transistor to a resistor and costs voltage headroom; it is an architectural option, not something the placer can add. Guard against reverse emitter-base stress, which can degrade current gain before an obvious catastrophic failure. A ratioed bandgap pair also relies on accurate temperature tracking; a common centroid does not remove all nonlinear thermal behavior. PDF pp. 509–523, 526

PN diodes. Diode-connected transistors inherit their parent family’s matching limitations. Control the actual regulated current: emitter-current regulation includes base current, so it is not identical to collector-current regulation. Match junction shape, contact arrangement, guard structures, material and surrounding diffusions. Distinguish series resistance from junction-voltage error and include leakage over temperature. PDF pp. 565–571

Zeners. Localized avalanche can let one region with the lowest breakdown voltage dominate the entire junction. Consequently, increasing area does not generally deliver inverse-square-root matching at low current. Surface walkout/walkback and unequal accumulated charge can produce later divergence. Use a characterized structure and lifecycle model; neither a circular outline nor CC placement can promise precise Zener matching. PDF pp. 566–567

Schottky diodes. Barrier material, inhomogeneity, contact perimeter, field relief and series resistance matter. A field-relief guard junction can improve field uniformity but can also inject minority carriers when it forward biases (§11.2, PDF p. 563). The source's comparison of aluminum and noble-silicide Schottkies demonstrates why material-specific primitives are necessary; it is not a license to synthesize an unqualified barrier. PDF pp. 568–571

For Philis, a bipolar/diode matching rule should name the matched output—VBE, current ratio, forward drop or reverse breakdown—and the controlled terminal conditions. Reject the tempting abstraction “all matched devices get the MOS area formula.” Validate geometry through extraction and the circuit's operating scenarios, including potential saturation and transient injection.

Worked examples: area ratio, base current and temperature

Under the simplified matched forward-active model IC = A JS exp(VBE/VT), two equal collector currents and an emitter-area ratio N produce a base-emitter-voltage difference with magnitude VT ln N. This equation assumes equal temperature and saturation-current density, neglects series resistance and Early effect, and is used here to expose sensitivities rather than replace a compact model.

Area-ratio example. Let N = 8 and assume VT = 25.9 mV. The intended magnitude is approximately 53.9 mV. If the realized ratio is larger by 1%, the resulting change is 25.9 mV × ln(1.01), approximately 258 μV. This is why drawing one emitter eight times larger can be inferior to constructing a ratio from identical emitter units: changes in perimeter, contact arrangement and dimensional bias may fail to track. Identical units do not eliminate random variation, but they make more systematic error mechanisms common.

Current-definition example. Suppose equal emitter currents are applied to two transistors with β values 100 and 80. Because IC = IEβ/(β+1), their collector-current ratio becomes (100/101)/(80/81) ≈ 1.00248. In the same simplified exponential model this alone gives about 64 μV of VBE difference. The example does not predict a process's β distribution; it demonstrates why an emitter-current constraint is not equivalent to a collector-current constraint. Layout can improve matching of the relevant structures but cannot erase a circuit's sensitivity to uncontrolled base current.

Thermal example. Suppose characterization of the chosen circuit gives a differential forward-voltage sensitivity of −1.8 mV/K at its operating point. A 0.05 K differential temperature then contributes about 90 μV. Those coefficient and temperature values are hypothetical design inputs, not universal constants. They show why a visually compact pair near one hot transistor may still miss an accurate reference's budget. Evaluate each device's electrically weighted temperature under all active power modes; distance to a heater is only a screening proxy. PDF pp. 509–523

The three examples belong in separate budget terms. Area/perimeter bias, β-dependent current error and differential temperature do not necessarily share a statistical distribution or cancel each other. Report their assumptions and correlations. Summing all worst cases is conservative; root-sum-square is justified only for the terms modeled as independent random contributions.

Diode matching and isolation must follow the operating mode

A PN diode, a Schottky junction and a Zener are not alternate symbols for the same physical primitive. In forward-biased PN operation, minority carriers can enter surrounding material; a Schottky barrier has different material and perimeter sensitivities; an avalanche device may conduct primarily through a small local region. Consequently, the engine must select matching and protection rules by device construction and operating mode.

For a forward-biased PN reference, use identical junction construction, contact geometry and guard surroundings, then separate junction-voltage error from current-dependent series-resistance error. For a reverse-biased sensing diode, leakage, collection area, perimeter and coupling may dominate. For a Zener reference, use characterized breakdown and accumulated-stress behavior; increasing total area does not establish an inverse-square-root mismatch law when the weakest breakdown region dominates. For a Schottky rectifier, check barrier qualification, current crowding, series resistance and the possibility that its guard junction injects when forward biased. PDF pp. 534–573

The collecting-guard figure illustrates a second distinction: electrical isolation depends on carrier paths in the substrate, not only on a drawn surrounding rectangle. Identify the injected carrier, the region it traverses, the junction that should collect it, the applied bias and the return impedance. A shallow ring can leave a deep path; an uncontacted or debiased ring can lose collection efficiency. A majority-carrier substrate tap and a minority-carrier collecting ring can serve different purposes even when their top views appear similar. PDF pp. 256–270

Sharing a well or tank also changes these paths. A saturated lateral collector can reinject carriers that another collector receives. Net equivalence and passing nominal LVS do not prove that merging is benign across switching and overload scenarios. Permit a merger only through a qualified template whose current-density, bias and injection restrictions are retained in the layout record.

Generation and validation procedure for Philis

  1. Select a qualified primitive. Preserve its exact vertical or lateral family, recognition layers, voltage limits and allowed transformations. The reason is that collector geometry and isolation cannot be inferred from an abstract BJT symbol.
  2. Enumerate unitizations. Keep identical emitter units for ratio-sensitive structures, while respecting current-density and base-resistance limits. Larger units can reduce some random effects but worsen access resistance or lateral-transistor gain.
  3. Assign physical units to electrical devices. Balance active emitter locations and, where relevant, base and collector access. A ratioed array's connectivity determines the correct weights; a bounding-box centroid is insufficient.
  4. Construct the complete environment. Add contacts, collector access, guards, dummies if qualified, wells and local supply returns. Reject contact arrangements that introduce unequal terminal drops or violate precision spacing despite being permitted for ordinary connected emitters.
  5. Route the actual sensed quantities. Preserve Kelvin joins where the circuit requires them. Match finite base-current paths when they contribute error, and include collector-voltage differences in the Early-effect calculation.
  6. Extract and evaluate scenarios. Check DC matching, leakage at temperature extremes, transient saturation, reverse emitter-base stress, substrate injection and relevant thermal distributions. A power transistor additionally needs a qualified forward/reverse safe-operating-area check.
  7. Retain a reasoned result. Report the modeled quantity, every constraint's source, extracted values and unsupported effects. A geometry that passes a transistor-ratio test at one bias has not thereby proven a bandgap's temperature curvature or a power cell's turnoff survival.

Required PDK information. Obtain recognized cell variants, emitter/perimeter models, β and Early-effect distributions or sensitivity models, permitted current densities, saturation/injection restrictions, reverse-stress limits, guard recipes and substrate stack, thermal/package assumptions and any power-device SOA data. These should be versioned facts, not coefficients inferred from a single textbook figure.

Focused regression examples. Compare identical emitters with unequal base-contact distances; equal emitter currents with unequal β; a matched pair exposed to a controlled thermal gradient; a lateral pair with one saturated collector; and a PN diode whose reverse leakage matters more than its nominal forward voltage. The expected outcome is that the checker identifies the correct mechanism, not merely that the common-centroid metric is zero.
