Analog Layout Synthesis: A Survey of Topological Approaches

Helmut E. Graeb, editor · 2011

Topological placement, hierarchy, routing, retargeting, sizing and constraint management.

Open supplied PDF · 311 physical pages · File identity and coverage

Choose a search space that preserves intent, then verify what that search space omits

Source: Helmut E. Graeb, editor, Analog Layout Synthesis: A Survey of Topological Approaches (2011). This is an edited collection with seven technically different contributions, not one algorithm. Its placement chapters explain how representations change which layouts are reachable and how much effort is spent exploring illegal candidates. Its routing and design-flow chapters explain why geometry alone is insufficient: connectivity, parasitics, sizing, technology migration and constraint semantics must remain consistent.

Engine-level conclusion. Philis should preserve exact constraints through suitable representations and moves, retain multiple useful realizations, and postpone irreversible choices until their electrical and routing consequences are understood. “Optimal” always means optimal within a stated candidate space, objective and model. A compact symmetric packing is not automatically the best routed circuit.

Coverage and chapter map

Substantive sections were read in every chapter, emphasizing definitions, methods, limitations and their application to Philis. Chapter 1’s contour-data-structure implementations and some of Chapter 5’s simplex derivations were surveyed rather than reproduced line by line. The reading includes chapter conclusions and selected experiments; experiments were not rerun, and references cited within the book were not independently reviewed. 1. Balasa: make symmetry feasibility a property of the encoding

Source finding. A sequence pair (α, β) specifies relative orders. In this chapter’s convention, A preceding B in both sequences puts A left of B; A preceding B in α and following B in β puts A above B. A packer converts the ordering to coordinates through horizontal and vertical constraints. The author’s central observation is that fast packing is of little use when most generated codes violate the analog symmetry requirements. PDF pp. 25–26, PDF pp. 48–51

For distinct members x and y of one symmetry group about a common vertical axis, the chapter gives the following sufficient symmetric-feasible condition (§1.3, Eq. (1.1)):

posα(x) < posα(y) ⇔ posβ(sym(y)) < posβ(sym(x)).

sym(x) denotes the partner of x; a self-symmetric device maps to itself. Mirrored partners reverse the corresponding order in the other sequence. This is explicitly a sufficient condition: the next page discusses legal symmetric placements excluded by it. Treat “outside this subset” as unsearched, not physically impossible. Multiple symmetry groups also need compatible interleaving/nesting; a group-wise condition alone does not license arbitrary intermingling. Nested groups require an appropriate dependency order. PDF pp. 49–50, PDF pp. 60–62

The chapter compares segment trees, red-black interval trees, deterministic skip lists and a specialized priority queue for contour/LCS evaluation. Asymptotic packing improvements are valuable only after the encoding, symmetry handling and update workload are fixed. Different representations also have different redundancy and move locality. Fewer codes do not guarantee better quality under finite search.

Action and reason. Prototype a symmetry-preserving topological seed generator for blocks where the existing coordinate optimizer spends much effort repairing overlap or symmetry. Keep physical scoring and verification common to both approaches so the experiment measures representation quality rather than different objectives.

Tradeoff and validation. Record reachable-layout restrictions and retain a fallback search outside the sufficient-condition subset. Enumerate tiny examples to verify the pair-order condition, packer and legal move invariants; include multiple and nested axes. Then compare completed routes and extracted margin under equal wall-clock budgets. The current separate_overlaps entry point (line 17) is a useful place to measure how much postprocessing a topological seed might avoid.

Orientation caveat. This chapter distinguishes mirror symmetry, identical-orientation “perfect” symmetry and self-symmetry. Identical orientation can better protect anisotropic-process matching while making geometric mirror routing impossible. Preserve electrical terminal correspondence instead of equating mirror placement with identical device orientation. PDF pp. 21–22

2. Lin and Chang: connected symmetry islands make hierarchy efficient

Source finding. A symmetry island is a connected placement in which each module abuts another member of the group. The automatically symmetric-feasible B* tree packs one representative from each pair and half of each self-symmetric module, then reconstructs the other side. Self-symmetric representatives must touch the symmetry axis; this becomes a boundary restriction on the tree. The hierarchical HB* tree embeds these islands among nonsymmetric modules and retains rectilinear contours rather than wasting every indentation inside a rectangular bounding box. PDF pp. 79–87

In Chapter 2’s B* convention, a left child starts to the right of its parent, xchild = xparent + wparent; a right child has the same x coordinate, and a contour determines y. Other chapters use their own traversal/packing conventions. Copying a child rule from one chapter and a compactor from another without reconciling them is unsafe. The reported linear packing bound is for the specified representation and contour operations; it is not a bound on a complete electrical optimizer. PDF pp. 80–85, PDF pp. 90–91

The search perturbs hierarchy and island interiors, preserving boundary restrictions. If an island’s outline changes, contour nodes and dependent modules must be updated. Extensions align multiple axes, nest groups, and insert nonsymmetric clusters within symmetric arrangements. Wells and guard rings still need reserved space; “connected” should be interpreted using legal module envelopes, not by forcing active geometry to touch. PDF pp. 91–96, PDF pp. 100–106

Action and reason. Use recognized hierarchy as a collection of alternative physical group shapes, including outlines, access sides, routing reserves and legal orientations. Pack whole groups while permitting coordinated changes inside them. This reduces unrelated movement and preserves close matching structures without flattening every constraint into pairwise penalties.

Tradeoff. An island policy can exclude useful space for routing, shielding or a central heat source. A proximity preference is not automatically an abutment requirement. Allow explicit exceptions and compare multiple group candidates; the chapter itself extends the framework beyond strict islands.

Validation. Test contour changes that remove an indentation, create a dangling child, rotate a self-symmetric representative, and combine different well domains. Check both overlap freedom and pin access. In Philis, Block (line 8) supplies a natural logical hierarchy, but a logical block should acquire a physical interface before it becomes an indivisible island.

3. Plantage: derive hierarchy, enumerate small problems, retain several answers

Source finding. The SMP multigraph distinguishes matching from symmetric partners, matching within a recognized building block, proximity within a block, symmetry itself, and general netlist proximity. An ordered clustering process turns those requirements into an HSMPG tree, then generates variant, alignment, symmetry and common-centroid constraints. This is useful because a transistor can participate in conflicting relationships, such as matching its differential counterpart and matching a current-mirror partner. PDF pp. 119–129

The proposed priority favors differential matching over matching within each branch, then block proximity, symmetry and general net proximity. The authors justify this by the relative effects on offset versus common branch behavior, and explicitly allow simulation-derived priorities. That ordering is an application-dependent heuristic, not permission to decide universally that offset matters more than gain.

Action and reason. Preserve every recognized relationship, its origin and the reason it wins or loses during clustering. Use circuit specifications and sensitivity when relationships conflict. Avoid silently discarding a mirror relationship merely because a differential-pair grouping was selected first.

Tradeoff and validation. An overlapping relationship graph is more expressive than a tree, but local enumeration needs a tractable decomposition. Keep crossing constraints at the parent interface, compare plausible decompositions on small examples, and verify the emitted hierarchy against the original graph. recognize (line 175) and placement (line 53) are the relevant existing recognition-to-requirement boundary.

LP and MIP packing. Given an ordering, linear spacing inequalities and symmetry/common-centroid equalities can determine compact coordinates. For a vertical symmetry axis xs, with lower-left coordinates and dimensions:

[(xi + wi/2) + (xi′ + wi′/2)]/2 = xs;   yi + hi/2 = yi′ + hi′/2.

These are center equations; copying them as corner equalities changes the meaning for unequal dimensions. A common-centroid equality additionally requires choosing the correct electrical weights. The chapter’s geometric group center is area-weighted; that equals the required electrical centroid only under appropriate unit-device assumptions. PDF pp. 112–115

Deep-trench-isolation devices illustrate a genuinely disjunctive spacing rule: sharing is permitted over a small interval, intermediate distances are forbidden, and sufficiently separated trenches are permitted again. The chapter adds binary branch variables and shared stretch constraints, rather than pretending one convex penalty represents the legal set. The full model also limits a device’s total left/right stretch, so independently legal pair distances can still be jointly impossible. PDF pp. 135–137

Action and reason. Keep explicit branch state for disjunctive technology constraints and coordinate branch changes across neighbors sharing a resource. Philis’s DtiBand (line 18) is already a branch-based entry point. Compare its pair abstraction with the actual foundry rule before generalizing the book’s DTI example.

Validation. Check both interval endpoints, the forbidden gap, a three-device chain sharing a middle stretch budget, and an impossible combination with symmetry. A solver timeout or rejected local branch change is not proof of global infeasibility. If using big-M formulations, derive finite bounds from the die and dimensions; oversized constants harm numerical conditioning.

Enhanced shape functions. A conventional shape function retains a width/height frontier. A horizontal composition gives (w1+w2, max(h1,h2)); vertical composition gives (max(w1,w2), h1+h2). Enhanced shapes also retain their B* trees, allowing contours to fit together more tightly. Plantage enumerates small basic groups and their compatible variants, then combines results bottom-up. This is deterministic hierarchy-guided enumeration, not an exhaustive enumeration of every full-circuit layout. Composition preserves child constraints only under the stated assumptions about additional parent constraints. PDF pp. 137–146

Action and reason. Retain a frontier of useful group variants rather than selecting one smallest-area candidate early. Extend the interface to include pin locations, access, critical parasitics, contours and environment obligations. A slightly larger shape can give the parent a shorter sensitive route or a better substrate separation.

Tradeoff and validation. More dimensions enlarge the frontier. Use bounded diversity or documented approximate pruning, and measure what was pruned. Width/height dominance alone is safe only when the discarded state is replaceable in every relevant parent context; it is not sufficient once pin topology or parasitics differ. Validate against exhaustive small-parent compositions, including two children with equal dimensions but different pin access.

4. Dündar and Unutulmaz: a routing representation must carry physical connectivity

Source finding. Uniform grids make implementation straightforward but trade memory for geometric resolution. Nonuniform grids adapt to obstacles; tile/corner-stitching representations retain free space efficiently but complicate escape points and updates. Topological representations express ordering well but lack the exact neighbor geometry needed for parasitic extraction. This argues for a hybrid architecture, not for using one representation in every stage. PDF pp. 168–174

The chapter distinguishes net membership, current-class subnets and physically connected groups. Terminals belonging to one net are not necessarily connected yet. Rip-up can split a physical group even though the netlist is unchanged. High- and low-current portions of a shared ground should sometimes use separated return paths, because voltage drop on shared resistance modulates a sensitive reference. Net splitting here is a routing topology decision and must preserve the intended electrical node. PDF pp. 172–175

Action and reason. Make incremental physical connectivity and branch currents first-class routing state. Recompute affected current paths after a branch or shared segment changes; verify opens independently from budget checks. A zero-length unrouted net must not count as an electrically successful route simply because its parasitic score is small.

Tradeoff and validation. Fully dynamic connectivity is complex; reconstruct affected nets when that is simpler. Test insertion, rip-up, via removal, preconnected primitive terminals, separate sensitive returns and multi-terminal current redistribution. Philis’s ParasiticBudget::satisfied deliberately lets zero length pass (lines 30–33), so the independent open check is part of the acceptance contract.

Mirror routing can route one side against obstacles mirrored from both sides, but crossing differential nets need a balanced connector and matched layer transitions. Equal wirelength alone does not equalize coupling to asymmetrical neighboring nets. Shields need an appropriate low-impedance reference path; a floating or noisy shield can worsen the problem. Extraction accuracy should increase from simple estimates to detailed models as candidates mature; the chapter separately discusses RF interconnects, where inductance and transmission-line properties can be essential. PDF pp. 175–178, PDF pp. 204–209

The routing survey distinguishes minimizing raw parasitics, enforcing allocated parasitic bounds, and optimizing sensitivity-derived performance costs. It also reviews interleaving routing with placement, constructing wires as movable layout objects, and templated routes. The LDS template example is particularly useful: independently preserving horizontal and vertical relations from one original layout can allow new overlaps after resizing; additional relations are needed to guarantee valid regenerated instances. Templates encode useful knowledge only if their valid parameter domain is checked. PDF pp. 182–193, PDF pp. 198–203

Action and reason. Use global routing to evaluate selected placement candidates and return congestion/electrical failure locations to placement. Keep successful local templates as reusable alternatives with parameter-domain validation. This targets repeated placement/routing incompatibility without requiring a monolithic optimizer.

Validation. Compare final connectivity, DRC, extracted matching, area and runtime against the sequential baseline. Include a template resized until an old visibility relation ceases to apply, and a matched route with a one-sided obstacle. The TrackGrid representation around line 394 is a current concrete grid choice to evaluate, not evidence that all analog routing must become gridless.

5. Said and colleagues: compaction preserves design knowledge only if that knowledge is explicit

Source finding. Retargeting starts with an existing netlist/layout, target device dimensions, target technology and optional constraints. Layer mapping, recognition and constraint extraction precede compaction. A basic difference constraint xj−xi ≥ dij fits a directed graph; longest paths in an acyclic ordering find the minimum legal coordinates. However, symmetry such as xb−xa = xd−xc involves four variables and does not fit an ordinary pairwise difference graph. The chapter’s coefficient-graph simplex retains general linear constraints while exploiting graph structure. PDF pp. 217–221, PDF pp. 227–238

Important practical limitations include stale edge order after orthogonal compaction, long wires created by independently pushing edges, conditional wide-wire rules, well-proximity/STI effects, changed device aspect ratios, hierarchy and full device replacement. A source well larger than the minimum might embody deliberate electrical intent; shrinking it blindly can destroy that intent. Via-array aggregation can reduce constraints, but reconstruction must retain required current capacity and legal enclosure. PDF pp. 241–247

Action and reason. Add a controlled reuse mode that preserves chosen topology, matching environment and route relationships while permitting PDK-driven dimensions and explicitly allowed variants. Prefer an established LP/MIP backend initially over reimplementing the specialized simplex solely for historical runtime claims.

Tradeoff and validation. Preservation limits optimization freedom and is not proof of electrical portability. Mark each invariant as preserved, transformed or reverified; regenerate constraints after geometry changes. Run target-PDK DRC/LVS, extraction and performance checks, including changed well margins and a via array whose old cut count is insufficient in the new process. Report an infeasible preserved topology distinctly from an infeasible circuit.

6. Castro-López and colleagues: size the circuit that will actually be built

Source finding. The chapter combines electrical sizing with a geometric-constraint module. For each candidate sizing, the module chooses geometric parameters such as finger counts and primitive shapes under a fixed template; the layout is generated or its parasitics estimated, then circuit performance is simulated. A bottom-up shape-function pass and top-down selection enforce aspect ratio and dimension goals. This recognizes that minimizing ΣWL does not necessarily minimize final layout area, especially after routing and spacing. PDF pp. 259–268

Two parasitic approaches are compared: generate and extract actual template instances, or use analytical device geometry with precharacterized routing tables. A naïve table with m samples along n independent dimensions requires mn entries. The authors first identify important parasitics and then sample their influential parameters more densely. That is a useful strategy, but a table remains valid only for its technology, template, model conventions and characterized parameter domain. Its validity is not unconditional across future sizing runs. PDF pp. 272–275

Action and reason. Feed Philis-generated geometry/parasitics into an outer sizing loop, first for recurring small blocks. Begin with direct extraction on shortlisted candidates to establish trustworthy data, then build a reduced surrogate only where generation cost justifies it. Track internal device parasitics separately from interconnect to prevent double counting.

Tradeoff and validation. Template reuse trades generality for predictable runtime and may miss better topologies. Preserve alternatives for electrically sensitive geometric parameters, even when one is smaller. Validate against held-out extracted instances and full circuit metrics; include unseen finger counts and near-boundary aspect ratios as deliberate out-of-domain tests. A simulation failure is an invalid/unknown candidate, not a numerical value to optimize through.

The reported experiments demonstrate nominally passing circuits that fail after extraction and improvements when geometry and parasitics participate in sizing. They support the architecture, not universal numerical speedups or signoff guarantees. For example, §6.5.2 says approach A takes approximately 15% more CPU time than approach B. Table 6.8 gives 880.3 s versus 612.31 s, about 43.8% more total time; because the iteration counts differ (3044 versus 2437), the per-iteration ratio is about 15.1% more. The prose is thus consistent with per-iteration overhead, not the stated totals. Specify the denominator and do not turn either historical comparison into a performance promise for Philis. PDF pp. 274–276

7. Jerke, Lienig and Freuer: constraints require a lifecycle and a verification plan

Source finding. The chapter separates technology, functional/electrical, methodological/geometric and commercial constraints. A required objective is a constraint; an optional “constraint” is an objective. It identifies five necessary services: management, derivation, transformation, sensitivity analysis and verification. DRC/LVS do not verify all analog intent, and a constraint that cannot be traced across hierarchy may be silently lost. PDF pp. 281–287

A higher-level limit can admit several transformations. An IR-drop bound can be met by changing placement distance, layer, width or routing topology. Fixing one transformation too early discards alternatives; a generated bound therefore needs its parent relation and validity assumptions. Constraint sensitivity also depends on the current design: a width change may matter greatly for a narrow wire and little after widening. Derivatives of quantities with unlike units require normalization before ranking. PDF pp. 288–291

Action and reason. Extend requirement metadata with stable identity, source, parent specification, derived-from links, units, scope, assumptions, enforcement tier, calibration/version and verification method. Keep the original electrical constraint even after deriving a cheap geometric proxy. This makes failures explainable and allows the engine to revise allocations without discarding the actual requirement.

Tradeoff and validation. More metadata and dependency tracking create engineering cost, but they prevent stale decisions after hierarchy collapse, variant changes or technology updates. Test rename/split/merge transformations, inherited shield constraints and conflicting spacing/symmetry requirements. Requirements currently identifies priced batches by kind and ordinal according to its lines 15–17 contract; persistent provenance should not accidentally depend on reorderable list position.

The proposed constraint-engineering system uses logic rules, specialized solvers and adapters to external tools. Its lasting idea is decomposition: a high-level requirement selects relevant objects, obtains electrical/geometric facts, and evaluates the necessary relation. Philis does not need to adopt Prolog to preserve that architecture. Cheap structural failures can precede expensive simulation; static final verification must use a frozen design/constraint snapshot; dynamic optimization checks need cache invalidation after relevant changes. PDF pp. 291–298

Action and reason. Produce a coverage report for final verification: which constraints were checked, against which model, which were unsupported, and which depended on uncalibrated proxies. Diagnose conflicting subsets where possible. This distinguishes “all implemented checks passed” from “all intended circuit requirements passed.”

Validation. Inject a missing model, a stale geometry cache, an inconsistent pair of constraints and an unsupported inherited requirement; each must produce an explicit result. Do not automatically relax true technology or user requirements to make the optimizer finish. Relax a derived proxy only by revisiting its valid transformation and rechecking its parent requirement.

Practical order of adoption

  1. Constraint provenance and verification coverage first: they make every subsequent representation experiment comparable and explainable.
  2. Small-group candidate frontiers second: they directly exploit existing recognized blocks without forcing a global algorithm rewrite.
  3. Topology-preserving moves and optional topological seeds next: adopt them where measured repair/rejection cost demonstrates a need.
  4. Routing feedback and physical connectivity contracts: these connect packing quality to actual circuit feasibility.
  5. Template-aware sizing and reuse modes: introduce them after consistent geometry/extraction interfaces exist.

The decisive benchmark should report completed, verified routed layouts; specification margins across required conditions; runtime and search variability; and the exact candidate-space restrictions. A representation’s packing speed, or a visually pleasing symmetry pattern, cannot substitute for those outcomes.
