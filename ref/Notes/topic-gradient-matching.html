Gradients, common centroid and higher moments

Derive cancellation, ratio patterns, odd-count exceptions and environment constraints.

A matched layout makes corresponding electrical quantities respond similarly to the spatial fields they encounter. Common-centroid construction is one technique for doing this. To enforce it well, the P&R must represent the quantity being averaged, the unit geometry, the local surroundings, and the routes connecting those units.

Derive constraints from the difference or ratio that matters

Many analog circuits depend more strongly on differences and ratios than on absolute device values: input-pair offset, mirror current ratio, resistor-ladder ratios, and capacitor DAC weights. Matching techniques arrange units so a shared physical variation affects the compared quantities similarly. The initial constraint is therefore an electrical requirement, such as a maximum input offset or ratio error; geometric matching constraints are derived means of satisfying it.

A common process shift cancels only when the compared quantities have an appropriate common response. Equal transistors at different drain bias can convert the same parameter perturbation into different current changes. Resistors of different materials need not track temperature. Record device family, composition, bias, and metric before enforcing a pattern. Parameter-to-performance sensitivity, PDF p. 1 The random-mismatch chapter develops the bias and geometry implications.

Derive the centroid equality

For an equal-unit first-order model, write p(x,y)=p₀+gₓx+gᵧy. A device with m units samples the average of this field. Subtract the normalized values of A and B:

p̄A − p̄B = gₓ(x̄A − x̄B) + gᵧ(ȳA − ȳB).
x̄A=x̄B and ȳA=ȳB therefore cancel every linear gradient direction.

Our extension to unequal contributing units replaces the ordinary mean with an explicitly justified electrical weighting. For ratio matching, normalize each member by its nominal value; this presumes an additive response to the specified field. The nth-order paper itself proves its recursive construction for two identically designed devices, not arbitrary weighted ratios. Comparing unnormalized coordinate sums of a 1:4 bank is incorrect because the larger member has four times the nominal contribution. Centroid equations, PDF p. 1 Additive unit model, PDF pp. 2–3

Constraint. Equal normalized active-unit first moments. Inputs. Unit coordinates, parent device, relevant electrical weights, exact ratio. Enforcement. Constructive pattern or explicit equality in the array solver. Reason. It eliminates the unknown linear gradient coefficients from the differential quantity. Limit. Macro bounding-box centers and areas are not electrical weights: guards, whitespace, and pin extensions can change the box without changing the active device.

The review demonstrates small CC transistor structures that perform worse because their parasitic and LDE mismatch exceeds their gradient benefit. Higher-order gradient constraints

For a polynomial field, define Mₐᵦ=Σwᵢxᵢᵃyᵢᵇ/Σwᵢ. To cancel arbitrary second-order gradients, match x², xy, and y² in addition to x and y. Equal radial moments x²+y² alone miss anisotropic x²−y² and mixed xy fields.

Place A at x=±1.5 and B at x=±0.5. ABBA has zero first-moment difference but an x² difference of 2. A quadratic field coefficient q therefore causes normalized mismatch 2q. The interactive moment laboratory demonstrates the difference between first-, second-, and third-order patterns.

The nth-order paper recursively reflects a lower-order pattern, retaining labels at odd target orders and exchanging A/B at even target orders. Starting with two units per device, it reaches 2n units per device. Constraint policy. Certify every requested monomial, but choose the required order from characterized spatial fields and circuit sensitivity. Reason for restraint. More units introduce contacts, boundaries, routes, and parasitics; algebraic cancellation alone does not justify their cost. Recursive construction and proof, PDF pp. 2–3

Finite areas and nonlinear device response

Real devices occupy area. Their moments are integrals over the region that contributes electrically. Identical translated unit shapes often make internal shape contributions cancel, but differently shaped units require explicit treatment. A shared geometric center does not establish equal second moments.

The physical process field and the electrical parameter may also differ. If oxide spacing t varies linearly, capacitance proportional to 1/t contains higher-order terms. Matching the first moments of t therefore does not prove exact capacitance matching. The DAC paper discusses how this nonlinearity leaves residual error as the gradient grows. Thickness-to-capacitance nonlinearity, PDF p. 12

Required model metadata. State whether a certificate concerns active geometry, threshold voltage, sheet resistance, capacitance, or sensitivity-weighted circuit contribution. Validate the approximation by perturbing the actual modeled process quantity. This keeps a geometric proof from being overextended into an unsupported electrical guarantee.

Separate environment constraints from centroid constraints

Compare the distributions of well-edge distance, diffusion-edge distance, orientation, dummy environment, neighboring geometry, metal coverage, contact geometry, and thermal exposure. Any can differ at coincident centroids. For MOS LOD, inner and outer ABBA units can have different stress even with identical W/L. Swapping A/B roles across rows can balance some contexts, but the resulting device orientations and routes still require checks. LDE mechanisms, PDF p. 2 ABBA counterexample, PDF p. 6

Enforcement technique. Generate contextual device variants with known edge conditions; use dummies and common pitch where characterized; constrain environment descriptors or their predicted device shifts. Preserve legal current orientation separately from macro mirror symmetry. The MOS construction chapter supplies the device-specific rules.

Reason for separate diagnostics. A single opaque matching score cannot tell whether a failure requires more active area, a different well boundary, a changed pattern, or a better source connection. Report moment, environment, and route mismatches independently, then translate them into a shared circuit error budget when calibrated sensitivities exist.

Proximity and dispersion protect different terms

Proximity keeps devices in a region where process and temperature fields may be similar. Dispersion makes multi-unit devices sample the same field across an array. Maximum mixing is not automatically optimal because it can increase array extent, wire length, and vias.

For linearized mismatch e=dᵀδp:
Var(e)=dᵀΣd.
d holds signed normalized electrical contributions;
Σ contains unit variances and pairwise covariances.

Reordering independent identical units at fixed total active area does not change the independent random term. Rearranging correlated units changes off-diagonal covariance terms and may reduce differential variance. Calibrate the spatial covariance model and check that it is positive semidefinite. The DAC authors explicitly warn that inherited correlation constants are not measured capacitor-process truth. Variation classification and covariance, PDF pp. 2–3 Correlation-data limitations, PDF p. 12

P&R metric. Use calibrated predicted differential variance where available; otherwise expose dispersion and distance as proxies with uncertainty. Sweep plausible correlation lengths and gradients instead of using one assumed number as a manufacturing constraint.

Construct a usable matched array, step by step

  1. Select legal units and their composition law. Verify terminals, active contribution, and permitted orientations; otherwise the array may realize the wrong circuit.
  2. Choose counts and identify impossible exact conditions. Distinct nonoverlapping identical single-site units on the chosen grid cannot share one physical location; report an approximation or subdivide only if permitted.
  3. Generate coordinates satisfying the chosen moments. Use central pairs, recursive patterns, or integer optimization. Constructive equalities reduce the burden on global search.
  4. Constrain adjacency and environment. Enforce diffusion-net compatibility, well/tap rules, dummies, and orientation. A short cannot be repaired by relaxing a matching score.
  5. Reserve internal route resources. Expose pin access, contact placement, and corresponding electrical paths before accepting the pattern.
  6. Certify the drawn result. Recompute counts, moments, environment descriptors, connectivity, and internal parasitics after every relevant transformation.
  7. Compare extracted circuit outcomes. Use common variation samples across candidates, keeping feasible alternatives with worthwhile tradeoffs.

Every step protects an assumption used by the earlier derivation. A moment certificate created before arbitrary internal routing is only a partial certificate.

Worked ratio and odd-count cases

Let A contain two units at (−1,0),(1,0), and B contain four at (−1,−1),(1,−1),(−1,1),(1,1). Both normalized centroids are (0,0), while their nominal values retain a 1:2 ratio. Their normalized y² moments differ by 1. This pattern matches linear fields but not every quadratic field.

One A unit at the origin and two B units placed symmetrically can share a centroid if the footprints fit. Two different nonoverlapping identical single-site units on the chosen grid cannot both occupy that origin. This restriction concerns that unit-cell representation; arbitrarily nested or differently shaped devices may have coincident centroids. A solver should explain that conflict or attach a quantified residual to an approximation. The DAC paper places its odd-count members near the array center and acknowledges that they are not exact CC members. Odd-count placement, PDF p. 7

Technique selection. Keep the circuit ratio fixed. If subdivision is allowed, check whether it changes contact resistance, perimeter effects, random mismatch, or device-model interpretation. If it is not allowed, do not manufacture an extra electrical unit merely to improve the picture.

Implementable constraints and discriminating tests

The current CentroidGroup uses bounding-box areas and macro centers. Add a unit-layout certificate to generated macros, transform it with each macro, and evaluate matching by electrical member. Preserve local coordinates through device-to-cell collapse. Keep the existing global cost as a clearly scoped search proxy.

For integer-grid equal-unit patterns, compare normalized sums by cross multiplication and bound arithmetic overflow. For sensitivity-derived floating weights, attach tolerances and model uncertainty instead of describing numerical equality as exact.

Tests. Include ABBA with zero first/nonzero second moments; a ratioed bank; an impossible odd-count case; a rotated macro; one unit displaced by one grid step; altered dummies at fixed centroids; and equal placements with unequal internal routes. Each check should detect its own mechanism. Circuit simulation should confirm the direction and importance of the predicted improvement before the constraint receives a strong optimization priority.
