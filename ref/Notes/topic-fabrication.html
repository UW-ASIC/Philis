Fabrication mechanisms and geometry

Connect process physics, masks, contacts and isolation to geometric constraints.

A fabrication-aware engine must connect every geometric action to the material, device or manufacturing mechanism it changes. This chapter teaches that translation from physical process to constraints, models and verifiable layout decisions.

From masks to an electrical object

The first task in analog placement and routing is to know what a shape means physically. A layer name and a rectangle are insufficient: the same N-type region can be an ohmic contact, a collector, a diode cathode or a collecting guard, depending on its depth, enclosing material and voltage. A process stack determines which regions connect, which form junctions, and which are separated only while a junction remains reverse biased. Those distinctions explain why a layout can be DRC-clean and nominally LVS-correct yet inject current into a neighbor during a transient.

Hastings develops this argument through device physics, fabrication and three representative processes. The engine should use those chapters to understand mechanisms, then obtain actual dimensions and legal constructions from its PDK. Do not convert an old process example into a modern generic cell. The representative-process chapter uses standard bipolar, polysilicon-gate CMOS and analog BiCMOS as archetypes. Appendix C explicitly says its numerical example rules represent older process classes and do not correspond to any single real process. PDF pp. 63–64, 154–210, Appendix C, PDF p. 836

Fabrication knowledge must travel with the geometry

Chapters 1–4 explain why shapes on different layers are not interchangeable obstacles. Diffusion extends beyond the drawn opening; depletion extends beyond the metallurgical junction; implanted dopants have tails; oxide steps alter topography; local pattern density alters printing, etching, or polishing; contacts and vias depend on the actual metal process. Minimum dimensions include manufacturing tolerances and electrical requirements. Consequently, a device footprint alone cannot express all interactions. §§2.3–2.6, PDF pp. 79–95, §2.7, PDF pp. 99–110, §3.2.3, PDF pp. 138–144

Drawn dimensions, effective dimensions and environmental effects

A drawn rectangle is the request sent to manufacturing. Printing, etching and lateral diffusion change the dimensions that matter to conduction. Depletion then changes the conducting cross-section under bias. The PCell may already account for some of these effects; extraction and compact models may account for others. Applying a second ad hoc correction can double-count an effect, while ignoring all corrections treats nominal artwork as a measured electrical device.

Worked example. Consider two nominally equal sheet-resistance materials patterned into strips. If both have width 2 μm and the same process widens each by 0.1 μm, the fractional width change is equal. If one strip instead has width 1 μm and the other 2 μm, the same absolute widening produces different fractional changes: 10% versus 5%. Their resistance ratio changes even when the drawn lengths were chosen to give the intended ratio. The numbers are an illustrative perturbation, not a claim about a specific process. The engineering response is to prefer equal-width units or explicitly optimize sensitivity to the dimensional bias.

A similar argument applies to capacitors: area and perimeter do not scale in the same proportion when the dimensions change. Contact end effects introduce an approximately additive resistance that cannot be removed by scaling length alone. Source/drain extension, well-edge spacing and STI proximity can change transistor behavior without changing its drawn channel dimensions. Each mechanism needs its own state variables.

Neighborhoods operate at several scales. Adjacent gates and dummy shapes affect local etch/printing; oxide steps and well boundaries affect topography and strain; metal-density windows affect polishing; package features act across much larger distances. One isotropic halo cannot faithfully represent all these effects. A halo is a conservative routing abstraction, useful when qualified, but should retain its underlying mechanism and layer set. How to translate a process fact into an engine rule

  1. Identify the object and mechanism. For example, “well implant scattering near a MOS channel,” rather than the vague instruction “keep devices away from edges.” This determines which geometry and device variant are relevant.
  2. Identify the physical input. The model may require distance to an edge, orientation, well shape, neighboring diffusion, terminal voltage, temperature or fill density. Missing inputs should be explicit.
  3. Choose the representation. Some requirements belong in a qualified cell template; others belong in a placement constraint, route restriction, extracted device parameter or signoff check. Enforcing a cell-internal rule only after routing makes avoidable rework likely.
  4. Attach the validity domain. State process option, device family, temperature/bias range and geometry range. A model fitted to one long-channel device cannot establish a guarantee for a different oxide or pocket implant.
  5. State the consequence. Report the quantity affected, such as threshold shift, current-ratio error, junction leakage or capacitance. This lets circuit sensitivity determine priority.
  6. Validate after transformation and composition. Rotation, merging, abutment, fill and neighboring blocks can change the originally qualified environment. Verification must consume the assembled layout.

Data model proposal. Give each primitive a stable PDK identity, device/extraction model, legal parameter and transform domains, active electrical moments, terminal polygons, well/body topology, local environment requirements and characterization provenance. Keep geometry rules and electrical models related but distinct. A database of scalar distances is useful for polygon legality, but cannot by itself represent a voltage-dependent junction or an aging-dependent mismatch constraint.

Validation example. Generate a qualified MOS cell in two legal orientations, then put one copy next to a well edge and one in the well interior. DRC may accept both. The LDE-aware extractor should report the difference that the device model expects; the analog checker should decide whether it spends too much of the circuit's error budget. If the extractor has no LDE model, report that limitation rather than assigning the same certified current to both.

Process and package choices that placement cannot invent

Fabrication choices include available resistor materials, capacitor dielectrics, gate oxides, isolation regions, thick metal and buried layers. They can change the best layout dramatically, but most are fixed before placement begins. The engine can compare permitted alternatives; it cannot assume an extra mask or unsupported material exists. Similarly, packaging sets thermal paths, stress distribution, bond rules and external inductance. A thermal isolation recommendation is meaningless unless the package model and power scenario are identified.

Keep a distinction between geometrically possible, extractable, modeled, and qualified. A creative merged structure might meet dimensions and be recognized by a custom extractor while still lacking reliable characterization. Production automation should favor known templates, retain experimental alternatives separately, and state which evidence would be needed to promote them.

The highest-value process questions to answer next are: which LDE parameters the compact models accept; how PCells emit those parameters; what transformations and abutments are qualified; which density/fill and antenna steps belong to the final flow; and whether the selected package has a usable thermal/stress abstraction. Those answers determine how far a physical “optimality” claim can go.
