Performance-driven wire sizing

Choose legal widths and parallel routes using resistance, capacitance, current and space budgets.

Wire width is a discrete physical design choice with competing effects: it changes resistance, capacitance, current density, spacing, pin access, and congestion. A good router chooses width to satisfy reliability and circuit requirements, rather than widening every net or minimizing every wire's capacitance.

Derive the competing constraints

For a uniform metal strip, resistance scales approximately as R□L/W. Increasing W can reduce series voltage drop and source degeneration. At the same time, larger conductor area and changed neighboring gaps can increase shunt or coupling capacitance. The effect on the circuit depends on where the wire sits: a low-impedance source path may benefit, while a high-impedance pole may become slower.

Current-density and voltage-drop limits provide lower-width requirements; routing capacity, spacing, and capacitance can impose upper practical limits or discrete alternatives. Width-dependent design rules mean a route that is legal at one width may fail after widening even if its centerline does not move. Physical motivation, PDF pp. 2–4

Constraint representation. Store a legal option set by layer and path type, including widths, spacing envelopes, via/access strategies, and characterized RC. For advanced gridded processes the options may be parallel tracks rather than arbitrary widths. For another process, continuous drawn widths snapped to a grid may be legal. The PDK determines the set.

A simple worked tradeoff

Assume a toy first-order objective τ(w)=(Rdriver+a/w)(Cload+bw), with positive constants and a continuous width w. Expanding gives Rdriver·Cload + Rdriver·bw + a·Cload/w + ab. Differentiating gives an interior stationary point:

w* = √[a·Cload / (Rdriver·b)]
when Rdriver>0 and b>0,
then clip to feasible bounds and compare legal discrete neighbors.

This is an original illustrative derivation, not the objective used by the wire-sizing paper. It explains why a finite optimum can exist: one term decreases with width and another increases. If Rdriver approaches zero or if capacitance barely changes, the tradeoff changes. In a real analog circuit, multiple nodes, poles, nonlinear behavior, noise, and matching invalidate this one-pole shortcut as a final selector.

Engine use. Such a model can rank early candidates or initialize a discrete search. Validate its predictions against the extracted circuit and abandon it when topology or operation changes. Do not hardcode the derived optimum as a universal wire-width rule.

Separate reliability minima from performance preferences

A wire required to carry a certain mission-profile current cannot be narrowed merely because the capacitance score improves. Derive its minimum viable cross-section from the appropriate process current-density model, temperature, lifetime, and waveform statistic. Then check the complete path, including contacts, necks, and vias; a wide trunk with a narrow escape is not reliable.

Within that feasible set, circuit performance may favor a particular width. Treat EM, IR drop, and transient voltage errors as explicitly named requirements, and RC-driven optimization as a separate decision. The EM chapter explains why average, RMS, and peak currents are not interchangeable, while power distribution explains segment currents.

Choose the granularity of the sizing variables

Per-net width is simple and reduces the search space, but a net can contain a high-current trunk, low-current leaves, and sensitive access segments. Per-segment width is more flexible, but adds variables, discontinuities, and contact transitions. Shared variables for matched routes preserve a useful symmetry, but equal width alone does not guarantee equal electrical paths.

Recommended progression for Philis. Begin with a small set of critical net or route-group variables. Add segment granularity only where extraction shows that a uniform width wastes capacitance or leaves a bottleneck. Group corresponding differential segments and maintain legal transitions. This concentrates simulation effort on changes with a physical reason.

The supplied wire-sizing paper uses discrete per-net choices, assigned after global routing and before detailed routing, with equal choices for symmetric nets. It demonstrates a useful tractable formulation rather than proving per-net sizing is always sufficient. Problem formulation and flow, PDF pp. 5–6

Parallel tracks are a topology, not just a width number

Two parallel conductors can reduce resistance when both are well connected at the needed terminals. Their effective resistance depends on lengths, contacts, current division, and any cross-connections. If only one track reaches a narrow pin escape, that access remains a bottleneck. Multiple parallel tracks also have different coupling and spacing behavior from one contiguous wide rectangle.

The paper realizes 1×, 2×, and 3× choices with parallel routing on a gridded process. The DAC paper similarly adds parallel wires and observes diminishing returns as capacitance grows and the critical bit changes. Carry the realized route graph through extraction instead of multiplying an old resistance by 1/N. Physical realization, PDF pp. 15–16 Parallel routing and shifting bottlenecks, PDF pp. 11–12

A practical sizing algorithm

  1. Establish a legal routed baseline and its circuit metrics. This gives an actual bottleneck and a comparison target.
  2. Identify candidate paths using sensitivity and current analysis. Reason: a small number of paths often matter more than most wires.
  3. Build legal width/topology options with their spacing and via requirements. Reason: unconstrained numerical choices may be impossible to draw.
  4. Evaluate a small discrete neighborhood. Change one or a coordinated set of corresponding paths, reroute locally, extract, and simulate. Include both narrower and wider legal alternatives where reliability allows.
  5. Retain feasible improvements and nondominated alternatives. Do not let improved gain buy a failed phase margin or lifetime limit.
  6. Refresh sensitivities and the critical-path set. The former bottleneck may no longer limit the circuit.
  7. Stop according to evaluation budget and measured improvement. Report the best observed candidate and search coverage, not an unsupported global-optimum claim.

This initial algorithm needs no learned model. It establishes a reference for more sophisticated methods and creates trustworthy training data.

When a surrogate can help

The paper's WAGN model uses device/pin/Steiner-node features and route features including width and via count. It produces a soft classification score denoted as the probability that a normalized FOM is below one chosen threshold. Calibration and manufacturing yield are not established by that notation. Search runs cheaply on that classifier, then a shortlist is simulated. Preserve the final simulation step: optimization actively seeks regions where a surrogate may be overconfident. Graph features, PDF p. 8 Two-stage optimization, PDF pp. 12–13

If using continuous relaxation, legalize the result to discrete choices and re-evaluate the resulting geometry. Partial floor/ceiling enumeration grows exponentially with the number of ambiguous variables, so cap it or use a structured discrete search. A route that falls back to a narrower width because of congestion must be rescored; the predicted result for the unbuilt wider route is invalid.

Adoption criterion. Compare total data generation, training, routing, extraction, and simulation cost at equal achieved specification quality. The paper's speedups are tied to its hardware, circuits, and evaluation budgets. Generalization to a different PDK or circuit family requires evidence. Runtime accounting, PDF pp. 18–19 Declared limitations, PDF p. 23

Philis implementation boundary

DetailedCfg already carries base width, signal/supply fattening, width-dependent spacing, via-array spacing, and net-current input. Extend it with a candidate option map rather than replacing these rules. Use the route graph to propagate chosen dimensions into every segment, escape, and contact.

The current current-per-width constant is a coarse process assumption, and the final winner cost is total capacitance. Introduce layer- and mission-specific reliability data and an electrical performance evaluator before interpreting the sizing result as optimal. Source/supply classification is already wired into the main flow; operating currents require a distinct data path.

Tests that justify the feature

Use a resistance-dominated source connection, a capacitance-sensitive high-impedance node, and a mixed RC-sensitive compensation path. Include a width-dependent spacing threshold, a pin-access neck, an asymmetric via array, and two matched nets sharing a width variable. Check that increasing width can help the first circuit, hurt the second, and require a tradeoff in the third.

Measure extracted R/C and circuit metrics after physical realization. Compare against uniform minimum width, uniform wider width, current-only sizing, and the existing router at equal evaluation budgets. A useful optimizer should explain why its selected paths differ, not merely report that a scalar FOM increased.
