Floorplanning, hierarchy and interfaces

Allocate device, route, power, guard, pad and package resources before local compaction.

An optimal floorplan gives every circuit block the environment, interfaces and routing resources it needs. Die boundaries, pads, protection, thermal fields, substrate paths and package constraints belong in the placement problem from the beginning.

Floorplanning chooses the physical environment of the circuit

Analog floorplanning is the decision about which circuit blocks share space, heat paths, substrate paths, routing resources and package surroundings. It is not finished when every rectangle fits. Devices that match inside a macro can experience a different external well boundary or fill environment after integration. A short net can be a poor route if it crosses a noisy return. A compact core can leave no channel for protection currents or a sensitive Kelvin connection.

The source's die-assembly chapter connects hierarchy, die size, pads, floorplanning, metallization and final review. That ordering is useful for automation: establish the boundary and interfaces early, reserve the resources that cannot be recovered cheaply, then refine placement with extracted electrical feedback. Keep circuit roles, physical constraints and process/package requirements visible throughout the hierarchy. PDF pp. 781–828

Hierarchy is an interface contract, not a wall around the physics

A macro interface should describe more than its outline and pin coordinates. It needs allowed transforms, routing access, supply and return requirements, voltage domains, well and guard interfaces, sensitive/noisy-net roles, thermal power scenarios, fill or RF exclusions and relationships to devices outside the macro. A pair that must match across hierarchy should remain a named electrical group after flattening or composition.

Rotation and reflection require special care. Rotating an entire matched block can preserve the relative geometry of its internal units, but it may change crystallographic orientation, source/drain direction, proximity to a heater or the surrounding package stress. Rotating one of two related subcells independently can directly break the intended matching relation. Transform the device descriptors, not merely the rectangle, then re-evaluate interactions with the outside world. A library's allowed transform set is a physical qualification statement, not an arbitrary optimization convenience. §15.1.1, PDF p. 783 (grouping matched devices); §13.2.1, PDF p. 691 (relative orientation); §13.3, PDF p. 715, rules 13–15 (package and thermal context).

When optimizing hierarchically, keep a record of which quantities are exact and which are estimates. An abstract macro pin may stand for multiple access shapes; its estimated thermal source may later split among devices. The placement stage should expose enough information to detect obvious conflicts without claiming it has already verified the flattened layout. Final extraction must use the concrete integrated geometry.

Worked example: the core is not always the die-size constraint

Consider a deliberately simplified square wire-bond die. There are 40 pads, allocated ten to each side, with a hypothetical 100 μm effective pitch. Suppose corner and assembly reservations require a further 400 μm per side. The pad plan therefore needs a 1,400 μm side. Separately, a 900 μm square core plus two 100 μm boundary reservations needs 1,100 μm. The illustrative die side is bounded by the larger requirement: 1,400 μm.

If placement reduces the core to 800 μm, its boundary requirement falls to 1,000 μm, but the pad requirement remains 1,400 μm. Core compaction has not reduced die area in this model. The freed region may instead improve channel access, guard separation, matching area, thermal separation or return resistance. An optimizer that scores every square micrometer of core reduction as a die-area saving would misvalue this candidate.

These dimensions are a worked abstraction, not package design rules. Real pads have unequal sizes and ESD structures; corners, bond trajectories, seal rings, scribe interfaces, power-pad multiplicity and bump arrangements complicate the bound. The engine should derive the external boundary from actual package and PDK constraints and expose whether the current design is pad-, core-, routing-, thermal- or assembly-limited. Historical textbook cost heuristics should not become current manufacturing economics. PDF pp. 785–810

Make routing feasibility part of placement

Pin access should be evaluated at the shape level. A pin centered on a macro edge is not usable if a guard, well enclosure, neighboring cell or routing-direction restriction blocks every legal approach. Critical nets may need a pair of comparable routes, a shield, a fixed join point or a wide-current transition. Those requirements consume more resources than ordinary shortest-path estimates imply.

A useful early channel model counts separate reserved resources. As an illustrative accounting exercise, a corridor with ten nominal track positions might devote three equivalent positions to a wide supply, two to a return/shield arrangement and two to pin escape and via access, leaving only three for freely assigned signals. The exact decomposition depends on geometry and layers; overlapping reservations must not be double-counted. The point is to avoid equating minimum wire pitch with available routable capacity.

At a corner, two channels that each seem adequate can fail jointly because turns and layer transitions overlap. Contact cuts require enclosure; a via array can exceed the width of its approaching wire; a wide conductor can conflict with output-grid parity. Use concrete access templates or a routing feasibility estimate that includes these effects. Preserve a feedback path from routing to floorplanning so congestion can trigger a move or channel enlargement before a late patch destroys matching.

Route priority should follow consequence. Reserve power and protection paths, critical local access and sensitive/matched connections while placement remains flexible. This does not imply one universal sequence for every design; priorities depend on which resources are scarce and which nets have hard electrical constraints. Document the reason for each reserved corridor. PDF pp. 811–817

Routing and floorplanning finish the analog circuit

Kelvin and star connections. The physical point at which sense and force conductors join determines which voltage drop the circuit measures. If they merge before the intended device or pad, shared current creates an unwanted offset. Separate net names and schematic resistors can assist LVS but do not prove the junction is in the right physical location. Likewise, matched emitters or sources should join an intended star at the actual location where common-mode rather than differential IR drop is desired. Include finite sense current and lead resistance for high accuracy. PDF p. 818

Noise. Coupling depends on aggressor slew, coupling capacitance, victim impedance and timing, not merely the aggressor's average frequency. A configuration bit that switches infrequently can still upset a sensitive measurement. Use circuit roles to separate incompatible nets, controlled crossing geometry, and shields tied to the relevant quiet reference. Check the shield's impedance; a shared noisy return can defeat its purpose. Slowing a signal is a possible circuit-level remedy only when its timing permits it. PDF pp. 819–820

Voltage and current. High-voltage spacing depends on the maximum voltage difference between adjacent conductors under allowed scenarios, not just their voltage to ground. EM is a local bottleneck condition; resistance is an integrated path quantity. The source's via illustration shows why vias near the inside of a corner carry more current than nominally identical outer vias. Prefer appropriate straight transitions and sufficient distributed access, then check the actual current distribution. PDF pp. 821–822

Capacity and access. Route pitch must account for via footprint, enclosure and output-grid legality, not only minimum line spacing. Reserve space for wide supplies, shields, matched routes and local access before filling channels with ordinary signals. At intersecting channels, available tracks can disappear into turning and via demand; a channel-width scalar alone is an imperfect congestion model. Use blockage/access-aware estimation early enough to revise the floorplan. PDF pp. 811–817

Thermal and stress placement. Conventional molded-package stress often has low gradients near the die center despite large absolute stress there. Heat-source avoidance can favor an off-center location. Bumps, pillars, die attach, board loading and multiple changing power sources create different distributions. Optimize the mismatch-relevant field difference over the active region, not simply distance from a heater or absolute stress. Preserve useful symmetry under unequal power scenarios rather than assuming equal power forever. PDF pp. 399–411, 525, 715

Whole-die feasibility. Core area and pad-ring requirements can limit die size independently. Pads, ESD devices, guard rings, seal rings, scribe interfaces, bumps, bond trajectories and assembly exclusions all consume resources. Put these structures and the actual pin order into the early floorplan. A smaller core may produce no die-area saving on a pad-limited chip; it may instead free area for lower resistance, extra taps, shielding or matching improvements. PDF pp. 782–810

Finalization. The book ends with a checklist covering DRC/LVS, high-current and resistance checks, antenna, ESD, latchup, dummy exclusions, noisy/sensitive routing, pad requirements, Kelvin locations, corner/slot/seal requirements and archiving. Philis can translate this into a reproducible evidence bundle: final layout and libraries, exact process/rule/model versions, extraction, evaluated scenarios, pass/fail/unknown results and constraint provenance. This is stronger than retaining only a final objective score. PDF pp. 823–825

A practical floorplanning workflow with explicit reasons

  1. Compile operating scenarios and interfaces. Classify blocks by sensitivity, injection/noise, power, temperature and voltage domains. A digital label alone is too coarse: an infrequently toggling trim line can still disturb a conversion.
  2. Build the actual die boundary. Place package pins, pads or bumps, mandatory exclusions, seals and primary protection. The reason is that these structures constrain both available area and current entry/exit paths.
  3. Place high-consequence regions. Reserve RF exclusion areas, high-voltage isolation, large power structures and precision matching groups. Use their actual access and environmental requirements, not only rectangular bounding boxes.
  4. Plan power, return and substrate networks. Evaluate where current joins the shared reference. Prevent a noisy return from traversing a sensitive reference segment merely because the nets have the same logical name.
  5. Place quiet groups using relevant fields. Compare thermal and package-stress mismatch over the active devices. A center preference or heater distance can be a first filter, but it is not the final evidence.
  6. Reserve and test routing channels. Include shields, matched access, via transitions, Kelvin endpoints and protection currents. Move blocks when feasibility estimates reveal a bottleneck instead of demanding impossible downstream routing.
  7. Integrate wells, guards and fill policies. Check that new surroundings preserve the intended device environment. A subcell's local rule pass does not automatically include integration effects.
  8. Flatten the relevant interactions and evaluate. Extract the complete interconnect and environments for the design's scenarios, then compare circuit metrics and reliability constraints. Keep a set of feasible alternatives when one trades area against precision or power loss.

The output should explain why each block is where it is: pin-access demand, a noise separation, a symmetry relationship, a thermal requirement or an available routing corridor. An explanation like “the objective improved” is insufficient if it does not name the underlying electrical consequence.

Inputs and checks that prevent a convincing but unusable floorplan

Obtain package geometry and mechanical restrictions, allowed pad/bump cells, seal/scribe rules, PDK voltage-spacing and well/guard rules, available routing layers and via constructions, power/current scenarios, noise budgets, thermal/package models and pin-order flexibility. Mark each as fixed, selectable from qualified variants or estimated. The optimizer must not assume it can move a package pin or change protection topology simply because doing so lowers cost.

Useful adversarial checks include a pad-limited core that becomes smaller without reducing the die, a narrow channel that fails only when via enclosures are included, an independently rotated cross-hierarchy matching pair, a sensitive sense lead accidentally joined to force current early, a trim signal that switches only once but injects excessive charge, and an RF macro whose exclusion is violated by final fill. For each, retain the geometric and electrical evidence that caused rejection.

Finally, review the finalized geometry rather than a pre-fill or pre-pad snapshot. Archive the exact layout, rule decks, models, extracted networks, scenario results and remaining external qualifications. A reproducible floorplan is one whose decisions can be re-evaluated when the PDK, package or circuit changes. PDF pp. 823–825
