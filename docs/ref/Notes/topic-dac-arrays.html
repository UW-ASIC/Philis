Constructive capacitor-DAC arrays

Balance dispersion, connectivity, ratio error, bridge sensitivity and worst-bit settling.

A capacitor DAC is a circuit made of a spatial array and its interconnect. The optimal array preserves the intended charge ratios while meeting code-dependent accuracy, settling, power and manufacturing constraints. Common centroid is one part of that contract.

Define the electrical contract before filling a grid

Start with the actual switch topology and reference scheme. For a conventional N-bit binary charge-scaling bank, the bit capacitors have 1, 2, 4, …, 2N−1 unit weights, and a grounded dummy contributes one more unit, giving 2N units in the bank. The name “dummy” here means an electrically intentional capacitor in the DAC denominator. It is different from perimeter dummy geometry used to regularize processing. Confusing these two kinds of dummy changes the transfer function.

A split bank has separate LSB and MSB top-plate nodes connected through an attenuation capacitor. Record the bridge's two terminals, the individual bank totals, and the intended attenuation ratio. Do not merge all top plates because a binary-array routing template did so. Nor should P&R silently replace a binary DAC with a split DAC: that changes the circuit architecture, reference loading and error sensitivities. Binary and split circuit models, PDF pp. 2–4

Why high-weight bits and carry transitions deserve special attention

Let T(k) be the normalized settled output for code k, after applying a stated offset/gain reference. With ideal step q = 1/2N, define DNL(k) = [T(k+1)−T(k)]/q−1. Define INL(k) = [T(k)−Tideal(k)]/q. These are definitions of measured transfer error, not a promise that a particular geometric score predicts it. Endpoint and best-fit references produce different INL values; store the reference with the result. Linearity definitions, PDF p. 4

Consider an illustrative 3-bit binary bank using the nominal full-scale reference, without gain removal: bit weights 1, 2, 4 plus a unit grounded dummy. Suppose only the MSB has an excess δ = 0.04 unit, with all other intended values exact. The total is 8.04. The 011→100 carry produces a step (4.04−3)/8.04 = 1.04/8.04 ≈ 0.12935 of full scale. Relative to a nominal 1/8 step, DNL ≈ +0.0348 LSB. The arithmetic depends on this normalization and simplified model, but it reveals why an aggregate RMS capacitor error can hide the important failure: multiple smaller bits switch against the large bit.

Action. Store the bit identity of every physical unit through placement, routing and extraction. Evaluate the transfer across all codes when tractable, or use a justified screening strategy followed by the critical transitions. Reject a candidate on its worst required error, not the average error across capacitors. For dynamic tests, also include transitions that produce large reference steps, simultaneous switching or asymmetric initial conditions; the static worst carry need not be the dynamic worst case.

Thermal noise sets another conditional lower bound on the effective sampling capacitance; it becomes a unit-size constraint only after the topology and noise allocation are fixed. Independent derivation, with a source gap: for one ideal linear capacitor fully equilibrated through resistance at temperature T, classical equipartition assigns its fluctuating voltage energy ½C·Var(v)=½kBT, giving Var(v)=kBT/C. The assumptions are thermal equilibrium, a classical frequency range and an ideal isolated sample after equilibration; finite settling, correlated sampling and complete converter topology change the result. No exact kT/C passage was located in the supplied PDFs, so do not attribute this equation to the DAC paper. A real charge-redistribution network has several noise sources and transfer factors. Derive the allowed unit capacitance from the system noise allocation before asking the placer to minimize area. Increasing unit C may reduce relative mismatch/noise while increasing reference energy, switch loading and settling time.

Unit geometry, odd counts, and what “common centroid” can actually certify

Use a qualified unit cell with explicit plate pins, layer occupancy, legal transforms, dummy requirements and capacitance-model identity. Identical drawn area alone is insufficient: perimeter fraction, fringe environment, plate orientation, vias and overlying fill may differ. Every unit should carry its electrical weight and local active geometry; an array's outer bounding box is not its capacitance-weighted centroid.

Even-count groups can be placed as centrally reflected pairs so their equal-weight first moments vanish about the array center. The bank's two one-unit members cannot both occupy the unique center of a nonoverlapping grid. The paper places these members close to the center. Report their nonzero moment residual rather than certifying exact centroid coincidence for the entire bank. Odd unit counts in more general ratios need the same honest treatment. Construction and one-unit exception, PDF p. 7

A larger bank does not guarantee better matching. A compact contiguous bit has short routes but samples a restricted region; a highly dispersed bit samples more of the spatial field but uses longer wiring and more vias. If the process variation is uncorrelated, average unit count dominates variance. If it is spatially correlated, the signed covariance expression dᵀΣd determines ratio variation. If it is systematic, moments or a characterized spatial field determine error. Keep those mechanisms separate, as explained in gradient matching and random mismatch.

Construct several pattern families because the objectives conflict

Spiral construction. Place low-count capacitors near the center and grow outward, adding units in centrally reflected pairs. Favor adjacency within the same bit so bottom-plate routes form relatively few components. The reason is electrical: high wire and via resistance can dominate array speed. The cost is weaker spatial dispersion and potentially larger nonlinear-gradient or correlated-variation error.

Chessboard construction. Disperse the largest bit across one alternating color and recursively allocate the other positions to smaller bits. This spreads electrical weight across the bank and exposes different members to more similar fields. The price is a fragmented bottom-plate graph: many pieces need branches, trunks and vias. A geometric improvement can therefore reduce settling quality.

Block chessboard construction. Group some same-bit units into connected blocks while retaining a broader alternating structure. Vary block size and assignment. This provides intermediate candidates between maximum dispersion and minimum internal routing. The useful optimization variable is a family of legal connected patterns, not an arbitrary label permutation that later proves unroutable. Spiral, chessboard and block variants, PDF pp. 7–8

Proposed Philis enumeration. For each allowed outline/aspect ratio, generate a bounded set of these families. Attach first/higher moments, bit adjacency counts, perimeter exposure, route channel demand and estimated terminal RC. Discard exact-topology or manufacturing failures. Keep nondominated estimates for detailed internal routing and electrical evaluation. Record rejected patterns and reasons so a failed array can be diagnosed as a missing legal representation, channel shortage or performance conflict.

Do not bake the paper's correlation coefficient or length into the generator. The authors explicitly describe these commonly reused parameters as inherited assumptions rather than measured capacitor statistics. Parameter sweeps can compare robustness to uncertainty, but only qualified data support a manufacturing-yield claim. Limits of the statistical parameters, PDF p. 12

Route the two plate networks with different objectives

On each bottom-plate bit graph, find connected components of neighboring same-bit units, for example with breadth-first search. Connect nearby units with branches, connect components with trunks, then join trunks through peripheral bridges as needed. Mirror the required connections for symmetry while preserving legal pin access. This reduces arbitrary routing decisions and makes the cost of fragmentation explicit.

Top plates share a node in a binary bank, so a tree can connect them while controlling length and top-to-substrate loading. In a split bank, construct separate top-plate trees for the two nodes. An MST is a useful low-cost starting topology under its edge model; it is not necessarily the minimum-delay or minimum-error tree once terminal loading, layer choices and extraction enter. Bottom- and top-plate routing, PDF pp. 8–10

Extract a terminal network that distinguishes intended plate-to-plate C, unwanted coupling between a bit route and a top plate, coupling to substrate/rails, and route resistance. Added coupling between two nodes contributes differently depending on their switching state. “These two nodes move together” is a scenario-specific simplification, not grounds to erase their physical capacitance from every simulation.

Widening the currently slowest bit or adding a qualified parallel track can improve delay. Recompute every bit after each change: the critical bit may move. Include the added capacitance and routing congestion, and verify legal via access at both ends. A parallel line that is only well connected at one end is not the assumed parallel resistance. This connects directly to wire sizing.

Derive the settling requirement instead of copying a frequency label

For a single-pole full-scale step with residual error exp(−t/τ), reaching half an N-bit LSB requires exp(−t/τ) ≤ 2−(N+1), hence t ≥ (N+1)ln(2)τ. This derivation assumes a single pole, full-scale initial error and that half-LSB allocation. If other errors consume part of the budget, the allowed residual is smaller. A distributed RC network or nonlinear switch needs its actual transient response.

The paper's Eq. (32) reports f = 1/[2(N+2)ln(2)τ] under its charging/discharging assumptions and labels it a 3-dB frequency. Preserve that model as a distinct paper metric. It is neither the generic first-order pole 1/(2πτ) nor a universal sample-rate limit. The handbook deliberately separates these definitions so an implementation does not optimize the wrong specification. Delay and frequency model, PDF pp. 5–6

A practical evaluator records the switch sequence, available settling interval, output load and measurement time. It reports the maximum residual over required codes, process/voltage/temperature and mismatch scenarios, plus reference disturbance. Use an efficient RC estimate to screen many layouts, then transient-simulate the candidates near the accuracy or speed limit.

A split-DAC bridge needs its own geometry and sensitivity model

The attenuation capacitor is often a noninteger multiple of the unit. Simply shrinking one dimension to obtain the desired nominal C may change its fractional edge sensitivity relative to the units. Under the paper's simplified MOM geometry, a common small edge shift δ gives approximately ΔC/C = 2δ(1/H + 1/l), where H and l describe its effective coupling geometry. The construction solves both the target capacitance and an equal effective perimeter-to-area sensitivity condition. Nonunit attenuation capacitor, PDF p. 6

The reason is ratio stability: the bridge and unit bank should respond similarly to common geometric errors. For Philis, enumerate legal quantized candidates near the analytic solution, extract them with the same neighboring environment, and compare their effective attenuation sensitivities. The analytic equations are a starting point for the stated MOM structure, not a general MIM or fringe-capacitor rule. Include bridge parasitics in the complete split-network transfer; its isolated nominal capacitance does not certify correct attenuation.

Where this fits in Philis and how to prove the improvement

The current capacitor generator constructs merged plate/comb geometries with a bounded set of aspect variants. A dedicated array generator should retain per-unit bit identity, internal route graphs and separate plate terminals, then expose a macro interface to global P&R. Do not claim that the existing per-device primitive implements the paper's routed array algorithms.

  1. Connectivity proof: compare generated and extracted plate/bridge graphs, including the electrical dummy and every bit count. Reason: a good matching score is meaningless on the wrong circuit.
  2. Spatial proof: compute actual weighted moments and a stated covariance model on placed units. Reason: a named pattern does not prove its instantiated weights or odd-count exceptions.
  3. Route proof: extract branch/trunk/via RC and check every bit's charging path. Reason: high dispersion can lose on electrical delay.
  4. Transfer proof: evaluate code INL/DNL and settling with explicit references and operating scenarios. Reason: these are the actual converter constraints.
  5. Comparative proof: compare spiral, chessboard, block and a simple baseline at equal unit size, testbench, PDK, legal outline policy and compute budget. Reason: an improvement must not come from an unreported size or simulation advantage.

The acceptance result should show feasible alternatives and their margins: one design may offer better matching at a slower rate, another lower area at higher reference energy. This is the applicable meaning of “optimal” for a constrained converter, rather than a universal pattern ranking.
