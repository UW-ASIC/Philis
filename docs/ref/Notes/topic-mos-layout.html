MOS layout, orientation and local effects

Match active geometry, signed terminal orientation, wells, STI, dummies and bias conditions.

A good MOS layout preserves device identity, bias, terminal orientation, active-area balance and local process environment, then verifies the resulting circuit. Common-centroid placement is one technique within that larger matching problem.

Start from the circuit’s matching target

A differential input pair is often judged by input-referred voltage offset at a specified current, whereas a current mirror is judged by current-ratio error at specified terminal voltages. The two use the same transistors but different sensitivity functions. The first decision is therefore to identify the matched electrical quantity, allowed bias range and importance of local versus global variation.

For intuition, in a strong-inversion square-law model I = β(VGS − VTH)²/2. A small threshold perturbation gives a relative current change approximately −2δVTH/VOV at fixed gate voltage. The same threshold perturbation contributes nearly directly to the voltage correction needed to restore a fixed current. This local derivation explains why overdrive and transconductance belong in a mismatch budget. It is an illustrative model, not a substitute for the compact model in short-channel or weak-inversion operation.

Hastings explicitly discusses the area rule's limitations, pocket-implant behavior and body-bias dependence. Philis should propagate characterized variation through actual operating-point derivatives and compare candidates over the circuit's scenarios. A distance penalty can encourage proximity while still being insufficient to predict offset. PDF pp. 686–690

MOS: distinguish voltage matching, current matching, orientation and LDE

Hastings begins MOS matching by distinguishing equal-current voltage matching from equal-gate-voltage current matching. Threshold and current-factor variation enter these cases differently. In the long-channel, strong-inversion, saturation approximation at fixed drain current, increasing width both averages threshold variation and raises transconductance; these effects can cancel in the relative current-error expression. Increasing length can still help. This is the basis of the source's current-matching versus voltage-matching sizing guidance—not a universal theorem for arbitrary compact models, inversion levels or pocket-implanted devices. PDF pp. 686–690, 712–714

Propagate mismatch through the actual operating-point sensitivities. A useful first-order decomposition is δI ≈ (∂I/∂VTH)δVTH + (∂I/∂β)δβ + (∂I/∂VDS)δVDS + (∂I/∂VBS)δVBS, including covariance where characterized. This is an implementation proposal, not a replacement compact model. Use derivatives from supported device models and simulation; distinguish fixed-current, fixed-voltage and ratioed operation.

Orientation is a terminal-direction property

Equation 13.61 defines Φ = (1/N)ΣΦi for identical units, with Φi = +1 or −1 according to drain-current direction. Equal signed orientation is required; equal absolute magnitude is insufficient. Three right-oriented and one left-oriented unit give +1/2, as do nine right-oriented and three left-oriented units. Zero is convenient but is not compulsory. In two dimensions, track both directional components. The formula and tilted-implant diagram were visually checked. PDF pp. 708–711

During pattern construction, retain source/drain labels and legal sharing boundaries. During hierarchy transforms, rotate or reflect the orientation descriptor consistently. Add a test in which two macros have identical outlines and coincident centroids but opposite normalized source/drain directions: the engine should detect the mismatch. For asymmetric drain-extended devices, use explicitly qualified transformations; apparent geometric mirroring can change a non-self-aligned channel length under mask misalignment. PDF p. 691

Worked construction: a matched pair with real terminals

  1. Choose a valid unit. Begin with a PDK-approved device variant and identical unit W/L. Verify that decomposing the original devices into these units preserves the authorized total dimensions and terminal topology.
  2. Assign units to devices. For two equal devices with two units each, ABBA or a two-dimensional AB/BA pattern provides coincident unit centroids under equal weights. This is the placement skeleton.
  3. Assign source and drain sides. Mark each unit's terminal direction before merging diffusion. An ABBA label sequence without terminal labels can accidentally give A and B different orientation averages.
  4. Check allowed sharing. Adjacent source/drain regions can merge only when the same electrical node and the qualified device structure permit it. Otherwise insert separation and recompute active moments and compactness.
  5. Complete the environment. Add end dummies, appropriate body contacts, well enclosure and active extension. Verify that edge fingers see comparable LDE conditions.
  6. Route gates and drains. Compare resistance, capacitance, coupling and access symmetry. Preserve the gate environment rather than adding arbitrary poly straps across one side.
  7. Re-extract and simulate. Confirm device identity, effective geometry, terminal correspondence, LDE parameters and final electrical error. Check again after fill.

The order matters because late terminal assignment can invalidate the assumed pattern, and late dummies can move the macro boundaries without fixing active geometry. The pattern generator should produce a complete electrical unit arrangement, not just an A/B coloring for a later stage to interpret.

Why zero centroid error does not finish the job

Imagine four equal active rectangles in an ABBA row. A linear threshold field across the row cancels in the average difference. A nearby well edge can still change just the leftmost finger; a source contact with extra resistance can shift one device's source voltage; a metal plate can change one finger's process environment; and a quadratic temperature field can have different averages over A and B. None of those failures is repaired by reporting a zero first-moment residual.

Use a staged model: an exact structural check for identical variants and legal terminal topology; active-moment checks for smooth fields; neighborhood checks for LDE and dummies; extracted RC and bias checks for wiring; and scenario/aging evaluation for circuit accuracy. These layers explain which technique addresses which mechanism without promising that one scalar score encompasses all of them.

Compactness and repeated small CC subarrays are useful when nonlinear fields are uncertain, but more subdivision consumes contact and route area. In a fixed footprint, that may reduce active area and worsen random mismatch. A route-intensive pattern can also add enough capacitance to slow a critical node. Compare alternatives at the circuit level and retain the reason for choosing a simpler pattern where it wins. PDF pp. 393–394, 710–711

Meaningful validation and required characterization

Build a test family that holds nominal W/L and connectivity constant while independently changing source/drain orientation, well-edge distance, active extension, dummy topology, metal coverage and body-contact distance. Where the PDK provides LDE or statistical models, compare the predicted current or offset; where it does not, distinguish a controlled-environment preference from a calibrated guarantee.

Test hierarchy transforms explicitly. A matched group entirely inside a macro can preserve its internal relations after rotation, yet the external well, fill or package environment can change. Two matching devices in independently rotated subcells can lose their relative orientation. A correct implementation transforms electrical descriptors and then recomputes external interactions.

For long-term matching, compare stress scenarios as well as initial nominal bias. Hot-carrier and bias-temperature effects depend on voltage, duty and temperature history. Equal initial geometry is not evidence that two differently stressed devices age identically. Finally, do not automatically swap source and drain for an asymmetric or high-voltage device just because an ideal symmetric MOS symbol would allow it. PDF pp. 691–706
