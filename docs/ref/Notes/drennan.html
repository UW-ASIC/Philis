Understanding MOSFET mismatch for analog design

Patrick G. Drennan and Colin C. McAndrew · JSSC 2003

Bias- and geometry-dependent mismatch and the limits of simplified scaling rules.

Open supplied PDF · 7 physical pages · File identity and coverage

Engine conclusion. The quantity to optimize is mismatch of the required circuit behavior at its actual bias. Gate area, distance and topology are useful inputs, but are insufficient predictors. Patrick G. Drennan and Colin C. McAndrew, Understanding MOSFET Mismatch for Analog Design, JSSC 38(3), March 2003, pp. 450–456, supplies the strongest correction in this collection to treating a universal Pelgrom area law as a complete electrical objective.

Source and coverage

All seven supplied PDF pages were read: introduction and local variation (p. 1); parameter choice and model inadequacies (p. 2); propagation/back propagation of variance (p. 3); measured/model comparisons (p. 4); current-mirror geometry and bias (p. 5); polarity and multiple-unit examples, conclusion (p. 6); references and biographies (p. 7). Rendered pp. 1–3 and 6 were checked for equations and table values lost in text extraction. The paper's examples use particular 0.13–0.4 µm technologies; numerical comparisons are examples, not present-day PDK constants. Supplied paper, PDF p. 1.

Local statistics and electrical sensitivities are separate

The local variation model states that length variation averages across width, width variation averages across length, and area-distributed physical fluctuations average across gate area:

σ²L ∝ 1/W; σ²W ∝ 1/L; σ²p ∝ 1/(WL).

This describes physical fluctuations such as edge roughness, dopant clustering, oxide and mobility variation. It does not imply that every electrical parameter has the same simple area dependence. An electrical parameter can be sensitive to several physical variables, and those sensitivities themselves change with geometry and bias. Local variation is also distinct from global process variation shared by a die: a process corner does not substitute for mismatch simulation. Eqs. (1)–(3), PDF p. 1; global versus local distinction, PDF p. 2.

For small independent physical perturbations, the governing relationship is propagation of variance:

σ²e ≈ Σi(∂e/∂pi)² σ²pi(geometry).

The general covariance form, useful for an engine implementation, is σ²e ≈ J C JT. This covariance identity is derived by applying Cov(JΔp) = J Cov(Δp) JT to the first-order expansion; the paper itself writes the independent-parameter case in Eq. (8). Correlations must either be retained or represented by a justified independent parameter basis; they must not disappear merely because a data structure stores independent coefficients. The paper evaluates sensitivities using the compact transistor model in SPICE, thereby including weak and strong inversion, linear and saturation operation, body bias, series resistance, and short/narrow/graded-channel behavior represented by that model. Accuracy is still limited by the compact model, measured data and linearization. Eqs. (7)–(11), PDF p. 3.

Why threshold and gain-factor shortcuts fail

Threshold voltage depends on oxide thickness, doping, geometry and several short/narrow-channel effects. β shares physical dependencies, including oxide thickness. Treating the two as independent random causes can count the same physical fluctuation twice. The paper reports that such double counting can produce substantial prediction error. Likewise, a threshold mismatch number is not generally an input offset number: the local input-referred relation is σVgs = σId/gm, with σId expressed in amperes and gm in A/V at the stated bias. If current mismatch is stored as a relative fraction, multiply it by the nominal current first. Both current mismatch and gm vary with bias. An input pair's performance must therefore be evaluated where it operates. Parameter distinction and Eq. (6), PDF p. 2.

Characterization using only nearly square transistors can hide incorrect geometry scaling. Wide/short and narrow/long devices are common analog choices, so a model that fits square devices can still mis-rank useful placement variants. Replacing drawn geometry with effective geometry alone does not recover every omitted physical dependency. The later model comparisons support the physical-parameter approach across the tested bias and geometry space; they do not license extrapolation beyond the process model's characterized range. Geometry experiment design, PDF p. 2; measured/model comparisons, PDF p. 4.

Geometry/bias and polarity reasoning, PDF p. 5; multiple-unit reasoning, PDF p. 6.

For an ideal combination of n identical independent unit devices, relative standard deviation falls as 1/√n compared with one unit. But replacing a single 80 µm-wide device with four 20 µm-wide devices at the same total width gives the same ideal result when width effects are neglected: each unit has greater relative local variation, and their averaging restores the large-device result. Do not compare an n-unit array with one small unit and advertise the result as a benefit over a same-area unsplit device. Shared or correlated systematic errors do not average away.

Table III is a further warning that physical unit count and functional role interact. In its particular mirror example, the reported relative current-mismatch standard deviation is 3.86% for one reference/one output unit, 1.52% for one/four, 3.05% for four/one and 1.41% for four/four. The reference current is scaled to hold the voltage bias constant. These results show that reference-side and output-side duplication are not interchangeable in that circuit, because reference-device errors propagate through the gate voltage. They are not a universal instruction to duplicate output devices. Table III and conditions, PDF p. 6.

Characterization strategy the engine can consume

Back propagation of variance fits physical-parameter variances to measured electrical mismatch using a matrix of squared compact-model sensitivities. Measurements must span geometries and biases that make each parameter observable. For example, source/drain sheet resistance becomes visible under conditions where channel resistance is relatively small; a dataset consisting only of extracted threshold values cannot identify it. The resulting regression is useful only when the parameter set is identifiable above measurement error. The paper distinguishes this physics-based parameterization from an empirical principal-component fit. Back propagation and observability, PDF p. 3.

Proposed model interface: accept either foundry statistical compact models or a characterized covariance/sensitivity service. Its query includes process corner, device flavor, W/L, multiplicity versus finger semantics, body/drain/gate biases, temperature and functional output metric. Its result includes mean, covariance or samples, validity limits and model provenance. Geometry and bias outside those limits must produce an explicit extrapolation/unknown status, not an apparently precise mismatch score.

When only a Pelgrom fit is available, it remains useful as a documented local approximation. It should carry its fitted geometry/bias interval and should not be silently extrapolated to a different transistor shape, device flavor, inversion region or unit construction. The paper's correction is a demand for physical and electrical consistency, not a reason to discard all calibrated area-based approximations.

Actions for Philis, each tied to an acceptance test

  1. Separate topology recognition from performance prediction. MatchingPair and Matching, lines 27–80, distinguish mirror and cross/differential structures but evaluate a common distance/area gradient ratio. Preserve recognition for generating candidates, and attach a bias-aware electrical budget to the candidate. Test a fixed-current mirror width sweep where area increases but predicted mismatch improves little; an area-only ranking should be flagged as insufficient.
  2. Preserve an explicit unit-device mapping. Track which physical fingers implement each reference/output device, unit dimensions, shared diffusion and connectivity. The reason is that statistical independence and current sensitivity depend on that mapping. Test equal-total-width split versus unsplit structures with an idealized model, then repeat with a PDK model including width effects; report the difference.
  3. Budget electrical symmetry after routing. Equal device geometry does not ensure equal VDS, VSB or source voltage when access resistances differ. Annotate the extracted circuit and re-evaluate mismatch and systematic error together. Test a symmetric pair with deliberately unequal source access resistance: the electrical metric should worsen despite unchanged centers.
  4. Keep thermal and spatial systematic models distinct from local random mismatch. Add their deterministic mean shift or covariance through an explicitly defined combination. The reason is to avoid double counting a physical process already included by the compact model and to avoid assuming that every error is independent. Test covariance-sensitive cases and common-mode perturbations.
  5. Search within legal sizing freedoms and report tradeoffs. A placement engine can compare approved geometry variants and request circuit re-sizing when needed; it cannot change W/L without re-verifying functionality. Accept a variant only if mismatch, headroom, speed, power and routing all meet their budgets. A low-mismatch but unsaturated mirror is not a better layout.

Validation and limits

Build a validation matrix spanning square, wide/short and narrow/long geometries; current and voltage bias; weak/strong inversion; drain and body bias; n/p flavor; reference/output unit counts; and actual routing asymmetries. Compare sensitivity estimates with independent PDK Monte Carlo simulations and, when available, silicon distributions. Inspect mean as well as standard deviation, and identify the physical parameter contributions. Small-signal propagation can miss large deviations, nonlinear tails and discrete defects, so a yield target needs a distribution and sample-confidence assessment beyond the local equation.

The supplied paper does not specify a complete placement algorithm, a universal distance limit, a stress model, or a universal orientation rule. Its contribution is the electrical objective and statistical model against which geometric placement decisions should be judged. Read together with the supplied Pelgrom and Duinmaijer paper and the common-centroid source notes.
