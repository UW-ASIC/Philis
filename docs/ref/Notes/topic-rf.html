RF, power and specialty-device boundaries

Know when distributed, electromagnetic, high-voltage or special-device models are required.

RF passives and power, high-voltage, annular, JFET and memory devices expose the limits of a rectangle-only optimizer. Their frequency response, distributed current, electric fields and process qualification must travel with the layout primitive.

Frequency, power and process-specific devices need richer primitives

Some devices cannot be evaluated accurately as a compact rectangle plus a frequency-independent parasitic estimate. An inductor includes an electromagnetic field extending into nearby metal and silicon. A power transistor distributes current and heat along its fingers. A high-voltage transistor relies on carefully shaped depletion and electric fields. A JFET or embedded nonvolatile-memory element depends on process-specific structures and operating modes that an ordinary MOS generator may not support.

The right abstraction is a qualified physical primitive with an explicit domain of validity: frequency, voltage, current, temperature, waveform, environment and permitted transformations. The engine can optimize placement and access around that primitive, or enumerate characterized variants. It should not silently substitute a generic device merely because its schematic terminal count matches.

Inductor optimization is a multi-parameter frequency problem

A spiral obtains inductance from magnetic flux linkage between portions of its winding. Adding turns can increase inductance, but also lengthens the conductor, increases inter-turn and substrate capacitance and places more conductor in regions where magnetic coupling and loss differ. Wider traces reduce simple DC resistance while changing capacitance, proximity effects and usable winding area. Thin or nearby conductors can support eddy currents that dissipate energy. These mechanisms explain why neither maximum turn count nor minimum DC resistance defines an optimum RF inductor.

Hastings recommends attention to upper low-resistance metal, short connections, the central opening, surrounding material and appropriate shielding. The process and operating band determine which option wins. A solid conductive shield can itself support loss-producing loops; a patterned shield is useful only if its geometry interrupts the relevant loops while its connection and capacitance remain acceptable. Parallel use of metal layers to reduce resistance is not the same topology as a multilayer series winding. PDF pp. 355–363

Worked screening example. For an illustrative 2 nH inductance at 2 GHz with 2 Ω series resistance, the low-frequency series-model approximation Q ≈ ωL/R gives about 12.6. Adding 1 Ω of lead resistance reduces it to about 8.4 even though the winding's nominal inductance is unchanged. This model deliberately omits substrate loss, capacitance and the frequency dependence of resistance; near self-resonance it is inadequate. Its purpose is to show that lead planning can materially change the circuit value of a supposedly fixed cell.

A first-order isolated LC estimate places self-resonance near 1/(2π√(LC)), but a real distributed inductor needs an appropriate frequency-dependent port model. Define the extraction ports and reference/return conductors, include substrate and neighboring materials, and examine the required band rather than a single nominal L. Compare Q, loss, resonant behavior, differential balance and coupling into or from nearby circuits. The optimal geometry is the feasible candidate that best serves the actual RF circuit.

Automatic fill is an integration operation on the electromagnetic device. Keep the cell's qualified exclusion region or regenerate its EM model with the intended fill. The same applies to a new supply strap, shield or overpassing signal. An identical nominal spiral with different surroundings is not automatically the same modeled component.

Inductors, power devices, JFETs and high-voltage structures

Integrated inductors. More turns do not monotonically improve performance. Winding resistance, skin/proximity effects, substrate eddy currents and parasitic capacitance compete with inductance and mutual coupling. High frequency makes conductor shape, neighboring metal and return paths part of the device. The source favors high-level, low-resistance metal, short leads, a controlled empty center and limited unrelated material nearby. A solid shield can create eddy-current loss; a patterned shield must interrupt the relevant current loops. Stacking metal in parallel is different from adding serial winding layers, which can greatly increase interwinding capacitance. PDF pp. 355–363

Treat RF passives as characterized electromagnetic cells with frequency-dependent port models, transform restrictions and exclusion regions. Evaluate Q, self-resonant behavior, loss and coupling across the specified band. Preserve equal terminal environments for differential inductors. Automatic fill and routing must honor these regions or trigger fresh EM extraction. Minimized DC resistance and a correct nominal inductance are insufficient acceptance criteria.

Power bipolar devices. Emitter debiasing, thermal runaway and turnoff current focusing distinguish continuous, switching and pulsed operation. Base contact placement and ballast must be designed for those conditions. A structure good for a linear pass element can have poor reverse-bias safe operating area during turnoff. Repeating a small-signal transistor until its total area is large enough does not establish power capability. PDF pp. 485–505

Power MOS devices. Include silicon, metal and package contributions to on-resistance. Limit distributed gate delay, keep source/body resistance under control, and check electrical as well as electrothermal safe operating area. A positive temperature coefficient in some operating regions does not imply universal immunity to current localization: the text discusses impact-ionization feedback and the Spirito effect. SenseFET units must sample comparable temperature, terminal voltage and edge environments; a small sense element at the edge can see first-finger effects absent from the average main transistor. PDF pp. 644–668

The rule of one-third: useful approximation, with a source check

For a uniformly distributed, singly terminated metal finger, the source derives an effective added resistance approximately one third of its disconnected end-to-end resistance. That differs from a lead delivering all its current to a load at the far end: current is collected or delivered continuously along the finger. Unequal material coverage, tapering or strong debiasing requires a fuller distributed model. PDF pp. 650–664, 870–876

Using the visually checked definitions on pages 871–872, let r = Rsheet/Wmetal be resistance per length, g = 1/(L RSi) be silicon conductance per length, and u = L√(rg). The exact one-finger model gives Rtotal = RSi u coth(u), so Rmetal ≈ rL/3 for small u. This notation renames the source's finger length W to L to avoid confusion with metal width.

Apparent source inconsistency. Equation E.14 as displayed omits the square root implied by E.1, E.2 and E.7 when expressing γW. In the same derivation, the displayed E.6 has a plus sign for the second exponential term although E.3 and E.5 require a minus, and E.8 writes the current prefactor γ/G although differentiation and units require G/γ. Equation E.9 gives the consistent total-resistance expression used above. The audit therefore checks the differential equations and boundary conditions, not an uncorrected transcription of every displayed intermediate equation. Its quoted approximation accuracy also needs care: accuracy of total resistance is not automatically the same as relative accuracy of the much smaller added metal resistance. Implement the exact dimensionless model or validate the approximation against it; do not copy an isolated penetration-distance threshold without reconciling the equations. This is our algebraic check of the supplied edition, not a publisher-confirmed erratum.

High-voltage, annular and JFET structures. Drift regions, RESURF, field plates, oxide options, body ties and isolation define their operation. Annular transistors are not ordinary rectangles with an interchangeable source and drain. JFET pinch-off and parasitic junctions depend on a process-specific channel construction. Embedded NVM likewise needs programming/erase voltage, endurance and antenna/protection considerations beyond its static footprint. Restrict automatic transformations and mergers to the characterized variants; offer unsupported structures as explicit capability gaps. PDF pp. 616–639, 669–685

Derive the one-third approximation and know when to stop using it

The one-third rule has a simple energy interpretation that is useful for implementation review. Consider a metal finger of length L with resistance per length r, contacted at x = 0. If current leaves uniformly through distributed identical silicon elements, metal current is approximately I(x) = I0(1 − x/L). The dissipated metal power is the integral of rI(x)² along the finger. Integrating the square gives P = I0²rL/3, so the effective additional resistance seen at the terminal is Rwire/3. A wire feeding a lumped far-end load instead carries I0 everywhere and dissipates I0²Rwire.

The approximation assumes the silicon current is approximately uniform. If metal voltage drop appreciably changes local transistor overdrive or conduction, that assumption fails: more current may be concentrated near the terminal. The exact uniform-conductance distributed model quoted above captures this electrical redistribution but still does not include temperature-dependent conductivity, nonlinear transistor operation or a resistive return network.

Numerical comparison. Let the aggregate ideal silicon resistance be 1 Ω and the metal finger's end-to-end resistance be 0.3 Ω. Then u = √0.3 ≈ 0.548 and the exact model gives 1.0981 Ω total resistance. The one-third approximation gives 1.1000 Ω, a total-resistance error of about 0.18%. If the metal resistance instead rises to 3 Ω, the exact model gives 1.8440 Ω while the approximation gives 2 Ω, an error of about 8.5%. These are model calculations, not measured process values.

The example also shows why percentages must identify their denominator. A small error in the total resistance can be a larger fractional error in the added metal contribution. Implementations should compare the approximation against the exact model in the domain where they intend to use it. Changing to both-end contacts or multiple taps changes the current profile and effective resistance; it should trigger a new network evaluation rather than reuse the single-ended factor automatically.

Power, high-voltage, JFET and NVM layout decisions

Power MOS. Optimize silicon, metal, via and package contributions together. Wider aggregate gate width may lower ideal channel resistance yet demand more gate drive and create a distributed gate-delay problem. Feed arrangements can improve switching uniformity while consuming contact area and adding capacitance. Source/body access affects both conduction and parasitic bipolar behavior. Evaluate the relevant pulse and electrothermal safe-operating-area conditions; a favorable temperature coefficient at one bias is not a general proof against current localization.

Power bipolar. Collector and base access, emitter debiasing, ballasting and turnoff conditions govern current spreading. A shape suitable for steady linear operation can be poor during reverse-bias turnoff. Retain the qualified structure and its forward/reverse SOA evidence. Geometrically repeating a small-signal cell does not establish a safe power primitive. PDF pp. 485–505

Sense elements. A small SenseFET at the periphery may sample different temperature, source/drain drop or edge behavior from the main array. Choose its units and connections to represent the intended main-device quantity, then extract the actual terminal differences. Its drawn width ratio alone cannot establish current-sense accuracy over load and temperature.

High voltage. Drift-region charge, depletion, field plates, well/body arrangement and oxide options determine field crowding and breakdown. Keep their exact qualified variants and bias restrictions. A field plate may resemble metal routing but participates in device operation; moving or filling it can change the model. Source and drain are often physically asymmetric. Model-aware transforms are mandatory. PDF pp. 669–685

Annular MOS and JFETs. Annular devices have curved channels, unequal inner/outer perimeters and process-specific stringer or isolation considerations. An average perimeter is not automatically the model's effective width. JFET channels and gates depend on specific implants, diffusions or epitaxial regions; their pinch-off, leakage and capacitance cannot be recovered from a generic planar-MOS footprint. Use recognized PCells and their compact/extraction models. PDF pp. 616–622, 633–639

Nonvolatile memory and trim. Programming and erase voltages, charge storage, endurance and retention introduce scenarios absent from nominal analog operation. Respect their wells, antenna protection, isolation and access requirements. A trim line's rare operation does not excuse coupling into a high-impedance node during programming or subsequent use. Device variants and trimming architectures must be explicit design inputs. PDF pp. 623–632

Generate candidates inside a verified capability envelope

  1. Declare the performance domain. Record frequency band, port impedance/reference, voltage/current waveforms, temperature, required lifetime and package context. This prevents a scalar nominal value from masquerading as a complete requirement.
  2. Select qualified geometry and transformations. Enumerate supported winding variants, contact schemes, power cells or high-voltage PCells. Reject unsupported shape changes explicitly.
  3. Reserve the full environment. Include EM exclusions, fill policy, guard/well regions, gate-drive access, power-return area and thermal space. These are part of the physical candidate, not decorations added afterward.
  4. Use staged evaluation. Apply fast conservative bounds first, then extracted RC, electromagnetic or electrothermal models as appropriate. Keep a clear label on each approximation and its calibrated range.
  5. Evaluate the circuit metric. Check gain/noise/oscillation behavior or power loss/sensing/SOA using the actual port and device models. A favorable standalone inductor Q or transistor on-resistance need not give the best circuit.
  6. Freeze and recheck the final geometry. Changes to leads, fill, shielding, contacts or neighboring power blocks can invalidate a previously characterized environment. Retain geometry hashes, model versions, ports and scenario results.

Required characterization includes metal thickness and frequency-dependent conductivity, dielectric/substrate stack, accepted EM ports and models, coupling/exclusion rules, device SOA and pulse limits, thermal/package parameters, qualified high-voltage structures, special-device recognition and programming conditions. When that information is absent, the correct engine behavior is a capability boundary with a stated external qualification requirement, not an invented penalty weight.
