Resistor geometry and precision ratios

Control contacts, bends, segmentation, thermoelectric effects and temperature response.

Precision resistor layout is an exercise in controlling how material, shape, topology, temperature and wiring affect the circuit’s value or ratio. The correct segment pattern is the one that balances those electrical sensitivities while preserving power, parasitic and manufacturing constraints.

Start from the error that the resistor network can tolerate

Resistance is often used as a ratio, a transconductance-setting element, a current sensor, a time constant or a degeneration element. Those applications care about different errors. A uniform 10% shift in two divider resistors can leave the ideal ratio unchanged, while 0.1% differential error changes its output. A current-sense resistor cares about absolute value and terminal definition; a high-value bias resistor may care more about leakage and voltage coefficient. State the function before selecting a layout technique.

Hastings distinguishes random variability from systematic geometry and environment effects. Increasing area primarily attacks some random contributions; a larger wrong geometry remains wrong. The useful engine contract includes material/model, value and tolerance, required ratios, bias range, temperature range, power, terminal-current direction, parasitic limits and any trim/Kelvin intent. PDF pp. 284–295, 368–385

Resistors: geometry, connectivity and electric fields are inseparable

Chapter 6 shows why a resistor is not just sheet resistance multiplied by drawn length/width. It has dimension biases, contact crowding, end effects, corner effects, nonlinear voltage and temperature dependence, distributed capacitance, and sometimes parasitic junctions. For a right-angle equal-width corner, the book gives 0.5587 squares rather than the approximate half-square mnemonic. This small difference matters if the algorithm uses many corners to obtain a precision ratio. Dogbone heads do not inherently make two devices match better: identical systematic head contributions can cancel, while unmatched heads cannot. PDF pp. 279–295

Thermoelectrics are a connectivity test, not a centroid test

Equation 8.34 is ET = S ΔTC, where S is the contact Seebeck coefficient and ΔTC is the temperature difference between a segment's contacts. Figure 8.22, visually checked here, contrasts connections that add these voltages with connections that largely cancel them. The engine should trace the ordered path through each series chain and accumulate signed thermoelectric sources. This is separate from matching the average resistance under a temperature field. Close placement of serpentine heads reduces endpoint temperature difference, but their orientation must also address mask-alignment sensitivity. PDF pp. 401–402

Material and trim decisions. Thin-film, poly, diffusion, well, metal and pinched resistors occupy different places in the accuracy/area/power/parasitic trade space. Diffused resistors can dissipate heat more effectively through silicon, while deposited resistors avoid the same junction parasitics. Trimming can compensate a value at its calibration condition, but cannot generally remove thermal gradients, material-dependent temperature behavior, hysteresis or later drift. Preserve trim accessibility, programming-current paths and exclusions when trim structures are present; component substitution or adding trim is a circuit-design choice, not a permissible placement repair. PDF pp. 295–323, 418–422

Worked example: equal shapes can have unequal electrical weights

Construct a 25 kΩ resistor from two 10 kΩ sections in series with a parallel pair of 10 kΩ sections. All four drawn strips can be identical. For either series section, ∂R/∂Ri = 1. For either member of the equal parallel pair, ∂R/∂Ri = 1/4. The sensitivity weights are therefore 1, 1, 1/4 and 1/4, not four equal weights. This is the kind of distinction behind the source's general centroid expression. PDF p. 390

Place them at x = −3, −1, +1, +3 arbitrary pitch units, with the two series sections on the left. Their ordinary geometric mean is zero, but their sensitivity-weighted mean is (−3 − 1 + 1/4 + 3/4)/2.5 = −1.2. The array will respond as though its effective resistance were displaced toward the series sections under a field producing equal local absolute resistance changes. Pair the two series sections symmetrically and the two parallel sections symmetrically to remove this first-order displacement.

This calculation teaches two implementation requirements. First, the engine must know how the units compose electrically; a bag of rectangles cannot determine the weights. Second, the weights depend on the perturbation. If local changes are fractional and unequal nominal units are used, include each nominal resistance in the sensitivity. Document that distinction rather than choosing one universal centroid formula for all networks.

Worked example: segment-count sensitivity in a ratioed pair

Consider a desired 10 kΩ : 15 kΩ pair. One construction uses two and three identical 5 kΩ units. Another uses three 3 kΩ units plus one 1 kΩ partial unit for the first resistor, and five 3 kΩ units for the second. Suppose, only to illustrate sensitivity, that each segment contributes an extra 10 Ω from its ends and contacts.

The identical-unit option adds 20 Ω to 10 kΩ and 30 Ω to 15 kΩ. Those contributions scale with the intended ratio, so the ratio is unchanged in this simple model. The partial-unit option adds 40 Ω and 50 Ω. Its first-order fractional ratio change is 40/10000 − 50/15000 ≈ 0.000667, or 0.0667%. A nominally accurate drawn length did not protect the ratio against the same small end effect.

In practice, head corrections need not be identical for short and long sections, and resistance has temperature and voltage dependence. Search candidate unit values and compositions; compute sensitivities to width, sheet resistance, heads, contacts and route contributions; then compare area, number of contacts, required dummies and CC quality. This is the practical use of the book's segmentation-sensitivity discussion. PDF pp. 394–399

A generation and verification procedure

  1. Choose only allowed materials and variants. Filter by voltage coefficient, power, parasitics and available characterization before optimizing footprint.
  2. Enumerate a modest set of widths and segment compositions. Preserve schematic value and topology; reject candidates outside model or PCell limits.
  3. Compute electrical sensitivities and moments. Include partial segments and series/parallel connections. Use these to form balanced local subarrays.
  4. Generate boundary conditions. Add qualified dummies, body contacts, field plates or exclusions and record their electrical ties. Match active environments, not merely labels.
  5. Route according to the network function. Preserve thermoelectric cancellation, ratio-scaled metal contributions and any Kelvin boundary. Avoid asymmetrical over-body coverage.
  6. Extract the complete network. Include bodies, shields, contact/via resistance and distributed capacitance. Verify value, ratio, voltage dependence, settling and power.
  7. Evaluate temperature and package scenarios. Compare uniform heating, a directed gradient and a nearby heater. Retain the best feasible tradeoffs rather than a single heuristic score.

Discriminating validation. Keep the resistor bodies fixed while changing only interconnect polarity, one via stack, an overlying metal plate or the body bias. A good checker must show which change affects thermoelectric voltage, series resistance, passivation, capacitance or conductivity modulation. Conversely, adding decorative geometry outside all characterized interaction regions should not change an electrical centroid. These checks guard against confusing physical effects with arbitrary footprint measures.

Uncertainty. When material coefficients or package fields are missing, report a relative geometric comparison with an explicit uncalibrated budget. Do not turn the textbook's qualitative “moderate” or “exceptional” grades into guaranteed ppm accuracy. The source itself warns that prediction is difficult without quantitative matching data.
