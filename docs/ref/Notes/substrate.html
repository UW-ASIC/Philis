Substrate Noise: Analysis and Optimization for IC Design

Edoardo Charbon, Ranjit Gharpurey, Paolo Miliozzi, Robert G. Meyer and Alberto Sangiovanni-Vincentelli · 2001 print / 2003 ebook

Noise transport, contact networks, isolation, sensitivity and measurement.

Open supplied PDF · 194 physical pages · File identity and coverage

Engine conclusion. Substrate isolation is an electrical transfer problem involving injectors, receivers, the complete contact network and package return paths. Minimum distance and the presence of a guard ring are useful construction constraints, but neither proves adequate isolation. A justified placement action reduces a named circuit impairment—offset, jitter, gain error, spur, noise figure or instability—over specified activity, frequency, process and package conditions.

Edoardo Charbon, Ranjit Gharpurey, Paolo Miliozzi, Robert G. Meyer and Alberto Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Substrate Noise: Analysis and Optimization for IC Design, print copyright 2001; supplied electronic edition copyright 2003. The book provides both physical mechanisms and a complete chain from circuit sensitivities to placement constraints.

1. Represent the physical path before imposing a distance

A switching transistor can inject through source/drain junction capacitance and impact-ionization current. A well can isolate its devices when held at a quiet low-impedance potential, yet become a large capacitive injector if its potential bounces. Majority-carrier substrate taps and guard rings can similarly spread the noise of their reference connection. Passive devices also couple through their parasitic capacitance; diffusion resistors and capacitor bottom plates are not electrically invisible to the substrate. The reception mechanism determines the consequence: a MOS body voltage changes drain current through body transconductance, while a capacitive pickup drives the impedance of the receiving node. Chapter 2, PDF pp. 35–40.

Action and reason. Associate each candidate injector with injection terminals, spatial support, waveform/spectrum and operating modes; associate each receiver with body/junction terminals, input impedance and performance sensitivity. These records identify what a physical move actually changes. A generic label such as “digital” misses both quiet digital activity and noisy analog oscillators or output stages. Unknown activity is uncertainty, not a zero-amplitude source.

The complete contact network matters because injected current divides among all available return paths. Moving a tap can change voltages at distant receivers even if their separation from the original aggressor is unchanged. Include well contacts, rings, substrate ties, relevant junctions, backplate connections and their metal/package impedances. Contact depth, substrate doping profile, die boundaries and backside termination are inputs to extraction. Replacing this network with one independent resistor per aggressor-victim pair can miss the influence of third contacts. Global current paths, PDF p. 25; multi-port extraction, PDF pp. 44–49.

A useful small-signal contract is v(ω) = Zloaded(ω) iinj(ω), with receiver and return loads included consistently. For a voltage-driven test, define Hva(ω) = Vvictim/Vaggressor. The book plots “isolation” as 20 log10|H|, so more negative is better. An implementation calling positive attenuation “isolation” must state its sign convention to avoid reversing optimization. The loaded transfer, not bare contact-to-contact resistance, is the performance-relevant quantity. Definition, PDF pp. 124–126.

2. Use the least expensive valid transport model

The governing continuum equation is ∇·(σ∇Φ) + ∂[∇·(ε∇Φ)]/∂t = 0. In a homogeneous region with negligible displacement current this reduces to Laplace's equation. The relevant criterion is dielectric relaxation time ε/σ relative to signal time scales, with suitable boundary conditions: contact potentials, zero normal current on insulating boundaries, and current continuity at interfaces. The book's GHz-scale examples are historical process examples, not a universal cutoff at which every substrate is resistive. Capacitive junctions and package inductance can give the complete coupling path strong frequency dependence even if the substrate bulk itself is modeled resistively. Equations (3.1)–(3.3), PDF pp. 45–46.

3. Convert circuit specifications into local budgets

The book inserts local noise generators at sensitive nodes, then computes how their amplitude, shape and phase affect circuit performance. This separates receiver sensitivity from the later placement-dependent propagation problem. A local first-order model has ΔK ≈ JΔΠ, where Π contains process, layout, temperature and noise parameters. There are many budget allocations satisfying one performance constraint; the source selects allocations with greater implementation flexibility. Worst-case sensitivities address nonlinear dependence over a specified interval, rather than assuming one derivative at nominal covers every mode. Constraint generation, PDF pp. 70–73.

  1. Name the impairment and budget. For an oscillator use a jitter or phase-noise requirement, for an amplifier an input-referred noise or stability requirement, and for a sampled circuit an error at its sampling aperture. The reason is that identical substrate voltages can have very different consequences at different nodes.
  2. Characterize sensitivity over relevant phase and bias. The PLL example demonstrates that equal peak substrate voltage produces different jitter when its timing changes. A noise amplitude bound without a phase assumption is incomplete. Evaluate worst-case relative timing when correlation is not guaranteed.
  3. Allocate shared degradation explicitly. Summing worst-case contributions gives a conservative bound; quadrature addition requires justified stochastic independence. Coherent clock harmonics require phase-aware combination. A collection of individually passing aggressor limits can still exceed the total receiver budget.
  4. Translate the budget to geometry through a calibrated transfer model. A minimum-distance rule is justified only if that model shows sufficient attenuation over the legal geometry range. If isolation saturates before the target is reached, report infeasibility or change the isolation/return structure.
  5. Re-evaluate after routing. Wire capacitance, resistance, supply drop and well-return impedance change the relevant transfer and operating point. The PLL example couples resistance and capacitance constraints because widening reduces resistance while increasing capacitance.

Phase sensitivity and PLL example, PDF pp. 75–82. The five-step workflow is a proposed engine implementation of the book's derivation.

4. Noise signatures require realistic activity

Switching noise is an aggregate waveform, not simply a white-noise density or a sinusoid at the clock frequency. The source's SUBWAVE/SUBRES flow characterizes per-cell injection and supply-current patterns versus slew, loading and supply conditions, combines them with switching traces, and iteratively updates delays affected by noise. Fast edges, glitches and delayed hazards can create important spectral components far from the fundamental clock. The book reports two nonconvergent cases in its timing/noise iteration; convergence should therefore be checked and reported, not assumed. Chapter 4, PDF pp. 56–68.

Engine action. Keep a finite set of activity scenarios with provenance, clock relationships, transition direction and timing uncertainty. Cache injection signatures for reusable cells and expose their valid slew/load ranges. Score peak time-domain errors and selected spectral bands using the receiver's sensitivity. This is cheaper than transistor-level simulation for every move while retaining the mechanisms that determine the result. A clock-average activity factor can be adequate for some power estimates yet miss the coincident burst that dominates substrate noise.

The reference terminal deserves its own rule: evaluate Vsignal − Vreference at the circuit's actual receiver. In the source's single-ended examples, a guard ring tied to internal ground can improve a signal measured against that ground but worsen the signal measured against an external reference. Optimizing absolute voltage at one node can therefore optimize the wrong quantity. Grounding schemes, PDF pp. 139–147.

6. Noise, feedback, loss and bias are different objectives

The substrate can close a feedback loop between amplifier stages; junction capacitances and distributed resistance then change bandwidth, reverse transmission and stability. It can also shift local DC body potential, alter MOS bias current, and modulate junction capacitance. Its resistive components produce thermal noise, and substrate loss reduces passive Q and attainable matching gain. Consequently, an AC noise transfer test alone is insufficient for a high-gain or RF block. Chapter 7, PDF pp. 108–116.

Preserve capacitor terminal roles. Connecting an appropriate capacitor's bottom plate to a low-impedance reference can reduce substrate loss at a sensitive active node; swapping terminals may be legal in the schematic yet alter extracted behavior. Multi-device extraction also matters: a single capacitor's isolated substrate model can become wrong when two nearby capacitors are used differentially, because mutual bottom-plate coupling is absent from the isolated model. This is a concrete reason to extract the assembled layout rather than summing independent device models. Capacitors and model-context failure, PDF pp. 113–115.

Spurious substrate signals can be frequency-translated or rectified: clock leakage into a sampler can become offset, low-frequency noise can mix with an interferer into band, and an oscillator can be pulled by a nearby strong signal. Reserve spectral constraints and operating-phase information in the specification. Staggering switching away from a sampling aperture can be more effective than adding distance, but changes to circuit timing require circuit-level authorization and verification. ESD snapback is a separate nonlinear regime involving substrate resistance and parasitic bipolar action; the book explicitly warns that small-signal linear substrate models do not suffice there. Spurs, timing and ESD, PDF pp. 117–120.

7. Make accurate analysis affordable during search

A contact move changes limited rows and columns of the potential matrix, enabling low-rank inverse updates rather than full recomputation. The source combines Sherman–Morrison updates, local conductance sensitivities, exact recalculation and reusable templates. Its most useful abstraction is performance-weighted extraction error: spend accuracy where parasitic uncertainty changes a critical specification. Small irrelevant conductances can be omitted only after bounding their cumulative performance effect. The source also reports that local-gradient accuracy worsens for larger moves; a cached derivative has a locality of validity. Incremental evaluation, PDF pp. 86–97.

Proposed search policy. Use a fast calibrated estimate to screen moves, carrying an error bound. Re-extract when a move exceeds the model's trust region, changes topology/contacts/termination, or approaches a budget boundary. Certify final candidates with the stronger model. Recheck both predicted failures and uncertain predicted passes: testing only apparent violations risks accepting a false pass from an optimistic approximation. Include model error in the allocated budget instead of treating estimator output as exact.

The annealing convergence discussion is conditional on theoretical schedules and state-space assumptions. It does not prove that a finite Philis run finds a global optimum. Evaluate practical results with independent seeds, comparable circuit constraints, extracted performance, runtime and feasibility counts. Appendix C, PDF pp. 168–169.

8. Concrete integration with the current repository

Isolation, lines 9–36, checks edge-to-edge minimum distance. Retain it as a geometric contract generated from a calibrated isolation analysis or as a clearly marked heuristic. A passed distance predicate must not be reported as verified substrate-noise performance. Add a separate electrical result with source scenarios, model validity, package assumptions, receiver reference and achieved noise/performance margin.

Candidate construction reserves guard-ring halos around lines 542–545 and builds rings before routing around line 338. This is a useful insertion point for candidate ring types, return-net identity and access requirements; selection needs an electrical evaluation as well as geometry. A connected ring proves connectivity, not a quiet return over frequency.

rises_mc, line 22, models steady-state temperature rise through point-source superposition in a semi-infinite substrate; ThermalGradient uses device temperature difference but a distance-based trial cost. Thermal matching and electrical substrate noise share a spatial domain yet involve different equations, sources and boundary conditions. Keep them as distinct analyses and combine their impact at the circuit specification level. A common “distance from aggressor” score cannot stand in for both.

9. Acceptance experiments and required evidence

  1. Two-contact sweep: vary separation, contact area, depth, bulk profile, backside termination and frequency; verify transfer rather than only bare resistance. This establishes where distance helps and where it saturates.
  2. Third-contact perturbation: add or move a grounded/floating tap while fixing the original pair. A valid network model must capture the changed current division.
  3. Guard-ring return sweep: vary ring width, metal resistance, package inductance and reference noise, and compare injector-side, receiver-side and dual rings. Include a case where the ring worsens the receiver error.
  4. Differential symmetry experiment: begin with balanced coupling, then perturb capacitance, receiver loading, device mismatch and return geometry. Verify common-mode rejection and the resulting differential floor.
  5. Activity/phase experiment: hold peak source amplitude fixed while shifting switching relative to a sampling/oscillator event. Demonstrate why one peak-voltage rule cannot predict every circuit error.
  6. Extraction convergence: refine panels/mesh and compare exact versus incremental updates over small and large moves. Bound the error in the actual circuit metric, including accumulated pruning error.
  7. Measurement calibration: use DC contact-network tests and RF two-port structures to calibrate propagation, then use transient and on-chip frequency-translated sensors to study circuit behavior. Model the measurement floor, probe coupling and package parasitics; on-wafer ideal grounding does not establish packaged performance.

Appendix D, PDF pp. 170–182 reports systematic errors from approximate substrate profiles, contact resistance and low-level measurements, and observations of guard rings increasing measured noise. These are reasons to carry calibration uncertainty and model provenance into the engine's final acceptance report.
