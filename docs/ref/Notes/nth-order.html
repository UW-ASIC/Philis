Source companion

An Nth-order central symmetrical layout pattern for nonlinear gradients cancellation

Xin Dai, Chengming He, Hanqing Xing, Degang Chen and Randall Geiger · ISCAS 2005

Recursive higher-order cancellation and a directly testable spatial-moment certificate.

Open supplied PDF · 4 physical pages · File identity and coverage

This paper turns gradient cancellation into an algebraic property that an engine can prove for a unit pattern. The useful result is stronger than “make it symmetric”: match the spatial moments corresponding to every polynomial term you intend to cancel.

The precise claim

Dai, He, Xing, Chen, and Geiger construct a recursively symmetric layout for two identically designed devices. Starting with two units per device in a first-order pattern, the nth-order construction uses 2n units per device and cancels polynomial parameter gradients through order n under the paper's additive unit model. The superscript matters: text extraction flattens it to “2n.” The claim concerns model cancellation; it does not establish best circuit performance or a universal minimum unit count. Abstract and gradient model, PDF pp. 1–2

Action and reason. Add a moment certificate to generated matching patterns. It provides an independently checkable invariant and identifies what the pattern actually cancels. A pattern label such as “CC” or “symmetric” cannot communicate its second- or third-order properties.

Derivation suitable for implementation

p(x,y) = c + Σ₁≤a+b≤n gₐᵦ xᵃyᵇ
Mₐᵦ(D) = (1/mD) Σᵢ xᵢᵃyᵢᵇ
Δp̄ = Σ gₐᵦ [Mₐᵦ(A) − Mₐᵦ(B)]

The paper sums unit values for equal-size devices; the normalized moments above express the same result for equal counts and make ratio comparison easier to state. Every unknown coefficient gₐᵦ disappears from the mismatch if its corresponding moment difference is zero. First order needs equal x and y centroids. Second order also needs equal x², xy, and y² moments. Testing just radial distance checks x²+y² and can miss anisotropic x²−y² or xy fields. §II, Eqs. (4)–(6), additive unit model, PDF p. 2 §III, Eqs. (15)–(23), cancellation proof, PDF p. 3

Why translations are safe. Translating a degree-n monomial produces the same degree-n part plus lower-degree terms. Once the lower-degree differences already vanish, translating two lower-order patterns does not spoil their lower-order cancellation. This is the induction step behind the recursive construction; it is why arbitrary placement of the recursion centers can work within the model.

Finite units. The paper approximates a unit's area-averaged parameter by its value at one representative point because the unit is small. For large or differently shaped units, compute area-integrated moments, or show that their identical translated footprints make the relevant internal moments cancel. Do not replace electrically weighted active areas with bounding boxes.

Recursive construction and its reason

  1. Start with a verified first-order pattern. For example ABBA on a line, or the two-dimensional diagonal pair. Reason: the induction requires all first-order terms already canceled.
  2. Create a reflected copy of the previous-order pattern around a new center. Choose disjoint, legal unit positions on the manufacturing grid. Reason: paired coordinates gain a predictable sign under central reflection.
  3. For odd target order, retain device labels in the reflected copy. A homogeneous odd-degree term changes sign under reflection, so the two contributions of each device cancel.
  4. For even target order, interchange A and B in the reflected copy. An even-degree term keeps its sign, so matching it across opposite labels cancels the A−B difference.
  5. Recheck every monomial up to the target order. Reason: the proof assumes exact reflection and preserved lower-order structure; integer-grid placement, variant dimensions, or an implementation error can violate those premises.

§III recursive definition, PDF pp. 2–3 If the base uses k units per device, the construction uses k·2^(n−1); the paper's 2n count follows from k=2. Keep this distinction if Philis uses a different legal base pattern.

For ratioed devices, more than two device types, heterogeneous footprints, or different electrical sensitivities, the paper's 1:1 construction is not automatically applicable. The general moment equations still suggest a solver formulation, but that extension must be separately justified and checked.

Why ABBA is insufficient for quadratic gradients

Place A at x=−1.5,+1.5 and B at x=−0.5,+0.5 in unit-pitch coordinates. Both centroids are zero. Under p(x)=p₀+q x², the mean A−B error is q(2.25−0.25)=2q. It has perfect first-order cancellation and a nonzero second-order error.

A two-row ABBA / BAAB pattern at y=±0.5 equalizes x², xy, and y² as well as x and y. It can still respond to a cubic term such as x²y. This illustrates why each claim should name the highest verified order rather than call the pattern “gradient-free.” These examples are our algebraic checks, not silicon results.

What cancellation does not guarantee

Nonlinear device response. If oxide thickness varies linearly but capacitance is proportional to its reciprocal, capacitance itself contains higher-order terms. Cancellation in the thickness field does not prove exact capacitance matching. Likewise, a linear threshold-voltage field passed through a nonlinear current law can leave residual current mismatch.

Local discontinuities and LDE. A well edge, active edge, hot spot, or abrupt fill boundary need not resemble a low-degree polynomial across the array. Higher-order symmetry does not automatically equalize these environments. Use the review's LDE analysis and Hastings alongside the moment certificate.

Random mismatch. The proof removes deterministic polynomial mismatch. It does not remove uncorrelated unit variation. At fixed total area, increasing unit count does not necessarily improve the random mismatch floor.

Routing and size. Each order doubles units in this construction. More contacts, edges, channels, vias, and internal routing can degrade noise, bandwidth, area, and even systematic matching. Practical implementation and acceptance tests

Proposed data. A generated pattern should expose its unit coordinates, electrical weights, orientation classes, parent-device map, exact ratios, and certified moments. Put the certificate at the cell generator boundary so cell collapse retains what CentroidGroup cannot infer from macro centers. Make the cancellation order a chosen capability, not an inferred promise from a pattern name.

Proposed search policy. First evaluate lower-order, compact, routable patterns. Add higher-order alternatives only when a characterized nonlinear field or a measured sensitivity sweep shows a benefit worth their implementation cost. This follows directly from the exponential unit count and the competing route penalties.

Certificate algorithm. Accumulate monomial sums separately for each device, then compare cross-multiplied normalized sums to avoid dividing by unit counts. Use translated local coordinates to reduce numeric growth. For integral grid coordinates, wide integers or arbitrary-precision arithmetic can establish exact low-order equalities; explicitly bound overflow. Electrical weights derived from a simulator require tolerances and uncertainty, so label those numerical certificates differently.

  1. Reproduce ABBA's zero x/y moments and nonzero x² difference.
  2. Check every xᵃyᵇ with 1≤a+b≤n for each claimed order, including mixed terms.
  3. Translate and rotate patterns, then repeat the checks; preserve terminal and orientation semantics.
  4. Move one unit by one grid step and verify a previously canceled mode becomes detectable.
  5. Generate legal contacts and routes, extract the circuit, and compare offset/ratio error versus lower-order alternatives at equal total device area and at separately reported equal unit geometry.

These tests address the actual theorem and its physical usefulness. Testing that a generator reproduces its own string template would not independently validate either.
