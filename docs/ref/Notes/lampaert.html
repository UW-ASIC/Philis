Analog Layout Generation for Performance and Manufacturability

Koen Lampaert, Georges Gielen and Willy Sansen · 1999

Performance budgets, module generation, placement, routing and defect-aware design.

Open supplied PDF · 185 physical pages · File identity and coverage

Optimal means electrically feasible, then efficient to manufacture

Source: Koen Lampaert, Georges Gielen, and Willy Sansen, Analog Layout Generation for Performance and Manufacturability (1999). The valuable contribution is the complete chain from circuit specifications to geometry, parasitic estimates, sensitivity-weighted performance changes, placement, routing, and manufacturing objectives. A small layout or short net is a means to that end, not the definition of success.

The book argues for optimizing the combined electrical consequences of interconnect, mismatch, and temperature. It explicitly criticizes converting the circuit specification into one fixed allocation of independent parasitic limits: a different allocation can produce an acceptable circuit that the original limits would reject. This is directly relevant to Philis’s per-net length budgets. A length limit is a useful implementable contract, but it is not, by itself, an electrical specification. PDF pp. 41–42

Interpretation limit. The book sometimes calls its output “guaranteed” performance-correct and says impossible specifications can be flagged without iteration. Its working model uses approximate extraction, first-order electrical sensitivities, and finite heuristic search. Those methods justify a prediction within a model; they do not establish global physical feasibility or a proof of infeasibility. Philis should report model feasibility, extracted-circuit feasibility, and search exhaustion separately.

Reading coverage and chapter map

This note follows substantive material in every chapter. Detailed reading covered the performance formulation and sensitivities, module alternatives, placement representations and constraint handling, parasitic estimation, routing cost/scheduling, defect models, implementation interfaces, and conclusions. Historical tool surveys and several implementation derivations were surveyed rather than exhaustively reconstructed. Numerical tables are historical demonstrations, not reproduced experiments. 1. Express layout quality through remaining circuit-performance margin

Source finding. A performance quantity P has acceptable interval [Pmin, Pmax]. After considering process variation, the pre-layout design occupies [Pproc,min, Pproc,max]. Under the book’s additive layout-shift approximation, the allowed shift is the difference between those intervals:

Pmin − Pproc,min ≤ ΔPlayout ≤ Pmax − Pproc,max.

For example, if phase margin must exceed 60° and the least favorable pre-layout process result is 64°, the nominal layout has at most 4° of degradation under that approximation. A 10° nominal surplus at the typical process point is not the available robust margin. If parasitic effects vary with corner, evaluate the shift at each corner; subtracting one corner-independent shift from an interval is then insufficient. PDF pp. 34–37

Proposed action and reason. Introduce performance contracts identifying metric, units, lower/upper limits, analysis, load, stimulus, PVT/operating conditions, baseline result, and reserved uncertainty. Keep hard geometric legality independent. Associate each proxy budget with the contract it approximates and its derivation, because otherwise a placer can optimize a number without knowing whether it protects phase margin, settling, offset, or nothing measurable.

Tradeoff and validation. Circuit evaluation is expensive and some specifications are discontinuous near failure. Start with a small set of stable metrics and compare proxy-predicted margin against extracted simulation over deliberate placement and routing perturbations. Record false-feasible and false-infeasible decisions separately; a good average error does not establish trustworthy acceptance.

The existing Requirements definition already separates hard, budget, and cost tiers. Preserve that separation. ParasiticBudget checks drawn length; although its generic extraction helper contains a placeholder, the active annotator path constructs per-net length limits from class/load-derived capacitance allowances and process wire capacitance. That is useful physical guidance. The remaining gap is a traceable circuit-response constraint and validated stack/topology assumptions, rather than the mere absence of budget generation. Label heuristic length bounds by their calibration and validity.

2. Compute signed, scaled sensitivities and know when they stop being valid

Source finding. The inner loop estimates geometry-dependent parasitics and applies a first-order performance model. In generalized notation, with p the parasitic vector and a reference p0:

ΔPj ≈ Σi Sji (pi − p0i),   Sji = ∂Pj/∂pi.

The source writes the zero-reference special case. The sign matters: capacitance can worsen one metric and improve another, and coupling depends on the two connected nodes. A single positive “criticality” scalar cannot represent this entire matrix. The book reviews perturbation, direct sensitivity and adjoint sensitivity; it implements perturbation because of simulator availability. Finite differences trade truncation error against solver noise and require an appropriate perturbation size. Adjoint efficiency comes from many parameters sharing a scalar response; generally each independent scalar response needs its own adjoint solve, rather than accepting the book’s broad wording that two solves cover arbitrary numbers of responses. PDF pp. 42–45

A dimensionless two-sided violation can be implemented as the following proposed normalization, not as an equation copied literally from the source:

vj = max(0, (Lj − P̂j)/sj, (P̂j − Uj)/sj),   sj > 0.

L and U are specification bounds; s is an explicit engineering scale in the same units. The source’s §4.9, Eq. (4.11), uses the same continuous piecewise bound violation without the proposed per-metric scale. Its upper branch correctly subtracts the upper degradation bound; the earlier edition of these notes misread that superscript. The scale here is an additional normalization, not a source erratum. Do not mix MHz, degrees, and millivolts by an unqualified sum. PDF p. 107

Proposed action and reason. Cache sensitivities by circuit, PVT, device variant, operating point and parasitic reference. Refresh after a material sizing or topology change, after prediction error grows, or after leaving a calibrated neighborhood. Use central finite differences where feasible, compare two perturbation sizes, and retain the estimated numerical error. A speed advantage is valuable only if the model ranks moves reliably.

Tradeoff and validation. Refreshing too often erases the speed benefit; refreshing too rarely creates confidently wrong rankings. Benchmark rank agreement and feasibility error, not only regression error. Evaluate several nearby variants with actual extraction and simulation, including a case where a compensation-node capacitance changes the dominant pole. In Philis, global placement lines 205–221 differentiate geometric rule costs; that is not yet a circuit-performance sensitivity calculation.

3. Make primitive alternatives electrically meaningful

Source finding. Module generation returns several realizations of the same electrical intent; placement selects an aspect ratio, orientation and implementation. The source compares fixed procedural subcircuits, dynamic diffusion merging, and precomputed alternative partitions. Fixed generators encode expert structures and reduce search, but miss unanticipated sharing. Dynamic merging explores more choices but complicates legality and runtime. Some common-centroid and interdigitated cascode structures still need dedicated generators. Junction capacitance depends on source/drain area and perimeter, so diffusion sharing changes performance as well as area. PDF pp. 66–74

Proposed action and reason. Store, per variant, legal orientations, terminal polygons, source/drain area and perimeter, internal metal/via parasitics, matching environment, bulk domain and accessibility. Compare variants after adding their internal electrical consequences to external routing estimates. Without those fields, a compact variant can win solely because capacitance or pin-access difficulty has disappeared from the score.

Assumptions and tradeoff. Shared diffusion requires compatible device/process type, connectivity, bulk potential and foundry geometry rules. The book’s same-net/layer/bulk overlap conditions are a conceptual minimum, not a modern signoff rule deck. Enumerating all partitions can explode combinatorially; retain an electrical/area/access Pareto subset and permit selected coupled variant changes.

Validation. Extract two variants with equal intended W/L and compare junction geometry, terminal connectivity, pin accessibility and the most sensitive circuit metrics. Verify that legal same-net sharing improves the appropriate extracted capacitance and that a similar-looking incompatible merge is rejected.

The source separates a fast generator interface mode from detailed layout generation and puts process layers/rules in technology data. It also synchronizes generators with simulation models. For Philis this argues for one authoritative variant descriptor consumed by placement, rendering, extraction and verification, with an explicit convention for parasitics already included in device models. Otherwise back-annotation can double-count them. PDF pp. 73–74, PDF pp. 167–168

4. Preserve exact intent in moves; estimate actual routing demand

Source finding. Position, orientation and shape constraints are different relationships. LAYLA uses separate move groups, allowing a position-symmetric pair to carry its own orientation rule and shape coupling. Symmetric translation, pair swapping, pair flipping, and moving the symmetry axis preserve relationships by construction. Same orientation for matching devices and mirrored orientation for geometric symmetry are explicitly different. The desired electrical correspondence must decide which one applies. PDF pp. 99–105

Proposed action and reason. Preserve hard symmetry and matched variant constraints in coordinated moves, and maintain a compatible terminal correspondence. Check that constraints intersect consistently before optimization. A low scalar cost cannot justify breaking an exact constraint, and independent edits followed by repeated repair can waste time or oscillate.

Tradeoff and validation. Restricting moves can disconnect the search space or trap a useful ordering. Include whole-group translation, axis motion, variant replacement and pair-order changes, then verify reachability on small enumerated examples. Use cases with self-symmetric tails, unequal pair sizes, multiple axes and equal-current-direction requirements. Philis already has SymmetryGroup emission around lines 113–130 and detailed placement with legality-first acceptance; the research supports extending those contracts rather than replacing them with one penalty weight.

The book’s free-coordinate representation allows useful sharing but also illegal overlap; its claim that this representation is best is a historical engineering choice, not a theorem. Later topological methods in the companion survey remove much illegal search. Whichever representation is used, a terminal is an accessible region, not merely the device center. The book specifically uses terminal-edge Manhattan distances and geometric net approximations to estimate capacitance and coupling. PDF pp. 95–99, PDF pp. 108–112

Proposed action and reason. Reserve directional space for pin escape, high-current widths, shielding and passing nets; update it using coarse routes. The mechanism is simple: a dense placement can require a long detour or become unroutable, so area saved before routing is not necessarily area saved afterward. The source’s static-plus-dynamic halos are a useful starting abstraction.

Tradeoff and validation. Larger halos reduce density and can overconstrain placement. Feed measured global-route congestion back into selected congested boundaries and compare final routed area, detour, opens and electrical margin. Do not validate this change with HPWL alone. The book itself attributes a remaining 10–15% historical area penalty relative to manual layouts largely to separated placement/routing and conservative space reservation. PDF p. 116, PDF p. 172

5. Distinguish random mismatch, systematic environment and thermal response

Source finding. The reviewed pair model is σ²(Δq) = Aq²/(WL) + Sq²D², with process-calibrated constants, device area WL and separation D. It describes a particular decomposition into local random variation and distance-related variation; it is not a universal device law. The book also requires compatible structure, shape, finger count, orientation, local surroundings and temperature. Common-centroid organization targets spatial gradients; it does not eliminate the finite-area random term. PDF pp. 53–55

Its placement mismatch cost sums absolute sensitivities times three-sigma parameter deviations. That is a conservative aggregation, not the same quantity as the standard deviation of a combined circuit response. For statistically modeled parameters, the appropriate linear covariance propagation is σ²(P) ≈ SΣSᵀ. An arithmetic sum of “three sigma” contributions has no universal three-sigma yield interpretation. The book’s area/distance separation is helpful for responsibility boundaries, but a square root of summed variances is not generally additive in area and distance. PDF pp. 112–113

Proposed action and reason. Annotate a matching rule with its protected response, process model and systematic mechanism. Retain exact common structure/orientation/environment requirements where justified; optimize proximity only against the part of error it can influence. For thermal matching, evaluate device temperatures from power sources and their thermal transfer rather than treating Euclidean closeness as proof of equal temperature.

Assumptions and validation. Thermal superposition assumes an adequately linear thermal model with known stack and boundary conditions; power can itself depend on temperature. Calibrate a fast kernel against a more detailed solution and validate temperature differences at sensitive pairs as well as maximum temperature. Compare two equidistant pairs placed along versus across a thermal gradient. Update the field after materially changing sources or placement. Philis’s emitted thermal budget uses a tuning default at lines 37–40; its number should ultimately come from response sensitivity and technology, not from the book’s examples.

Substrate isolation is likewise technology-dependent: spacing can be effective in one substrate and weak in another; guard-ring performance depends on contact location and return impedance. The book introduces these mechanisms but does not supply a universal foundry rule. Its frequency examples and broad temperature/reliability rules should not be carried into modern PDK defaults. PDF pp. 59–64

6. Route with segment physics and a shared performance budget

Source finding. Routing evaluates segment resistance R = R□,layerℓ/w and overlap, lateral and fringe capacitances. Here R□ is sheet resistance, ℓ and w use identical length units, and via/contact resistance needs separate treatment. Different portions of a branched net can carry different currents; width belongs to the segment. The book sizes widths from current per allowed current-per-width and sizes via arrays from via current capability. This is a physical reason to preserve branch currents, not a justification for applying one nominal DC limit to every waveform and temperature. PDF p. 143, PDF pp. 147–148

Proposed action and reason. Add layer/width/via-aware R/C and coupling estimates to incremental routing costs, with a circuit-level budget evaluator at route acceptance. Wider wires reduce R but can add C and congestion; shielding can reduce aggressor coupling while increasing ground capacitance. The right decision depends on the sensitivity matrix and actual available margin.

Tradeoff and validation. Incremental estimates require a consistent ownership rule for shared coupling terms, or a net pair’s capacitance can be counted twice. Compare complete-route recomputation with incremental updates after insertion, rip-up and width changes. Use two equal-length routes on different layers, two equal-length routes with different neighbors, and a multi-terminal tree with changing branch currents.

LAYLA preroutes nets independently, estimates their performance impact, preserves critical routes and reroutes less influential nets to remove conflicts. This suggests an urgency based on remaining performance margin and avoidable damage, not a permanent ordering by fanout or wirelength. In Philis, route_net (around lines 635–710) already combines base path cost, congestion history, present overuse and a penalty field; electrical price fields can enter there, but final acceptance must still evaluate the coupled state. PDF pp. 150–152

Search caveat. The source discusses A* with a lower-bound predictor. A geometric lower bound is not automatically an admissible bound on a signed, coupled performance objective. Negative incremental sensitivities or route-history-dependent coupling can also invalidate ordinary shortest-path assumptions. Use a nonnegative surrogate for path generation plus full-state electrical acceptance, or explicitly implement the richer search state and prove the required cost properties. Similarly, pruning a partial path for exceeding a budget is safe only when all completions cannot restore feasibility.

Symmetric routing should compare electrical consequences and surroundings, including layers, vias, endpoints and nearby conductors. Mirroring centerlines is useful but not sufficient when surrounding objects are asymmetric. Where exact mirroring is impossible, expose an electrical matching budget and document the permitted escape rather than silently accepting a large geometric penalty. PDF pp. 148–150

7. Spend justified margin on manufacturability and testability

Source finding. The source separates local defect faults from parametric variation. For a defect diameter χ bridging two long parallel conductors of overlap length L and spacing s, its simplified critical area is A(χ) = L·max(0, χ−s). Combining this with a defect-size density D(χ) gives expected fault count λ = ∫A(χ)D(χ)dχ; under the stated Poisson assumptions, the no-fault probability is exp(−λ). These formulas require calibrated defect mechanisms and geometrical approximations. They are not equivalent to mismatch yield. PDF pp. 153–157

The manufacturability phase protects the performance limits while reducing defect exposure and preferentially avoiding difficult-to-detect bridging faults. The testability weighting uses separation of good/faulty response distributions. An inverse statistical distance is an optimization proxy, not itself a probability that a fault escapes testing. Nor does accepting a statistical hypothesis establish the posterior probability that an individual manufactured circuit is good. PDF pp. 158–160

Proposed action and reason. Once extracted electrical specifications and required guard margins pass, explore spacing, route arrangement and via redundancy using calibrated reliability/manufacturing objectives. Preserve performance as a constraint. This recognizes that many electrically equivalent layouts differ in production quality.

Tradeoff and validation. Without foundry defect data and a production test model, report geometry-based exposure only; do not label it yield. Benchmark exposure changes and any added area/C/R. Use fault injection with the intended tests before claiming improved test coverage. Keep deterministic DRC, electrical yield, defect yield and escaped-fault risk as separate outputs.

Engine work justified by this book, in dependency order

  1. Make performance contracts and budget provenance explicit. This gives each existing heuristic a falsifiable purpose; validate with a pre-layout and extracted reference circuit.
  2. Characterize variants and routing parasitics consistently. Electrical feedback is meaningless if internal parasitics are omitted or double-counted; validate with standalone primitive extraction.
  3. Connect signed sensitivities to shared circuit budgets. This recovers valid tradeoffs excluded by fixed per-net bounds; validate prediction errors near specification boundaries.
  4. Feed routing congestion and extracted error back to placement. This repairs the separated-stage weakness identified in the conclusions; validate final routed area and performance across multiple seeds.
  5. Add manufacturing/testability optimization only when its inputs exist. Otherwise a numerically precise score would imply knowledge the engine does not possess.

The strongest success criterion is a reproducible, electrically verified feasible layout with measured tradeoffs and known model limits. This book supplies the architecture of that objective, while modern PDK data, extraction, statistical models and independent verification must supply its calibration.
