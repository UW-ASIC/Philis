Signal routing, symmetry and shielding

Control sensitive-node loading, differential imbalance, crosstalk, Kelvin paths and returns.

Analog signal routing preserves small voltages, charge, ratios, phase, and impedance. The constraints should describe those electrical needs, while the router chooses realizable geometry that meets them with acceptable area and congestion.

Define the routing contract per net and per relationship

A useful net description includes terminal roles, expected signal range and spectrum, DC and transient current, source/load impedance, allowed R/C, permitted layers, shielding needs, and related nets. Relationships include differential partners, a Kelvin sense and its force path, an aggressor/victim pair, and the separate plates of a capacitor array. Net classification is a starting point; the circuit condition sets the actual budget.

Why. A high-impedance bias node and a low-impedance output can have similar geometry but very different sensitivity to the same injected charge. A clock's edge rate can matter more than its repetition frequency. A long quiet route may be safer than a short route alongside a fast switching net.

The performance survey describes symmetry, exact per-layer route matching, critical-net shielding, and current-flow constraints as useful techniques, while warning that geometric constraints alone do not guarantee circuit performance. Routing survey, PDF p. 2

Route scarce electrical resources deliberately

  1. Reserve critical pin access and protected device regions. If every legal escape is consumed, a nominally good floorplan becomes unroutable.
  2. Reserve supplies, quiet returns, and guard/shield connections. Signal protection depends on these networks being physically realizable.
  3. Route constrained pairs or bundles with coupled decisions. Their layer/width/topology choices are related, so independent shortest paths can make later matching expensive.
  4. Route remaining nets with congestion negotiation. Keep analog costs tied to measured budget use rather than one unexplained global penalty.
  5. Extract and repair the limiting electrical violations. A successful DRC route can still exceed a sensitive-node budget.

This is a policy template, not an unconditional ordering. A large supply mesh or fixed external pin may require a different schedule. The reason for each reservation should be the scarcity or sensitivity it protects.

Differential matching: corresponding paths and surroundings

A common-mode disturbance becomes differential error when the two sides respond differently. Geometrically mirroring the routes helps make their electrical models alike, but only if layers, widths, vias, loading, and nearby conductors also correspond. A matched pair whose routes see different clocks is not electrically symmetric.

Constraints. Preserve terminal correspondence; choose compatible layer/width sequences; match contact/via strategies; constrain extracted resistance and capacitance imbalance; and compare coupling from each significant aggressor to both sides. Derive tolerances from offset, CMRR, settling, or timing requirements.

Technique. Route a template and its reflected partner when obstacles permit. Otherwise search paired states or maintain a shared electrical budget while allowing asymmetrical geometry. Re-extract both sides after a repair. Matching only the set of layers misses a case where one side spends most of its length on a resistive layer and the other on a low-resistance layer.

Counterexample. Two 100 µm routes can use the same two metals yet have different allocations, via stacks, widths, and neighbors. Their total lengths and layer sets match while their terminal RC does not. Philis's current Differential predicate cannot distinguish all such cases.

Translate crosstalk into an electrical budget

The following estimates are derived here from the linear capacitor law and charge conservation. For a simple coupling capacitor Cc, injected current is i=Cc·d(Vaggressor−Vvictim)/dt. If a fast transition couples into a nearly floating victim with capacitance Cv to quiet reference nodes, excluding the coupling capacitance Cc, the instantaneous charge-sharing estimate is ΔVvictim≈Cc/(Cv+Cc)·ΔVaggressor. For the instantaneous step, conservation of victim charge gives Cv·ΔVvictim+Cc(ΔVvictim−ΔVaggressor)=0, which yields the stated ratio. This is an illustrative limit, not a universal noise formula: driver impedance, nonlinear clamps, finite edge rate, other couplings, and circuit dynamics alter the response.

For frequency-domain small-signal analysis, the victim response depends on both the coupling and the victim impedance. A constraint on minimum spacing is therefore an indirect way to reduce Cc; a noise constraint needs aggressor waveforms or spectra and a transfer model. Multiple aggressors can add coherently or partly cancel depending on phase and correlation. Do not sum their powers as though they were independent unless that assumption is justified.

Example. With illustrative Cc=1 fF, Cv=19 fF, and a 1 V fast edge, the simple charge-sharing estimate is 50 mV. The same geometry connected to a low-impedance node can produce a much smaller voltage excursion. The victim's electrical role changes the allowable coupling by orders of magnitude.

Router actions. Increase separation on important parallel runs, shorten overlap, change layers when the stack model supports improvement, move the aggressor, or add a correctly referenced shield. Choose based on estimated noise reduction per routing resource and confirm by extraction/simulation.

A shield is a connected conductor with costs

A grounded or otherwise quiet shield can intercept electric-field coupling. Its own impedance determines how quiet it remains. A floating shield can transfer charge; a shield tied through a long inductive or resistive path can carry noise; a shield connected to a noisy rail can become an aggressor. The label “shield” provides no electrical guarantee.

Constraints. Name the reference net, preserve continuity, bound connection impedance over the relevant spectrum, respect legal spacing, and account for added victim-to-shield capacitance. A differential pair may require balanced shield geometry. The substrate chapter explains analogous dependence on guard-ring and package return impedance.

Tradeoff. A shield can lower crosstalk while increasing victim loading and consuming a track. At a sensitive pole this can reduce bandwidth. It may also change density or the over-device environment. Do not impose blanket shielding on every sensitive device; the device-specific chapters explain when metal coverage itself can affect matching.

Enforcement. Treat the shield and its tie points as routable objects with connectivity checks and extraction. Reserve the resource before detailed routing; an annotation that merely raises spacing cost does not construct a shield.

Route topology controls voltage distribution

A chain, star, tree, and mesh distribute resistance differently. A source connection shared by many transistor fingers accumulates branch currents toward the common node; moving that node changes local source voltages. A capacitor-bank trunk can make the far units settle later. A bias tree with unequal shared impedance can couple nominally independent blocks.

Constraint. Bound terminal voltage differences or path transfer errors rather than minimizing only total wire length. Technique. Choose balanced trees, local stars, fishbone structures, multiple access points, or parallel routes according to the current distribution. Reason. These structures control shared impedance and which currents flow through each segment. Limit. A geometrically balanced tree does not have equal delays if its loads differ.

The CC review explicitly analyzes the small-signal source currents and local AC-ground location in different array patterns. This is why internal macro routing belongs in the same electrical model as global routing. Source-resistance analysis, PDF pp. 6–7

Special handling for high-impedance and charge-storage nodes

High-impedance nodes are sensitive to capacitive loading, leakage, and injected charge. Sample-and-hold nodes and capacitor top plates can also be sensitive to dielectric absorption, parasitic voltage dependence, and switching feedthrough. Minimize unnecessary metal exposure and identify every nearby switching conductor. Keep test structures, fill, and access stubs in the model.

Technique. Prefer direct routes with controlled surroundings, protected layers where justified, short device access, and deliberate reference conductors. Constrain parasitic ratios for charge-domain networks rather than demanding equal absolute parasitics on unequal intended capacitances. Use extraction to distinguish top/bottom plate relationships. The DAC chapter develops these rules in detail. Capacitor terminal parasitics, PDF pp. 2–4

For precision sense connections, separate force and sense paths where the circuit architecture requires Kelvin behavior. The sense route should not include uncontrolled voltage drop from the force current. This is a connectivity/topology requirement, not merely a request for a wider wire; see power and reference distribution.

Put the right information into route costs

Search costs can combine congestion, length, via use, estimated R/C, coupling, and budget criticality. Normalize physical residuals and retain their identities. A route that is short but consumes all of a critical capacitance budget should have a different marginal cost from an equally long insensitive rail.

A local edge cost is only an approximation when an objective depends on a whole route graph, pair matching, or aggregate coupling. Maintain route-level state and reevaluate completed candidates. If an optimization introduces signed sensitivity rewards, do not feed negative edges into an algorithm that assumes nonnegative edge costs without changing the algorithm or proving an equivalent safe formulation.

Constraint interaction. Width changes spacing and congestion. A new layer adds vias. A shield adds capacitance and a return route. A matched detour changes both sides' performance. Price these consequences or reserve them explicitly; treating each repair as independent can make the engine oscillate between violations.

Philis additions and acceptance tests

The current global router and detailed router already have negotiation and targeted repair mechanisms. Extend their measured state with terminal RC, corresponding-path signatures, per-layer coupling models, and explicit shield connectivity. Preserve the existing same-layer spacing and aggregate-coupling checks as useful low-cost screens, with their physical scope stated.

Test a length-matched but RC-mismatched pair; two individually acceptable aggressors that together exceed the victim budget; a floating versus well-tied shield; a layer change that increases via resistance; unequal loads on a symmetric tree; and an internal macro route that dominates the external route. Simulate the corresponding circuit error after each repair. A useful new constraint should distinguish a known harmful layout from a harmless one without systematically rejecting valid alternatives.
