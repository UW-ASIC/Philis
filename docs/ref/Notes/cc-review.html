Common-Centroid Layout for Active and Passive Devices: A Review and the Road Ahead

Nibedita Karmokar, Meghna Madhusudan, Arvind K. Sharma, Ramesh Harjani, Mark Po-Hung Lin and Sachin S. Sapatnekar · ASP-DAC 2022, pp. 114–121

Gradient matching versus LDE, random variation and routed electrical performance.

Open supplied PDF · 8 physical pages · File identity and coverage

A common centroid is one useful property of a layout. Its value depends on which variations matter, how the units connect, and whether their physical environments match. This review supplies the strongest argument in the corpus for choosing layout patterns using circuit performance.

What this source establishes

Karmokar and coauthors survey capacitor and transistor common-centroid (CC) methods, then examine cases where clustered or interdigitated patterns perform better. Their argument is physical: CC removes a particular systematic error term, while potentially introducing resistance imbalance and layout-dependent device shifts. The review is evidence for a conditional pattern choice, not for either a universal CC rule or a universal rejection of CC. Introduction, PDF p. 1 §V, PDF pp. 6–7

Engine action and reason. Generate feasible clustered, interdigitated, and CC alternatives for matched structures. Evaluate the extracted circuit and the relevant variation scenarios for each. Retaining alternatives matters because a placement constraint cannot predict which error dominates every device size, bias point, or process.

The cancellation argument, including its assumptions

Let an equal unit of device A or B sample a parameter field p(x,y)=p₀+gₓx+gᵧy. The mean parameter difference is gₓ(x̄A−x̄B)+gᵧ(ȳA−ȳB). Coincident centroids remove this term for every gradient direction. To translate parameter matching into output matching, the review additionally linearizes performance as ΔP≈(∂P/∂p)Δp. These are two distinct approximations: spatial variation and circuit response. Equations (1)–(5), PDF p. 1

Why the engine needs unit metadata. A macro's center is not the centroid of its electrically contributing units. Store each unit's active region, electrical weight, parent device, orientation, and connection. For unequal units, use weights derived from the quantity being matched; metal padding or a large guard-ring box is not electrical weight. For a ratioed capacitor bank, normalize each member's sum by its nominal capacitance before comparing moments. Merely aligning the centers of the whole A and B boxes does not prove the required cancellation.

Scope. Zero first moments do not imply zero quadratic moments. Equal moments of threshold voltage need not imply equal drain current if units experience different bias or sensitivities. A geometrically symmetric arrangement need not match well-edge distance. Conversely, the defining centroid equations alone do not require every possible mirror symmetry: distinguish the algebraic condition from a particular symmetric pattern family.

Check. Apply synthetic x and y gradients directly to the unit devices and sum their modeled contributions. A valid CC pattern must reject both linear modes. Test after rotation, routing, dummy insertion, and device-to-cell collapse so later transformations cannot silently invalidate the property.

Random variation requires covariance, not a dispersion slogan

The review separates global, local spatially correlated, and local uncorrelated variation. Its correlation models and mismatch coefficients are process parameters. Layout can change correlated differential error; rearranging identical independent random units at fixed total active area does not make their intrinsic randomness disappear. Global variation cancels in a difference only to the extent that the compared devices and circuit sensitivities respond alike. §II, PDF pp. 2–3

Proposed general implementation:
e = dᵀδp
Var(e) = dᵀΣd
d = normalized electrical contribution of A minus that of B;
Σᵢⱼ = covariance of the corresponding unit parameters.

This is our covariance-algebra reformulation of the review's sums: for zero-mean perturbations, E[(dᵀδp)²]=dᵀE[δpδpᵀ]d=dᵀΣd. It makes the reason for dispersion measurable. Mixing members through a shared field can increase covariance between their aggregate values and reduce differential error, but dispersion can also lengthen wires. A process correlation model must say what distance unit it uses and what happens at zero separation. Covariance matrices must remain positive semidefinite; otherwise apparently excellent mismatch numbers may come from an invalid statistical model.

Engine proposal. Keep independent mismatch, correlated variation, deterministic gradients, and model uncertainty as separate report entries. Sweep uncertain correlation lengths instead of adopting the review's illustrative long or short correlation length as a foundry fact. Compare layouts using the same random field samples so simulation noise does not masquerade as improvement.

Counterexample. Splitting a fixed active area into twice as many independent equal subdevices doubles each subdevice's variance under an inverse-area law, but averaging twice as many leaves the aggregate variance unchanged. The benefit of splitting must come from systematic cancellation, environment balance, routing, or a changed total area—not the count alone.

Philis must verify those premises before reusing that conclusion. A dummy whose geometry, layer or bias differs from the qualified construction does not establish the required boundary environment. Some qualified dummy structures are intentionally floating.

Validation. Build ABBA and two-row arrangements with the same total W/L and nominal current. First disable gradients, then enable only LDE models. This isolates whether the layout itself causes a systematic offset. Add gradients afterward to reveal the actual tradeoff.

Electrical matching includes the route graph

For transistor arrays, diffusion sharing, source connection resistance, and gate/drain access constrain viable CC patterns. The review describes half-diffusion graphs and Euler paths for constructing diffusion-sharing arrays, then routing that accounts for electromigration and IR drop. These methods provide useful internal macro representations, but capacitor placement algorithms cannot simply be applied to MOS arrays: their adjacency constraints are different. §IV, PDF pp. 5–6

A source lead carrying small-signal current creates local source degeneration. For a simple single-branch model, gₘ,eff≈gₘ/(1+gₘRₛ). This closed form is our derivation from i=gₘ(vin−iRₛ), neglecting output conductance and body effect and assuming the branch current is the source-resistor current. The review’s Eq. (25) relates effective transconductance to local source voltage; its array analysis uses the actual unit-current distribution. Wider wires or a different connection tree can improve transconductance, while unequal resistance can create offset. Equal total drawn length does not guarantee equal terminal impedance because layers, vias, branch loading, and neighbors differ. Equation (25) and Fig. 9, PDF pp. 6–7

Engine action. Compare corresponding terminal-to-terminal resistance and capacitance, including internal macro wiring. Count vias by type and track per-layer lengths as intermediate diagnostics. Use the extracted multiport circuit for acceptance. This is justified because a low-resistance trunk cannot repair an asymmetric local source connection hidden inside the macro.

Tradeoff. A detour that matches wire lengths may increase both source resistance and victim coupling. In some FinFET layers, a bend also requires a layer transition and a high-resistance via. Allow electrical matching to choose a different layer, contact pattern, topology, or device pattern instead of adding length reflexively.

Capacitor construction and routing must be designed together

The review compares MOM finger, sandwich, pillar, and mortise-tenon structures, their density, and their ability to suppress unwanted top-plate coupling. Its preference for MOM in the discussed advanced-node setting is not a universal replacement rule for an available, characterized MIM device. Unit structure determines which parasitics are exposed to the router. §III-B and Fig. 5, PDF p. 4

For a charge-scaling DAC, preserve the distinction between intended capacitance, top-to-substrate capacitance, top-to-bottom coupling, and bottom-plate loading. They affect gain, code-dependent error, power, and settling differently. Two arrays with the same total parasitic capacitance can therefore have very different INL/DNL.

Action and reason. Parameterize unit capacitance, array aspect, dispersion, route topology, and shield choices jointly. Better parasitic matching may allow smaller unit capacitors, reducing area and switching energy, but the smaller units then have a stricter relative random mismatch requirement. The unit size must satisfy both statistical and extracted circuit constraints.

The paper surveys ILP, pair-sequence search, chessboard construction, and routing-aware stochastic approaches. Retain exclusivity, exact unit ratios, channel capacity, and connectivity as explicit invariants. A visually regular pattern with no legal route is not a usable placement. §III-C, PDF pp. 4–5 See the 2023 DAC paper for a concrete constructive alternative.

A decision procedure for Philis

  1. Identify what must match. Record ratio, circuit mode, terminals, performance metric, and allowable error. Reason: two equal-sized transistors may serve different electrical roles.
  2. Generate pattern candidates with valid connectivity. Include clustered, ID, CC, and multirow CC where legal. Reason: the best choice depends on the dominant error mechanism.
  3. Reject illegal or environmentally unsupported candidates. Enforce unit count, geometry, allowed orientation, wells, and routability. Reason: a mismatch estimate on unrealizable geometry is meaningless.
  4. Evaluate deterministic LDE and routed parasitics before random variation. Reason: Monte Carlo around an already shifted mean can conceal a fixable systematic error.
  5. Evaluate gradients and covariance under the stated process model. Reason: compact clustered layouts may perform well under independent variation yet degrade under short-range correlated variation.
  6. Keep feasible nondominated alternatives. Reason: one pattern may have lower offset while another has better bandwidth or area. Select according to the circuit's actual specifications.

The review's OTA example combines CC for larger structures with a clustered small mirror; its comparator example exposes how small structures can suffer from CC parasitic/LDE mismatch. Those examples establish the existence of the tradeoff, not a size threshold transferable to SKY130. §V-B, PDF p. 7

Exact connections to the current code

CentroidGroup::centroid uses layout bounding-box areas and macro centers, and retarget replaces members with collapsed cell IDs. Preserve local unit coordinates and electrical weights so merged groups do not appear matched merely because their representatives coincide. The current placement registers MatchingPair and CentroidGroup in the cost arm. These are guidance, not a completed mismatch feasibility test.

enumerate already filters merged macros whose shared pads or gate straps would short different nets. Build on that legality filtering. Report why a candidate was rejected and retain its pattern identity; otherwise an intended CC structure can silently become separate devices and the later score loses the reason.

Differential checks relative total length and a deduplicated layer set. Extend the electrical evaluator rather than describing this existing predicate as RC matching. The required test is two routes with equal lengths and identical layer sets but different via counts or layer-length allocation: the electrical check should distinguish them.

Reading coverage and limits

Equations with damaged extraction were reconstructed only where their meaning was unambiguous; no process constants were imported.
