Verification coverage and layout certificates

Check the emitted circuit and name the scope, models and evidence behind each pass.

Verification turns a layout preference into an accountable constraint. A certificate must identify what was checked, against which model and scenarios, and which required checks could not run. A clean report from a partial checker is evidence about its covered rules, not a complete manufacturing or performance guarantee.

Different checks answer different questions

CheckIts questionWhy another check is still needed
Intent / annotationDo matched groups, net classes, voltage domains and allowed transformations correspond to the intended circuit?Correct intent can still be implemented incorrectly.
Geometry DRCDo drawn shapes satisfy the supported process geometry rules?Legal shapes can connect the wrong circuit or perform poorly.
Connectivity / LVSDoes the recognized extracted device graph match the intended graph and relevant parameters?Correct topology can violate voltages, current density or analog specifications.
ERC / reliabilityAre electrical domain, well, oxide, current and other required conditions valid?Many require scenarios and models beyond connectivity.
Parasitic extractionWhat additional physical network does the geometry produce within the extractor's scope?A network alone is not a pass/fail circuit result.
Post-layout simulationDoes that realized circuit meet its metrics for the specified modes and conditions?Nominal simulation does not establish statistical yield or lifetime.
Variation, aging and manufacturing reviewDo required margins survive characterized uncertainty, mission history and final processing?The answer remains conditional on model and coverage quality.

This layered approach follows the layout/verification/performance feedback described in Lampaert and the modern automation survey. The explicit certificate schema below is a proposed Philis design. Layout verification loop, PDF pp. 18–20 Performance gap, PDF pp. 1–3 See also constraint systems.

Use result states that distinguish absence of evidence from success

Every rule evaluation should return an applicability decision, a result and supporting evidence. Useful states are pass, fail, not applicable with a reason, unknown because required data are missing, and evaluation failed. “Not applicable” is justified by the physical scope: an oxide antenna rule may not apply to a graph with no exposed MOS gates. An empty gate table caused by a mapping bug is unknown or an error, not non-applicability.

For a required rule, missing models, unsupported device recognition or failed execution should block the corresponding certification. The optimizer may continue exploring and label the candidate provisionally feasible under available checks, but the final report must retain the gap. A default numeric zero is especially dangerous for power, current, coupling or mismatch because it resembles a physically excellent result.

A report needs rule identity and version, severity/tier, affected objects, physical units, measured quantity, limit, normalized residual, model inputs, scenario, evidence location, and applicability explanation. Retain raw measurements even when the ranking uses normalized residuals. This makes two violations comparable without hiding whether one is a nanometer spacing shortfall or a statistical offset limit.

When constraints conflict, report a small explanatory subset if available: a fixed outline, a required symmetry island and a routing corridor may collectively be infeasible even though each is reasonable alone. If the search simply failed, say that instead of presenting an unproved infeasibility diagnosis. Metaverification—checking that constraints and their mappings remain consistent—is developed in the topological survey's final chapter. Chapter 7, §7.4.5, metaverification, PDF pp. 291–293 §7.5.2, conflict checking, PDF pp. 296–298 The distinction between a failed search and a proof of infeasibility is our methodological qualification.

Verify the emitted geometry, including transformations and late changes

Placement boxes and routing centerlines are approximations. Check the actual expanded polygons, cuts, enclosures, pin-access patches, device shapes, guard contacts, dummies and fill. Width-dependent spacing can make a route illegal after widening; a legal via center can still lack enclosure; a rotated macro can invalidate pin access or directional device rules. These changes are reasons to recheck the realized shapes, not reasons to ban useful variants.

Manufacturing rules depend on process stage, net relationship, width, length, voltage domain, density window and connectivity. Represent those conditions rather than reducing every rule to a single minimum spacing. Antenna checking needs the conductor connectivity present at the relevant etch stage. Final connectivity through upper metal may not have existed when lower metal accumulated charge. See manufacturability and protection.

Late fill, density repair, shielding, redundant vias and power reinforcement change parasitics and sometimes topology. Treat them as candidate mutations with invalidated dependent results. A pre-fill performance pass does not certify a post-fill layout when added capacitance or stress matters. For efficiency, use localized incremental checks only when their dependency region is complete; retain a full final check of the accepted geometry.

Preserve device identity through generation and LVS

The reference circuit and generated device must agree on dimensions, finger multiplicity, series/parallel composition, legal source/drain interchange, body connections and recognized model family. A macro containing several schematic devices needs a mapping to its internal physical units. A bounding box or a single macro identifier cannot establish the equivalence of every terminal.

LVS recognition limits must be visible. If a device family has no recognizer, omitting it from the reference may allow the supported subgraph to compare successfully while leaving the omitted device unchecked. State the number and identities of such devices and whether the requested certification requires them. This is particularly relevant to new passive structures or special devices whose electrical function cannot be inferred from arbitrary metal rectangles.

Use adversarial circuits when validating the graph mapping. A diode-connected MOS has G=D and can hide a gate/drain swap. A distinct-gate, distinct-drain device exposes it. Similarly, symmetric test circuits can conceal source/drain or bit-label permutations; add an asymmetric reference with known terminals. The purpose is to test semantic preservation, not just whether a familiar example yields a clean report.

Check extraction quality before using it as the optimization target

At the network level, check units, connectivity, nonnegative passive element values within the extractor's formulation, matrix symmetry where reciprocal physics requires it, and proper treatment of reference nodes. A coupling capacitor between i and j contributes +C to the two diagonals and −C to the off-diagonals. Shunt capacitance and mutual capacitance must not be conflated or double counted.

Use structures that isolate mechanisms: one straight wire on a known stack, two parallel wires with a spacing sweep, a layer crossing, a via chain, a branched resistive net and a capacitor with each plate separately accessible. Compare to analytic limits or an independent qualified extractor. Monotonicity is conditional: changing a wire's width can reduce its resistance while increasing its capacitance, so “all extracted metrics improve with width” is an invalid test.

Then check circuit consequences: equal total C placed on different nodes should produce different behavior when node sensitivities differ. Equivalent series/parallel resistor networks should preserve the appropriate DC result after valid reduction. Reduction must preserve the ports and bandwidth required by the testbench; a lumped approximation that matches DC can still lose settling poles.

An independent extractor disagreement is useful evidence, not automatically a failure of one implementation. Align device recognition, stack parameters, coupling cutoff, reduction policy and reference conditions first, then compare the remaining difference and its performance impact. See parasitic networks for the physical model.

Verify modes, corners, statistics and mission conditions explicitly

Supply sequencing, startup, shutdown, calibration, sampling phases and overload can create terminal voltages or current paths absent from the nominal operating point. For each required check, identify the scenarios that excite its mechanism. EM may require average/DC, RMS and peak currents under different rules; oxide stress depends on terminal voltage history; substrate noise depends on switching spectra and return impedance. One nominal DC solution does not substitute for these.

Electrical performance verification needs clearly measured gain, bandwidth, phase margin, noise, swing, settling, distortion, offset or converter linearity as appropriate to the circuit. Joint yield concerns the probability that all required metrics pass simultaneously. Taking the smallest of several single-metric yields or calling a 3σ value “99.7% circuit yield” generally ignores their dependence and combined failure event. Robust design gives the corresponding models and limits.

Use model-qualified temperatures and lifetime conditions for aging or reliability claims. An EM screen based on room-temperature current density is useful evidence only for that screen. Likewise, a point-source thermal placement estimate can guide separation but does not replace a package-aware thermal solution where temperature margins are tight.

What Philis currently checks, and where the report needs stronger evidence

signoff runs the DRC/ERC/LVS/PEX engine, turns reported violations into hard violations, and treats skipped/refused requested stages or engine failures as failures. It also detects label shorts and reruns label-free checks to gather other geometry findings. These are useful safeguards already present; the handbook does not propose them as missing features.

Two narrower coverage gaps remain in the reviewed implementation. Devices without deck recognizers are reported to stderr and left out of the LVS reference. Individual rules skipped inside a running stage are logged once rather than recorded as structured coverage in the candidate report. A future certificate should expose both sets per run and compare them to the coverage required for that circuit. The current scalar cost is checker.total_cap_ff(); a successful PEX stage still needs the electrical evaluations described above.

Report::feasible requires both hard and budget violation lists to be empty. Report::lex ranks by hard count, summed budget margins and cost. Preserve the difference between a ranked provisional candidate and a fully certified output. In particular, a boolean budget failure with zero stored residual could be present while adding zero to the budget sum; validate invariants on required budget-result construction.

A reviewable final layout certificate

A proposed output bundle contains the final geometry hash; schematic and generated-device mapping; PDK/deck/model versions; declared objective and required scenarios; per-rule and per-device-family coverage; all raw and normalized margins; post-layout metric vector; variation/reliability evidence where required; and the search termination reason. Include links to located failures and to the source/rationale of generated constraints.

This is useful for the engine itself as well as the designer. It prevents a later optimization pass from accepting a candidate on stale checks, makes missing model data actionable, and allows a different checker to reproduce the claims. The final result should say exactly which meaning of “optimal” it supports: for example, the lowest-area verified candidate among the evaluated layouts, under a specified electrical contract.
