Source companion

Matching properties of MOS transistors

Marcel J. M. Pelgrom and Aad C. J. Duinmaijer · ESSCIRC 1988

The supplied four-page conference paper on local area and spatial mismatch; not the 1989 JSSC paper.

Open supplied PDF · 4 physical pages · File identity and coverage

Engine conclusion. Matching is an electrical error budget with at least two distinct components: local variation averaged by device area and spatially correlated variation sampled by device location. Placement can reduce the latter; it cannot claim to eliminate the former. Both coefficients require process characterization. This supplied paper is the four-page ESSCIRC 1988 paper by Marcel J. M. Pelgrom and Aad C. J. Duinmaijer, printed pp. 327–330, rather than the often-cited expanded 1989 JSSC article.

Source and coverage

All four supplied PDF pages were read. PDF pp. 1–2 contain the statistical derivation, transistor model, test structure and measured dependencies; p. 3 contains correlation, the bandgap application and conclusions; p. 4 contains the measurement flow and figures. Equations (1)–(3), coefficient units and orientation discussion were checked in rendered pp. 1–2 because text extraction corrupts symbols. The figure page is the original experimental evidence, not a modern-process calibration. Open supplied paper, PDF p. 1.

What the derivation actually establishes

The model begins with many individually small contributions whose effects add and whose correlation distance is much smaller than transistor dimensions. Spatial averaging then produces a variance inversely proportional to area. A separate large-scale process variation can appear statistically random when the die position on the wafer is unknown, despite having a deterministic wafer-level shape. These are different physical sources and deserve different implementation variables. The authors model pair mismatch using:

σ²(ΔP) = AP²/(WL) + SP²D².

Here W and L describe one equal-size device, D is separation, and the constants describe the measured parameter and process. Treat A as a pair-difference coefficient under this convention. If the PDK instead supplies a single-device local standard deviation, two independent equal devices contribute twice its variance; mixing those conventions creates a √2 error. This convention check is an engine safeguard inferred from variance addition, not a separate claim that the paper offers a general unequal-device formula. Derivation and Eq. (1), PDF p. 1.

The simple area term has a domain of validity. The current factor β depends on W, L, mobility and oxide capacitance. The paper explicitly retains edge-variation contributions before approximating them by one area coefficient:

σ²(Δβ)/β² = AW²/(W²L) + AL²/(WL²) + Aμ²/(μ²WL) + ACox²/(Cox²WL) ≈ Aβ²/(WL).

The authors warn that the last approximation becomes less valid as W and L decrease. Thus even this early paper does not justify treating equal gate area as sufficient to guarantee equal current matching for arbitrary aspect ratios. Eq. (3), PDF p. 2.

Translate matching into a justified placement contract

  1. Establish the electrical quantity and operating conditions. Name the required current ratio, input offset, bias error or reference output spread. Include body bias and operating-point ranges. The paper models threshold through both Vt0 and substrate factor K; therefore matching gate geometry while exposing devices to unequal body potentials leaves an electrical error unaddressed. Required inputs are a circuit performance budget and matching model, not just a recognized topology.
  2. Separate a systematic mean error from stochastic variance. Reticle errors in the experiment caused a nonzero mismatch mean correlated with the mask set. A zero-mean Monte Carlo coefficient cannot represent that error. Store deterministic layout-context penalties or corrections separately from local random covariance, and propagate both to the performance metric. A highly repeatable biased ratio can fail a specification despite a small standard deviation.
  3. Allocate a finite spatial budget after sizing. For a validated equal-device model, imposing σgradient/σrandom ≤ η yields D ≤ ηA/(S√(WL)). The reason is explicit: the placement-induced term must consume no more than its assigned fraction of random mismatch. For η = 0.3, independent variance addition increases total σ by √(1 + 0.3²), about 4.4%, rather than 30%. η itself must come from the circuit budget; the paper does not prescribe 0.3. If S is unknown, report an uncalibrated proximity heuristic.
  4. Preserve orientation and local environment while reducing separation. Proximity targets the D-dependent term; consistent orientation avoids introducing a separately observed effect. Contact count, well boundary, dummy and routing environment need additional sources or PDK characterization: this four-page paper does not prove every modern layout-dependent-effect rule. A shorter pair distance is not beneficial if achieved by an uncharacterized 90° rotation or unequal body connections.
  5. Score a distributed matched structure as a distribution. For split devices, evaluate weighted unit positions and parameter covariance, rather than treating a macro bounding-box center as a complete physical model. Equal first moments cancel an ideal linear spatial field; random unit variation remains. This is a proposed extension using the paper's distinction between correlation scales; the detailed common-centroid construction rules belong to the other supplied sources.
  6. Validate at circuit level. Propagate the parameter variations through the actual circuit sensitivity or Monte Carlo model, including interconnect resistance and body-voltage imbalance. This identifies whether transistor mismatch is still dominant, or whether further area and matching effort would produce little improvement.

Statistical basis, PDF p. 1; nonzero reticle-error means, PDF p. 2; correlation and circuit application, PDF p. 3.

The bandgap example explains where to spend effort

The measured bandgap output standard deviation is about 19 mV. The op-amp contribution dominates at about 18 mV output referred, corresponding to roughly 1.6 mV input offset; the parameter-based calculation gives roughly 1.7 mV input referred, with threshold-related mismatch dominant. Resistor-ratio and bipolar mismatch contributions are smaller in this example. The lesson is to rank placement and sizing work by its propagated contribution to the final specification. Improving an already minor component cannot compensate for the dominant offset source. Conversely, large area increases become wasteful once another component sets the floor. The paper uses the model to estimate the area cost of a tighter reference specification; these numbers are an example of error budgeting, not a universal bandgap recipe. Bandgap application, PDF p. 3.

Current engine integration and evidence needed

MatchingPair at lines 19–80 uses a fixed GRADIENT_PER_AVT_UM2 = 2.5e-4, gate area, and center separation to bound the gradient/random ratio. The algebra of the equal-device ratio matches the proposed allocation above. The comment claiming that S/A carries across processes is stronger than the supplied evidence. That ratio should be a calibrated model input with an explicit applicability domain; cancellation of the symbol A in an expression does not remove the need to know S/A. A and S can reflect different physical populations.

Proposed acceptance record: retain model identifier, process/device flavor, pair versus single-device convention, W/L and bias validity ranges, A/S or covariance data, orientation state, target electrical metric, allocated budget, predicted error and extraction/simulation evidence. Add regression fixtures for area growth tightening the distance budget, nm-to-µm conversion, unknown-model status, unequal devices, and orientation changes. Test the electrical prediction against independently generated Monte Carlo data, not merely the implementation formula.

Read alongside Drennan and McAndrew: that later paper explains why the two-coefficient approximation must not become the engine's universal mismatch model.
