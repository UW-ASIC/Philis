Antenna, ESD, latch-up and operating stress

Protect process-stage and operating current paths with qualified structures and scenarios.

Protection is a property of physical structures, complete current paths, process history and operating scenarios. Antenna, ESD, latchup, electromigration, dielectric stress and aging require different evidence, even when the same metal and wells participate in several mechanisms.

Reliability is a set of conditional physical claims

A manufacturable layout must continue meeting its specification under the required voltage, current, temperature, handling and lifetime conditions. These are different questions. An ordinary operating current can cause long-term electromigration; a brief discharge can rupture an oxide; a transient pad excursion can inject minority carriers and trigger latchup. A circuit may remain functional after stress while its input bias or matching has degraded beyond the allowed error. Reliability therefore belongs in the engine's acceptance conditions, not in a single optional aesthetic penalty.

The source supplies mechanisms and examples, not the qualification limits for the target technology. Every numerical protection threshold must come from the appropriate process/device/package characterization and product specification. Keep separate records for normal operation, startup/shutdown, permitted fault conditions, manufacturing exposure, ESD qualification and aging. Passing one category cannot be substituted for passing another. PDF pp. 211–274, 748–776

Antenna is fabrication-history dependent. A long conductor may not yet connect to a gate when its layer is etched. Conversely, a small final connected component can have a problematic exposure history. The book distinguishes areal and peripheral ratios and discusses latent connections during incomplete etching. A higher-layer jumper changes the earlier connected component; it is not merely a change in final total wire area. A top-layer violation cannot be repaired by jumping to a nonexistent higher layer. A diode brings junction capacitance, leakage, placement and sometimes illumination requirements, all of which must be checked. PDF pp. 228–230

ESD protection must remain benign during operation. A snapback clamp has trigger and holding behavior, while a rate-triggered device responds to slew. A normal power-up ramp, a failsafe input above an unpowered supply, or an external source able to sustain holding current can therefore alter device choice. Extra rail width alone cannot repair a clamp that is inappropriate for the application. Primary pad protection also does not ensure a nearby thin gate survives fast local discharge; return points and local secondary clamps matter. The book's illustrated HBM/CDM values are examples from specific structures and eras, not current universal qualification requirements. PDF pp. 749–752, 766–776

Antenna checking as a process-stage graph problem

Plasma processing can deposit charge on a conductor before the final interconnect stack exists. The vulnerable gate oxide is the discharge path. During late etching, exposed perimeters can matter; during ashing, exposed surface area can matter. The source also describes temporary connections that disappear only after narrow gaps finish clearing. Final-net area alone is consequently an incomplete physical model. The exact foundry deck determines which effects are represented and how they are accumulated.

Proposed algorithm. For each relevant manufacturing stage, build the connected-component graph using only connections that exist at that stage. Associate each component with the vulnerable gate areas and oxide families it reaches. Compute the stage's defined exposed area, perimeter or cumulative quantity, then apply qualified diode credits and rule limits. Retain the offending stage and component as the diagnostic. If the process includes latent-connection rules, apply the foundry-defined geometric grouping; do not invent an oversize radius from a generic example. PDF pp. 228–230

Worked example. Assume a hypothetical M1 rule limits its exposed-area-to-gate-area ratio to 100. A gate of 1 μm² connected to 180 μm² of M1 fails that rule. A qualified M2 bridge can divide the early M1 into a 20 μm² gate-connected island and a remote conductor. At the M1 stage the gate now sees a ratio of 20. This calculation says nothing about the M2 rule, which must still be evaluated after its connections exist; it also ignores perimeter and cumulative rules unless the hypothetical deck includes them. The example numbers are deliberately illustrative, not usable PDK defaults.

Repair selection is itself an analog optimization. A bridge adds vias, changes resistance and parasitics, consumes upper-layer access and may disturb route symmetry. A discharge diode adds leakage and capacitance, has a process-qualified bias construction and may need a fill exclusion. The source's illuminated-junction mechanism for one antenna-diode type is a particularly useful reminder that an ordinary-looking diode footprint may carry non-obvious surrounding-metal restrictions. A top-layer violation cannot be fixed by adding an unavailable higher layer. After repair, repeat antenna and circuit checks on the resulting geometry.

Philis currently has a useful seed for an antenna metric, but the inspected kernel/analog/src/routing/antenna.rs sums routed area and divides by gate area. Treat that result as a screening estimate unless the target process explicitly validates that simplification. A stage-aware rule should name the graph, measure, gate family, diode credit and deck version. This recommendation concerns the inspected implementation, not every possible downstream signoff tool.

Confirmed integration issue in the inspected working tree. The SPICE parser emits MOS terminals in G, D, S, B order, and the hypergraph preserves that order. However, the antenna requirement annotator adds gate area to nets[1], which denotes the drain in this representation. That can attach a gate-area requirement to the wrong net before the geometric antenna estimate even runs. Resolve the gate by its named terminal or a shared typed terminal accessor, and add a regression with distinct gate and drain nets. A diode-connected device, where those nets coincide, can conceal the mistake. This is a code observation and proposed fix; no engine code was changed for these notes.

ESD architecture, local protection and complete discharge paths

ESD planning begins at the pins and domain boundaries. For each required stress polarity and pin-to-pin combination, identify how current enters, which qualified devices conduct and where it exits. Include internal supply, ground and substrate-return segments. A clamp with an acceptable standalone characteristic can fail to protect a gate if the path adds excessive voltage drop or if another device triggers first and absorbs the current.

The book distinguishes clipping, snapback and rate-fired protectors. A clipping device has a leakage-versus-clamp-voltage tradeoff. A snapback device must not remain on under an allowed supply or externally driven condition. A rate-fired protector must distinguish a qualifying discharge from the allowed supply/input slew or provide qualified control appropriate to the application. A failsafe input above an unpowered supply adds a further constraint. These are circuit and library decisions; an automatic router should preserve the selected protection architecture rather than silently synthesize a different one. §14.4.1, PDF pp. 750–752; §14.4.2, PDF p. 769, failsafe CDM clamps

Why secondary protection is local. The protected quantity is usually a voltage across an oxide or junction, not voltage at the package pad relative to an abstract ideal ground. A remote ground line can rise while a local source remains at another potential. Put qualified secondary structures near the vulnerable terminals and join their return to the same local reference. Preserve the specified limiting resistance or filter topology. Lower routing resistance is not universally beneficial if a deliberately inserted resistance coordinates protection.

Illustrative path budget. Suppose a qualified clamp develops 7 V at a chosen 2 A transient operating point, the relevant path contributes 0.4 Ω, and a 1 nH loop segment experiences 0.5 A/ns. The lumped terms give 7 + 0.8 + 0.5 = 8.3 V. An 8 V allowed transient stress would therefore be exceeded in this simplified example. Actual qualification needs the nonlinear time-domain network and appropriate pulse characterization; the arithmetic shows why clamp voltage alone is not the protected-terminal voltage. Shortening a local loop can matter as much as increasing the number of remote clamps.

Power handling cannot be inferred solely from total drawn transistor width. During snapback, one finger may turn on first and conduct a disproportionate fraction of current. The source discusses local ballasting and structure-dependent current spreading. Use qualified multifinger ESD cells with permitted routing and contact arrangements. Do not remove ballast or silicide-block geometry merely because a DC optimizer sees extra resistance. ESD damage can be parametric: reverse emitter-base avalanche may degrade β and input-bias accuracy without immediately destroying the transistor. PDF pp. 757–761, 766–769

Current, heat, dielectric stress and aging

Electromigration is local. Resistance integrates along a path, but reliability can be set by one narrow neck or crowded via. The relevant current and temperature metrics depend on the foundry lifetime model and waveform. A via array's total cut count is not sufficient when geometry feeds mostly the inner corner. Route straight distributed transitions where useful, check every bottleneck and retain the current direction, waveform and local temperature assumption. Extra metal can reduce resistance while leaving the critical neck unchanged. PDF pp. 217–220, 821–822

Heating depends on time and stack. A deposited resistor separated from silicon by oxide can have a different thermal path from a diffused resistor. A brief pulse can deposit energy before heat has spread through the same volume involved in steady state. Check continuous dissipation and transient energy using qualified models. A large total device area does not rule out current focusing within a smaller hot region. Electrothermal verification should include the feedback of temperature on conduction, not merely annotate a temperature after a fixed-current calculation.

Dielectric stress is not just a spacing lookup. Gate and capacitor dielectrics, interlevel insulators and field regions have different qualified voltage, time and temperature domains. Nearby-net spacing must use the maximum allowed differential voltage, including startup and domain sequencing. For a lifetime requirement, retain the applicable dielectric-aging model or conservative qualified operating limit. Historical source examples demonstrate the mechanism but do not establish a modern allowable field. §5.1.4, PDF pp. 220–224; §15.4.4, PDF pp. 820–821, differential-voltage spacing

Matching can age. Devices with equal initial geometry but unequal drain stress, gate bias, duty cycle or temperature can diverge. Model differential degradation where it affects the specification. A symmetric pattern cannot make different operating histories equal. This is especially relevant when the engine claims that a mirror or differential input remains accurate over lifetime rather than only at initial extraction. §5.3.1, PDF pp. 236–238; §5.3.4, PDF pp. 243–244

Guard rings and device sharing require a cross-sectional model

A substrate tap usually reduces majority-carrier voltage drop. A minority-carrier collecting ring intercepts carriers at a suitable reverse-biased junction. A blocking ring uses an effective high-low junction or dielectric barrier to obstruct their path. These are different mechanisms even when their top views look like similar rings. The relevant carrier type, depth, buried layer, bias and contact resistance determine whether carriers pass beneath a ring, over a weak junction, or through an opening. PDF pp. 256–270, 735–742

Engine actions and reasons: identify potential injectors over transient as well as DC scenarios; choose a qualified guard architecture for the actual substrate; ensure its required three-dimensional continuity; connect it to an appropriate low-impedance node; and verify the return path and local debiasing. A nominal enclosure without a sound electrical connection can fail. More width eventually gives diminishing benefit, and a ring that works on a heavily doped substrate may be marginal on a lightly doped one. A blocking ring typically needs complete enclosure; the text also discusses qualified collecting-ring arrangements near die edges that are not closed rectangles. Do not collapse these distinctions into “all rings must look alike.”

Sharing is an electrical transformation. Merging devices in a common well/tank saves repeated isolation area but introduces shared resistance, junction capacitance and minority-carrier paths. Net equivalence is necessary for many mergers but not sufficient for safe operation. Saturating lateral PNPs, noisy isolated MOS devices, shared contacts carrying large current, and resistors whose bodies become forward biased illustrate the failure modes. Whitelist qualified merger templates with terminal/bias and injection conditions, and otherwise retain separate regions. A merge should be re-extracted and rechecked under scenarios, not approved merely because DRC and nominal LVS pass. PDF pp. 723–734, 600

What the engine must produce to justify “protected”

Create a per-scenario evidence record containing the exact cell versions, current paths, terminal voltages, thermal assumptions, rule/model revisions, measured or simulated limits and remaining unknowns. Protection proof is a conjunction: the structure must be qualified; the integration must respect its restrictions; the evaluated paths must stay inside its domain; and the intended product qualification must be satisfied. A low routing cost establishes none of those facts by itself.

Regression cases should exercise different failure mechanisms: an antenna route whose final connectivity is unchanged by a successful stage-specific repair; an ESD clamp with an inadequate return path; an off supply with a driven failsafe input; a many-via transition with current crowding; a substrate injector beneath a shallow collecting guard; and a matched pair with unequal aging duty. Each test should fail for its physical reason and pass only after the corresponding mechanism is resolved. Where the PDK supplies no model, expose an unsupported or externally qualified requirement rather than a fictional numerical margin.
