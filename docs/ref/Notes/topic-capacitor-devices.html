Capacitor units and parasitic environments

Choose qualified plate/fringe structures and preserve ratios, edges and terminal identities.

Capacitor layout must control intended capacitance, both plate parasitics, lead coupling and the switching network. This chapter explains how unit geometry, common-centroid placement, dummies, shielding and routing combine to produce—or spoil—a precision ratio.

Define what capacitance the circuit actually uses

A capacitor can set a time constant, integrate current, divide charge, sample a voltage, filter noise or resonate with an inductor. Each function responds differently to plate parasitics and losses. For a ratioed charge-redistribution array, the intended signal is a relation among charges in a network of switches and capacitors. The relevant object is therefore the extracted capacitance matrix and its switching states, not only the sum of PCell values.

Hastings' capacitor chapter develops intended capacitance, fringing fields, nonlinear junction and MOS behavior, series resistance and different plate-to-surrounding parasitics. Those mechanisms explain why top/bottom plate identity and the electrical role of a shield should be part of circuit intent. A generic two-pin rectangle loses that information. PDF pp. 327–354

Capacitors: match the whole charge-transfer network

The book distinguishes junction, MOS, poly-poly, metal-metal, stacked, lateral-flux and trench capacitors. Their voltage dependence, leakage, plate resistance, substrate parasitics, dielectric absorption, breakdown and density differ. Two terminals on a schematic do not imply interchangeable plates in layout. Even when the intended capacitance matches perfectly, bottom-plate or lead coupling can change charge redistribution. Philis acceptance criterion. For a switched-capacitor network, assess the extracted network in each switch phase and under the intended charge-transfer operation. An absolute capacitance error, a ratio error, a parasitic to a quiet rail and a parasitic to a switching aggressor have different consequences. The physical unit pattern should therefore be evaluated together with its route topology and phase-dependent connections.

Worked example: a perfect unit ratio with a wrong total ratio

Suppose A uses one 100 fF unit and B uses two identical 100 fF units. Their drawn ratio is exactly 2:1. To isolate the arithmetic, assume the connecting leads add capacitance directly between the same two electrical terminals as each intended capacitor: 2 fF for A and 3 fF for B. The effective ratio is then 203/102 ≈ 1.9902 rather than 2. Its fractional error is about −0.49%.

This example deliberately makes the lead parasitic act in parallel with the intended device. Real arrays are more complicated: a parasitic to ground, a switching bottom plate or a neighboring bit does not always add in that way. The appropriate procedure is to extract the matrix, apply the switching phases and compute the actual transfer or settling error. The simple example establishes why “identical units” is a starting condition, not a final matching result.

Equal lead length is insufficient. One line may lie on a different layer, cross a ground strap, pass a dummy electrode, or run beside a switching signal. The same physical length then has a different electrostatic environment. The source therefore emphasizes lead capacitance, widths, crossings, shielding and surrounding material rather than length alone. PDF pp. 380–382, 425

Why the unit-size choice has an optimum

Increasing unit dimensions reduces edge/perimeter contribution relative to area and can improve local random matching. But a larger unit samples more spatial variation, makes a larger array and may complicate routing. Subdivision improves the ability to balance spatial moments while adding dummies, contacts and lead capacitance. The optimum depends on which term currently dominates the circuit error.

A practical search. Start with qualified unit cells across a small size range. For each, generate a compact balanced pattern with its final dummy boundary and legal access. Estimate random/peripheral mismatch using the characterized model, assess first and second moments for known or bounded fields, route the array, and extract the total matrix. Reject candidates that miss settling, voltage, leakage, density or area requirements. Rank the remaining candidates by the specified transfer-error or yield objective.

Do not maximize dispersion without a stopping criterion. If every improvement in device-gradient balance adds a disproportionately long top-plate route, the final conversion accuracy can get worse. Conversely, a slightly larger routing envelope may be worthwhile if it permits well-controlled, symmetric shielded connections. The correct comparison includes the entire array and the switches or reference connections that determine its operation.

For noninteger ratios, a special extra unit can compensate nominal area and fringe terms, but sharp corners, added holes or narrow features can create new manufacturing sensitivity. Characterize the actual geometry. A matched area/perimeter ratio is a first-order construction aid, not proof of identical field solutions at every bias. PDF pp. 374–378, 423–425

Choose shielding and isolation for a specific disturbance

An electrostatic shield works by providing a controlled boundary whose potential changes little under injected charge. That requires an appropriate connection and sufficiently low impedance over the relevant frequency range. A thin long ground lead can let a nominal shield move; a shared noisy ground can inject disturbances rather than suppress them. The shield also adds capacitance, changes routing access and may affect density or nearby devices.

A well underneath a capacitor can isolate a plate from the bulk substrate, but introduces a junction and requires a valid bias. If that junction forward biases during a switching excursion, the circuit can inject minority carriers. Dummy electrodes also need intentional connections: they are not electrically absent merely because the netlist calls them dummy. The ideal tie depends on plate configuration, noise and the permitted voltage range.

Boundary test. Compare the same array with a quiet shield, a resistive shield return and a shield tied to a switching reference. Extract the changes and simulate the actual measurement phase. This discriminates physical attenuation from a geometric “shield present” flag. Repeat after final fill because nearby floating or tied metal changes fringe coupling.

PDK inputs and signoff questions

Obtain nominal and nonlinear capacitance models, local mismatch coefficients and their geometry domain, top/bottom plate parasitics, dielectric-loss/leakage behavior, voltage and lifetime limits, supported electrode/dielectric stacks, dummy/fill rules and extraction technology. Where junction or MOS capacitors are used, include bias conditions and temperature dependence. Where trench or lateral-flux structures are available, use their specific characterization rather than forcing a parallel-plate approximation.

The final report should name the circuit metric, unit geometry, unit counts, moment balance, route and shield topology, extracted matrix, evaluated switch states, mismatch/yield assumptions and remaining uncertainty. A valid teaching conclusion is “this layout removes first-order spatial error under the assumed field and meets the extracted settling budget.” A claim of universal optimality or guaranteed converter accuracy needs substantially more evidence.
