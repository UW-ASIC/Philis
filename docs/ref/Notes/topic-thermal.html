Thermal fields, gradients and package effects

Distinguish absolute temperature, pair imbalance, source estimates and coupled thermal models.

Thermal optimality means controlled absolute temperature and controlled differences

Heat changes transistor bias, resistance, noise, passive values, stress, and interconnect lifetime. Two separate requirements follow: keep every device and conductor within its allowed absolute temperature, and keep temperature differences small where the circuit is sensitive to mismatch. A pair at identical but excessive temperature can satisfy the second requirement while failing the first. A cool pair can still have excessive differential error if its members see unequal heating.

This chapter combines Hastings’s Fourier-law and thermal-matching discussion with the EM book’s coupled thermal/electrical/mechanical mechanisms and the mismatch papers’ sensitivity framework. The heat equations, numerical examples, and proposed algorithms below are instructional synthesis; they are not a claim that the supplied sources provide a complete foundry thermal deck. Thermal and stress migration; Electrical sensitivity framework.

1. Start with heat generation, transport, and circuit response

A thermal analysis needs three linked descriptions. First, power is dissipated by active devices, resistors, interconnect resistance, and other losses. Second, heat flows through silicon, dielectric layers, metal, the die attach, package, and external cooling environment. Third, electrical behavior responds to the resulting temperature distribution. An accurate placement temperature map with incorrect power inputs is still an incorrect prediction.

For steady DC operation, resistor dissipation is I²R, and transistor heat follows its absorbed electrical power under the chosen port/sign convention. Dynamic operation requires average loss over an appropriate interval or a time-resolved dissipated-power waveform. Instantaneous absorbed terminal power can also charge stored electric or magnetic energy: energy conservation gives heat generation = absorbed power − dEstored/dt for a model with no other energy outputs. In periodic steady state, stored-energy change averages to zero over a complete period. A terminal may deliver electrical power elsewhere, so blindly summing absolute terminal powers can overestimate dissipation. Conversely, a single nominal operating-point value can miss an output stage, startup circuit, or periodically active block’s dominant heating mode.

Power modes should preserve correlation: a load, its driver, and the supply metal may heat simultaneously. An arbitrary independent maximum at every device can be very pessimistic; a nominal average at every device can be unsafe. Use explicit scenarios or a bounded model whose conservatism is stated. Unknown dissipation must remain distinguishable from a verified zero-power condition.

2. The heat equation explains the model hierarchy

ρmcp ∂T/∂t = ∇·[k(T,r)∇T] + q(r,t).

Here ρm is mass density, cp heat capacity per unit mass, k thermal conductivity, and q volumetric heat generation. The time derivative stores heat; the divergence term transports it. This equation is a derived energy-conservation statement combined with Fourier heat flux qheat = −k∇T. Hastings gives Fourier’s law in §8.2.7, Eq. (8.22), PDF page 386; the transient storage term is the conservation extension used here. Fourier-law basis, Eq. (8.22), printed p. 385 / PDF p. 386. Boundary conditions describe fixed temperature, specified heat flux, or heat exchange through a finite thermal resistance. A die edge, package surface, and ideal infinite heat sink are not interchangeable boundaries.

In a linear steady-state model, the temperature-rise vector can be written ΔT = RthP. Each matrix element describes the temperature response at one observation region to power in one source region. Off-diagonal elements are thermal coupling. For one fixed isothermal reference boundary and constant linear elements, a transient reduced model takes the form CthdT/dt + Gth(T − Tboundary1) = P(t), where 1 is the all-ones vector. With multiple independently varying boundaries, write their driving terms explicitly instead of subtracting one scalar boundary temperature. It can capture the fact that a fast burst initially heats a local region, while slower heating spreads through the die and package.

The source discusses analytical, lumped, and numerical thermal methods in the context of reliability. A hierarchy is useful: a cheap spreading model for placement search, a calibrated layered or reduced network for candidate evaluation, and a detailed field solve for critical or unusual structures. Accuracy should be judged against the temperature and electrical quantities that matter, rather than choosing the most expensive solver for every move. Coupled numerical and reduced modeling, PDF 58–69.

3. What a half-space point-source model does and does not mean

For a point heater depositing power P at the flat surface of a homogeneous, isotropic, semi-infinite medium with constant conductivity k, assume the remaining surface is adiabatic and T approaches T∞ far away. Hemispherical symmetry gives outward heat flux P/(2πr²). Fourier’s law then gives dT/dr = −P/(2πkr²), and integration from r to infinity yields ΔT(r) = T(r) − T∞ = P/(2πkr). This is the handbook’s derivation under those boundary conditions; it is not asserted to be an equation printed in the supplied books. The 2π factor differs from the 4π factor for a point source in an unbounded full space. Philis uses this half-space approximation with a floor on r near the source. The inverse-distance dependence captures an important placement effect: temperature gradients can be steep near a heater and weaker farther away. Linear superposition is exact for that chosen linear model, not for every real layered die/package problem.

The singularity at r = 0 indicates that a point source is an approximation. Replacing r with a geometric floor is a practical regularization; it is not equivalent to integrating the actual finite source shape through the actual material stack. Die edges, finite thickness, anisotropic layers, buried oxides, metal spreading, backside attach, and temperature-dependent conductivity can change both magnitude and symmetry. A substrate-only model also does not capture local heating in a thin metal wire surrounded by low-conductivity dielectric.

A package thermal resistance sometimes contributes an approximately shared temperature rise to nearby devices. That common rise can cancel in a differential temperature calculation while remaining decisive for absolute device and EM limits. The common-rise approximation itself needs checking near nonuniform attach, package edges, or multiple heat paths. It is incorrect to substitute a differential-placement estimate for an absolute junction-temperature model.

4. Equal thermal exposure can be better than minimum separation

For one symmetric point source, equal radius gives equal modeled temperature. For multiple sources, the relevant quantity is the sum of their transfer-weighted powers. Two positions equidistant from one heater can see very different exposure to another. Their equality can also disappear when operating modes change. Thermal matching should therefore compare transfer vectors across scenarios, not merely distance to the largest nominal heater.

If the pair is small compared with the scale over which temperature varies, ΔT ≈ ∇T·Δr. Placing its separation vector approximately along a local isotherm reduces first-order difference; placing it along the gradient increases difference. This is the thermal counterpart of matching a spatial field. Finite device extent, internal gradients, unequal self-heating, and nonlinear fields limit the point-center approximation. Hastings §8.2.7, Eq. (8.23), gives the corresponding first-order resistor-mismatch dependence and the isotherm-placement reasoning. Thermal-gradient mismatch, Eq. (8.23), printed p. 387 / PDF p. 388.

There are competing objectives. Keeping devices close often helps common-centroid construction, substrate rejection, parasitic symmetry, and routing. Moving them to equal thermal exposure can increase wire length or coupling. A multiobjective placer should retain these costs while using an actual thermal sensitivity or transfer estimate for acceptance.

5. Worked example: a small temperature difference can become an offset

Illustrative model: take a 10 mW point heater in a semi-infinite medium with k = 148 W/(m·K), the constant used by the current code. The model predicts approximately 1.075 K rise at 10 µm, 0.538 K at 20 µm, and 0.269 K at 40 µm. A pair at 10 and 20 µm has about 0.537 K differential rise. Two points at equal 20 µm radius have zero difference in this ideal model, although each remains about 0.538 K above the modeled boundary temperature.

If an illustrative input-referred sensitivity to the pair’s temperature difference is 1 mV/K, the first arrangement contributes about 0.537 mV systematic input error. A 50 µV allocation would require |ΔT| ≤ 0.05 K under that assumed sensitivity. Neither coefficient nor budget is a process recommendation. The calculation shows how to derive a thermal constraint from a circuit specification rather than choosing an arbitrary maximum temperature difference.

Uniform temperature change does not always cancel even for a matched pair. Devices can have different biases, ratios, or surrounding circuitry; a resistor ratio can use materials with different temperature coefficients; an amplifier’s common-mode operating point can move. Compute the circuit sensitivity to both common and differential temperature modes. The mismatch paper’s propagation framework naturally supports this, provided the temperature dependencies and any correlations are represented. Sensitivity propagation.

6. Use electrically weighted temperatures for multi-unit structures

A common-centroid array is not a single point. Each unit has a temperature and a different possible influence on the circuit output. If Δe ≈ ΣsiΔTi, the sensitivities si define the relevant weighting. A simple arithmetic average is valid only for the corresponding equal-weight model. Hastings explicitly assumes active planar regions, equal contribution per active area, linear electrical temperature dependence, and a spatially linear temperature field before identifying the centroid temperature with the device’s average. Centroid-temperature assumptions, §8.2.7, PDF pp. 387–388. Taking the hottest member of each group is useful for an absolute maximum-temperature check but is not generally the right statistic for differential offset.

For equal parallel mirror units, temperature changes both each unit’s current and the reference gate voltage that couples them. Their effective weights must come from the circuit. For resistors distributed across a gradient, the integral of local resistivity and geometry determines resistance; a center temperature may miss a strong internal gradient. For a capacitor array, temperature coefficients and dielectric stress can matter differently. This is why the thermal model should expose per-unit or spatial temperatures rather than only a single opaque “thermal score.”

Self-heating adds another asymmetry. Two geometrically identical devices may dissipate different power because one has a different drain voltage or output swing. A perfect geometric reflection does not equalize their power. The evaluation loop must update power from the circuit after parasitic extraction and consider whether the topology itself permits thermal matching.

7. Electrothermal feedback changes both performance and reliability

For a metal path under approximately fixed current, increasing resistance increases I²R loss, which can raise temperature and resistance further. Under a fixed-voltage excitation, the feedback differs because current can decrease. Active devices add bias-dependent feedback. Therefore a thermal solver cannot infer stability merely from the sign of a metal temperature coefficient; it needs the electrical boundary conditions.

The EM lifetime dependence on absolute temperature is strong and nonlinear. A small hot spot can dominate reliability even when the average die temperature looks benign. Temperature gradients also drive thermomigration, and nonuniform expansion changes stress. The EM source treats these mechanisms together and explains why a local electrical improvement can shift the dominant failure mechanism. Coupled migration mechanisms; Application temperature and lifetime.

A coupled solve may use fixed-point iteration, damping, or a more tightly coupled method. The implementation should check convergence of temperatures, currents, and power, not merely complete a fixed number of iterations and assume convergence. If the model predicts unstable feedback or fails to converge, report that condition and investigate the model or design rather than returning the last iterate as a certified result.

8. Thermal stress and package effects need their own model boundary

Materials expand by different amounts when temperature changes. Constrained expansion produces stress, which can alter transistor mobility and passive behavior and can influence interconnect voiding or extrusion. Die attach, package structures, TSVs, large metal features, and local process geometry can make stress strongly position dependent. The same temperature map can therefore correspond to different electrical errors under different mechanical boundaries.

Distance from a hot block is not a substitute for a stress-aware rule, and the EM back-stress mechanism should not be confused with stress-induced device mismatch. Use foundry layout-dependent models and qualified keepouts where available. If a process supplies only empirical stress-context restrictions, enforce them as such and retain their provenance. Do not invent a universal stress coefficient from the thermal conductivity or from a historical material table. Thermal-expansion mismatch and mechanical effects, PDF 45–57.

A source observation that a uniform ambient-temperature change did not significantly change a measured mismatch statistic in one process does not imply that local gradients or package stress are harmless. The supplied Pelgrom experiment is a particular characterization result; its scope should remain attached to it. Temperature and measured mismatch discussion.

9. Proposed engine workflow and constraints

  1. Obtain power scenarios and boundary conditions. Include ambient range, package/attach model, significant neighboring blocks, and transient duty cycles. Reject missing power as insufficient evidence rather than interpret it as a cold circuit.
  2. Build a thermal model appropriate to the search stage. Calibrate fast spreading or reduced models against a trusted solver over the supported placement domain.
  3. Map circuit sensitivities to temperature modes. Derive absolute-temperature limits, weighted differential limits, and internal-gradient limits from devices and circuit specifications.
  4. Generate candidate moves using thermal transfer information. Use local derivatives, cached Green-function changes, or incremental source updates instead of pair proximity alone.
  5. Evaluate all relevant power modes. A layout optimized for one active block may fail when another mode changes the dominant heater.
  6. Recompute electrical power after routing or sizing changes. Parasitic voltage drop, changed resistance, and altered bias can change the thermal map.
  7. Validate final candidates at higher fidelity. Include package and metal self-heating where they affect absolute temperature or reliability.
  8. Report limiting source, temperature mode, circuit sensitivity, and uncertainty. This explains why the layout was chosen and what additional data would improve confidence.

10. Specific implications for the current Philis implementation

The thermal kernel computes an O(n²) point-source superposition with k = 148 W/(m·K), a source half-extent distance floor, and missing power entries treated as zero. Layout::temp_mc stores a temperature rise, despite its short field name. It must not be passed directly to an EM formula requiring absolute kelvin. A group’s temperature in delta_temp_mc is its hottest member; that is not an electrically weighted group mismatch model.

ThermalGradient checks the resulting ΔT but its trial-move cost is a pair-distance pull because the temperature map updates per epoch. The code already contains an equal-isotherm test, illustrating the physical distinction. The next improvement is a trial-move thermal estimate or derivative that rewards equal transfer from the relevant heaters, while retaining exact epoch/final checks. This would make the search direction better aligned with the quantity it ultimately accepts.

The rule reports itself inapplicable when all powers are zero, which is useful for a genuinely unpowered case. The data contract still needs to distinguish a verified all-zero power map from missing operating information. The former supports a zero-heating conclusion; the latter supports only an unverified thermal status. This distinction should be propagated into the final report rather than hidden by a successful geometric run.

Validation and evidence limits

Test single-source distance scaling; equal-radius pairs; two-heater mode changes; finite-source near-field behavior; die-edge/package asymmetry; unequal self-heating in matched devices; and transient bursts with different thermal time constants. Compare group-weighted electrical error with a circuit model, not only maximum temperature. Verify that power scaling produces the expected response in the linear model and that the solver recovers a declared boundary temperature at zero power. Compare the calibrated fast model against a layered or numerical reference before claiming quantitative temperature margins. This chapter explicitly separates the current half-space heuristic, general heat-flow synthesis, and the supplied literature’s thermal/reliability evidence.
