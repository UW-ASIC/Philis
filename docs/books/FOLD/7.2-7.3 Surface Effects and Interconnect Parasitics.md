# 7.2-7.3 Surface Effects and Interconnect Parasitics

> **Chapter context**: Addressing Reliability in Physical Design

## Key Concepts

This section covers two broad categories of parasitic effects that layout designers must address: **surface effects** on the silicon die (specifically channel stop techniques and hot carrier injection) and **interconnect parasitics** arising in the metal routing layers (line losses, signal distortions, and crosstalk). Both categories can be mitigated through careful physical design choices.

---

### 7.2 Surface Effects (continued)

#### 7.2.1 Poly-Channel Stop Rings (wrap-up)

The section begins by concluding the discussion of parasitic channel suppression methods from [[7.1 Parasitic Effects in Silicon]]. **Poly-channel stop rings** are placed at both the inner and outer boundaries of an n-well:

- The **outer ring** is connected to the p-substrate potential (ground).
- The **inner ring** is connected to the n-well potential.

These poly interconnects shield all electric fields emanating downward from the metal layers above, preventing both parasitic n-channels and parasitic p-channels from forming. Poly-channel stop rings are widely deployed in **high-voltage chips**, though their use is normally restricted to wells at high potential because they consume significant surface area. Wells are selected for poly-channel stops based on their **voltage class assignments**.

> **Design guideline**: Combining poly-channel stoppers with NSD or PSD channel stoppers is not recommended, because the field oxide (FOX) would no longer be present in that configuration.

#### 7.2.2 Hot Carrier Injection

Hot carrier injection (HCI) is an aging/reliability mechanism that occurs in MOS-FETs during normal operation and leads to threshold voltage drift over time.

**Physical mechanism:**

1. When an NMOS-FET is driven with $V_{GS} = V_{GB} > V_{th}$ (assuming $V_{BS} = 0$) and a drain-source voltage $V_{DS} \geq V_{GS} - V_{th}$, the transistor enters **saturation**.
2. In saturation, the inversion channel becomes **pinched off** at the drain end, creating a region of very high electric field.
3. Above a field strength of approximately **1 V/um**, electrons reach a maximum drift velocity of about **10^5 m/s**. These are called **hot electrons** (or more generally, hot carriers, since the same effect occurs with holes in PMOS-FETs).
4. Hot carriers possess enough energy to:
   - **(i) Ionize silicon atoms** -- generating additional electron-hole pairs. The extra electrons increase drain current, while holes drift through the space-charge region to the backgate, producing a measurable **substrate current**.
   - **(ii) Overcome the energy barrier to the gate oxide (GOX)** -- charge carriers enter the oxide and become **trapped**.

**Consequences of trapped charges:**

- The trapped charges (which can be positive or negative for both NMOS and PMOS) act as **impurities** that shift MOS-FET threshold voltages $V_{th}$ and degrade gate breakdown voltages.
- With repeated operation under these conditions, charges **accumulate** in the GOX over time, producing an **aging effect** that causes $V_{th}$ to drift progressively.

**Scaling relationship:**

- Lateral field strengths increase with shorter channel lengths, making HCI worse in scaled technologies.
- In **older processes**, the channel length should not be shorter than approximately **3 um** to suppress HCI.
- In **advanced submicron processes**, different mitigation strategies are used instead.

**Lightly Doped Drain (LDD) -- the primary countermeasure:**

The most important methodology for suppressing HCI is the **lightly doped drain (LDD)** concept. The underlying physics is that the field strength in a space-charge region is inversely proportional to the dopant concentration (as discussed in Chapter 6, Sect. 6.2.2). In an LDD transistor:

- The drain zone has a **lower local doping** near the channel boundary.
- This allows the electric field to **spread out** over a larger volume in the drain zone rather than peaking sharply at the p-n junction.
- The reduced **field apex** at this critical point weakens the hot carrier effect and also **increases the breakdown voltage** at the drain.

**LDD trade-offs:**
- Requires an **extra doped layer** for both NMOS and PMOS transistors (additional process complexity).
- Slightly **increases track resistance** of the drain.
- Despite these drawbacks, LDDs have become an absolute necessity in advanced processes with very short channel lengths and are now standard practice.

### 7.3 Interconnect Parasitics

After covering parasitic effects in the bulk (Sect. 7.1) and on the silicon surface (Sect. 7.2), this section addresses parasitic effects in the **interconnect (metal routing) layers**. Real interconnects are not perfect short circuits -- they have distributed parasitic elements.

**Primary line constants** for a two-wire conductor (per unit length):

| Symbol | Parameter | Significance on chip |
|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| $R'$ | Line resistance per unit length | Significant -- causes IR drop and thermal losses |
| $L'$ | Line inductance per unit length | Negligible on-chip (dimensions too small, unless deep GHz range) |
| $C'$ | Insulator capacitance per unit length | Significant -- causes signal delay and distortion |
| $G'$ | Insulator conductance per unit length | Negligible (oxides are excellent insulators) |

For on-chip interconnects below the GHz range, the dominant parasitics are **R** and **C**, making the interconnect behave as an **RC element**.

#### 7.3.1 Line Losses

The parasitic track resistance is calculated as:

$$R = R_{\square} \cdot \frac{l}{w}$$

where:
- $R_{\square}$ is the **sheet resistance** of the interconnect layer (Ohms/square)
- $l$ is the line length
- $w$ is the interconnect width

This resistance causes two problems:

1. **Thermal loss**: Power dissipated as $I^2 \cdot R$ (Joule heating in the wire).
2. **IR drop**: A voltage drop $\Delta V = I \cdot R$ along the line, meaning the voltage at the end of the conductor ($V_e$) is lower than at the beginning ($V_b$).

**Layout design guidelines for IR drop:**

- The IR drop can be managed by adjusting $R$ through changes to line length $l$, interconnect width $w$, or layer selection (different $R_{\square}$).
- **Rule of thumb**: IR drops greater than **10 mV** should always be checked.
- Line length $l$ depends heavily on **pin locations**, so device **placement** should be optimized to minimize distances between devices that carry high current.
- The width $w$ should be selected so that the **current-carrying capacity** of the line is sufficient (discussed further in Sect. 7.5).

#### 7.3.2 Signal Distortions

Every on-chip interconnect has a significant **parasitic capacitance** $C$ to the substrate due to the thin deposited layers. The interconnect behaves as an RC element: for every signal transmitted, the capacitor $C$ must be charged through the resistor $R$.

- An ideal step function input is **delayed and distorted** by the time it reaches the end of the line.
- The **time constant** of the RC element is $\tau = R \cdot C$ (67% of the final value is reached after one time constant).

**Parallel-plate capacitor model:**

$$C = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r \cdot \frac{A}{d}$$

where:
- $\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r$ is the permittivity of the dielectric (oxide)
- $A$ is the wire surface area (proportional to width $w$ and length $l$)
- $d$ is the oxide layer thickness between the wire and the substrate

Key insight: **Lower metal layers** are closer to the substrate (smaller $d$), so they have **greater parasitic capacitances** than upper metal layers.

**Fringe field correction:** The simple parallel-plate formula underestimates the actual capacitance because it ignores **fringe fields**. For typical narrow interconnects (e.g., $w < 2\ \mu m$ for Metal1, $w < 4\ \mu m$ for Metal2), the fringe-field capacitance per unit length $C'_{fringe}$ actually **exceeds** the plate capacitance per unit length $C'_{plate}$. Typical values are in the range of **double-digit aF/um^2** for $C'_{plate}$ and **aF/um** for $C'_{fringe}$.

**RC optimization strategies for layout:**

| Action | Effect on R | Effect on C | Effect on RC |
|--------|-------------|-------------|--------------|
| Reduce wire length $l$ | Proportional decrease | Proportional decrease | Drops as $1/l^2$ (quadratic improvement) |
| Increase wire width $w$ | Decreases (proportional to $1/w$) | Increases (but only $C_{plate}$, not $C_{fringe}$) | Net decrease (R reduction dominates) |
| Use upper metal layers | Depends on layer $R_{\square}$ | Decreases (larger $d$) | Generally decreases |

The quadratic benefit of reducing line length makes **wire length minimization** the single most effective RC optimization.

**Differential-pair routing:**

External disturbances are another source of signal distortion. For sensitive analog signals, **differential signaling** provides robust noise rejection:

- The signal is inverted and carried on a **second wire routed in parallel** close to the original signal wire.
- Both wires pick up the same external noise.
- At the receiver, the inverted signal is subtracted from the original: the true signal is **doubled** while all common-mode disturbances are **cancelled out**.
- The effect can be further improved by **twisting** the two interconnects.
- This technique is discussed in more detail in Chapter 5, Sect. 5.3.3.

#### 7.3.3 Crosstalk

Beyond capacitances to the substrate, there are significant **coupling capacitances** between neighboring interconnects:

- **$C_{lat}$**: Lateral coupling between adjacent wires on the same metal layer.
- **$C_{vert}$**: Vertical coupling between wires on different metal layers.

These coupling capacitances cause **crosstalk** -- unintended signal transfer between wires that disturbs circuit operation. As IC feature sizes shrink, wire spacings decrease and these parasitics grow worse. Modern **Damascene processes** produce very narrow and tall interconnect cross-sections, making $C_{lat}$ **more significant** than $C_{vert}$ in state-of-the-art processes (the opposite was true in older processes).

**Key guidelines for crosstalk reduction in physical design:**

**1. Analog and digital isolation:**
- Steep signal edges (typical of digital signals) contain high frequencies that strongly disturb capacitively coupled wires (due to the AC reactance $R_C = |1/\omega C|$).
- Sensitive **analog signals** (e.g., sensor signals) should be routed **at a distance from digital signals**.
- **Clock nets** are the biggest culprits for generating crosstalk.

**2. Shielding:**
- If physical isolation is insufficient, crosstalk can be minimized by enclosing sensitive interconnects with **grounded wires** that act as shields.
- Three shielding options are available:
  - **Lateral shielding only** -- grounded wires on either side within the same metal layer.
  - **Vertical shielding only** -- grounded planes on metal layers above and/or below.
  - **All-round shielding** -- combination of lateral and vertical shielding for maximum protection.

**3. Avoiding minimum spacings:**
- While interlevel oxide (ILO) thickness cannot be changed in layout, **lateral wire spacing** within a layer can be increased.
- Critical interconnects (both aggressor "culprits" and victim "sensitive" nets) should use **greater than minimum spacing** when the footprint permits.
- After routing all nets, it is good practice to use available **whitespace** to extend or adjust wire spacing for improved isolation.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Sections 7.2 and 7.3 complete the trilogy of parasitic effects that layout designers must manage: bulk effects ([[7.1 Parasitic Effects in Silicon]]), surface effects (this section), and interconnect parasitics (this section). Together, these three categories represent the major sources of unintended electrical behavior that arise from the physical realization of a circuit. Understanding these parasitics is essential because they directly influence decisions made during placement (minimizing wire lengths for IR drop and RC delay) and routing (layer assignment, wire sizing, spacing, shielding, and differential-pair techniques). The next section, [[7.4 Overvoltage Protection]], shifts focus from temporary malfunctions to preventing irreversible damage from electrostatic discharge (ESD) and antenna effects -- moving from performance degradation to catastrophic failure prevention.
