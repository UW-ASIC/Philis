# 7.1 Parasitic Effects in Silicon

> **Chapter context**: Addressing Reliability in Physical Design

Section 7.1 covers the major parasitic effects that arise in the bulk silicon of integrated circuits. These are not intentional circuit elements but unavoidable consequences of having multiple differently-doped regions on the same substrate. Understanding and suppressing them through physical design is critical for reliable chip operation.

## Key Concepts

### 7.1.1 Substrate Debiasing

The p-doped substrate serves as the reference ground potential (GND/VSS) for all circuits on a chip. When a parasitic current $I_{sub}$ flows through the substrate, it encounters the substrate's finite track resistance $R_{sub}$, producing an unwanted voltage rise:

$$V = R_{sub} \cdot I_{sub} \quad \text{(Eq. 7.1)}$$

This local increase in ground potential is called **substrate debiasing**. It disrupts the assumption that ground is at a uniform 0 V everywhere on the chip, which can cause:

- **Malfunction of devices** whose operating point depends on a stable ground reference
- **Forward biasing of junction isolation (JI) diodes**, which should remain reverse-biased. If the local ground rises above the n-well potential, the JI diode turns on and injects minority carriers (see 7.1.2)
- **Ground bounce** -- the fluctuating ground creates interference through parasitic junction capacitances at the JI diodes, causing crosstalk that modulates adjacent circuitry
- **Increased noise** from substrate current flow

**Sources of substrate current**: A common cause is NPN transistors operated in saturation mode (collector potential as low as possible, to minimize losses in power transistors). When the base-collector diode becomes forward biased, it acts as a base-emitter diode in a parasitic PNP transistor, injecting holes into the n-well (parasitic PNP base) and ultimately into the p-substrate and p-epi, producing the substrate current $I_{sub}$.

**Why p+-substrate with p--epi is used**: A heavily doped (low-resistance) p+-substrate minimizes the IR drop from Eq. 7.1. However, devices require lightly doped silicon for their redopings to work, so a lightly doped p--epitaxial layer is grown on top. This dual-layer structure (p+-sub / p--epi) is a standard feature of many processes.

#### Counter Measures in Physical Design

- **Substrate contacts**: Provide as many substrate contacts as possible so that substrate currents can flow efficiently into the metal interconnect (which has very low resistance) and from there to the ground bond pad. The track resistance to the nearest substrate contact must be small enough to capture most of the current before it spreads into the substrate depths (chips can be up to ~1 mm thick).

- **Design rules (spacings)**: PDKs typically prescribe a maximum spacing between neighboring substrate contacts, enforcing a minimum density of contacts across the entire chip.

- **Guard rings**: When a substrate current source is known, substrate contacts are placed in its immediate vicinity. The best configuration completely surrounds the current injector with a ring of substrate contacts -- a **guard ring**.

- **"Currentless" SUB net**: The circuit ground net (GND/VSS) should *not* be used for substrate contacts. Instead, a dedicated SUB net (connected to the ground bond pad via a star point, as described in [[6.1-6.2 Isolation and Biasing Techniques]]) should be used. No current-carrying device pins connect to this SUB net -- only the (orders-of-magnitude smaller) parasitic currents flow through it. This prevents circuit operating currents from raising the ground potential.

### 7.1.2 Injection of Minority Carriers

The JI diodes isolate n-wells (cathode) from the p-doped region (anode) by remaining permanently reverse-biased. This polarity can be disrupted in two ways:

1. **Ground potential rises above the well potential** -- caused by substrate debiasing (7.1.1)
2. **Well potential drops below ground** -- caused by transients from chip-external inductances (e.g., motor windings, bonding wire inductance) that "pull" the well potential below ground

When the JI diode becomes forward-biased, it conducts an unwanted current $I$ flowing from chip ground through the substrate into the affected well. More critically, the forward-biased JI diode **emits electrons into the p-doped region** -- these electrons are minority carriers in p-type material.

#### The Parasitic NPN Transistor Model

The process is described in five steps:

1. **External pull-down**: An external inductance pulls the n-well potential below ground
2. **Forward bias**: The JI diode at the affected n-well ("culprit") becomes forward biased
3. **Culprit emits electrons**: The culprit n-well acts as the emitter of a parasitic NPN transistor
4. **Electrons diffuse**: Injected electrons spread by diffusion through the p-epi (the parasitic base)
5. **Victims collect electrons**: Neighboring n-wells (victims), whose JI diodes are correctly reverse-biased, have space-charge regions that attract and collect the diffusing electrons, forming unwanted leakage currents

The **current gain** $B$ of this parasitic NPN transistor depends on the parasitic base width and doping:

- **B > 1** is possible for directly adjacent wells (small base width); it can reach **B = 10** with injecting n-type buried layers (NBL) in BiCMOS/BCD
- The lightly doped p-epi is a "good" parasitic base (few holes for recombination), allowing electrons to travel long distances
- Even if only a few parts per thousand of the emitter current reach a distant well, this can disrupt sensitive analog circuits operating at the uA scale
- The small space-charge region at the p-epi/p-sub boundary *repels* electrons back into the p-epi, keeping them in the low-recombination layer and increasing their travel distance

#### Counter Measures in Physical Design

- **Identify possible injectors**: All n-wells directly connected to a chip pad are at high risk. Power transistors driving external actuators are typical culprits. Even bonding wire inductance can be sufficient with steep pulse edges.

- **Substrate contacts around the culprit**: Supply holes for recombination with diffusing electrons, weakening the parasitic base.

- **Spacing**: Larger well spacings increase the parasitic base width and recombination rate, but at the cost of area. Not very effective in lightly doped p-epi.

- **Increase p-doping**: Higher p-doping increases the recombination rate. In standard CMOS, the only option is substrate contacts. In BiCMOS, base doping is possible but not very penetrative. A heavily doped p-substrate (as in BCD processes) is effective underneath the n-wells.

- **Electron-collecting guard rings**: Additional n-wells (with no circuit function) placed around the culprit act as "deputy collectors". Their reverse-biased JI diodes attract diffusing electrons before they reach victim wells. Effectiveness increases with:
  - (i) Higher electrical potential (spreads the space-charge region / field catchment area)
  - (ii) Higher doping strength (enables low-resistance current discharge)
  - (iii) Greater width
  - (iv) Greater depth (increases catchment area)

  In **BiCMOS**, all available n-doping variants are used: NBL for maximum depth, Deep-n+ for low-resistance discharge. In **standard CMOS**, the NSD region (not the n-well, whose track resistance is too high) is used at maximum potential. There is a risk of saturation if doping is too low -- the auxiliary collector can itself become a parasitic emitter.

- **Placement at the chip boundary**: Culprits are typically placed near bond pads and at the chip periphery, making shielding easier. The guard ring can be stretched to the chip boundary so that only 3 sides (or 2 in a corner) need coverage.

- **Complete guard ring layout**: An inner **hole-providing guard ring** (PSD substrate contacts tied to GND) supplies holes for recombination. An outer **electron-collecting guard ring** (NSD tied to VDD) collects diffusing electrons. Both rings surround the aggressive n-well.

### 7.1.3 Latchup

Latchup is an unwanted low-resistance path triggered by the interaction between two complementary parasitic bipolar junction transistors ($Q_{NPN}$ and $Q_{PNP}$) formed by an n-p-n-p doping sequence. The most common occurrence is between adjacent NMOS and PMOS transistors -- a ubiquitous configuration in both digital (inverters) and analog (amplifiers, circuit breakers) circuits.

#### The Parasitic Structure

In a typical CMOS inverter:

| Doping Layer | Role in Parasitic Structure |
|---|---|
| NSD (NMOS source) | Emitter of $Q_{NPN}$ |
| p-substrate | Base of $Q_{NPN}$ / Collector of $Q_{PNP}$ |
| n-well | Collector of $Q_{NPN}$ / Base of $Q_{PNP}$ |
| PSD (PMOS source) | Emitter of $Q_{PNP}$ |

The two parasitic transistors are linked through the dual roles played by the n-well and p-substrate (they simultaneously serve as base for one transistor and collector for the other). The parasitic track resistances of these regions are labeled $R_{well}$ and $R_{sub}$.

#### The Positive Feedback Loop

1. A trigger event causes sufficient forward voltage at the base-emitter diode of $Q_{NPN}$ (or $Q_{PNP}$)
2. Minority carriers are injected into the parasitic base (p-substrate or n-well)
3. The resulting parasitic collector current produces an IR drop across $R_{well}$ (or $R_{sub}$)
4. This IR drop forward-biases the base-emitter diode of the *complementary* transistor
5. The complementary transistor turns on and its collector current causes an IR drop that further biases the first transistor

This is a **regenerative feedback loop**. If the product of current gains satisfies:

$$B_{NPN} \cdot B_{PNP} \geq 1$$

the current flow becomes self-sustaining. Two parallel current paths (electrons and holes) form between VDD and VSS. This state can only be eliminated by removing the external power supply. In the worst case, the resulting current **destroys the circuit**.

#### Latchup Triggers

- **Supply spikes**: If VDD is pulled up or VSS is pulled down, transient charging current flows through the junction capacitance $C_{JI}$ at the JI diode between n-well and p-sub. The IR drop across $R_{sub}$ or $R_{well}$ activates the parasitic transistors.
- **Coupling signals with steep edges**: At an inverter input, steep edges feed through parasitic gate-drain capacitors and drain-backgate capacitances ($C^N_{DB}$, $C^P_{DB}$) directly to the output. The resulting overshoot/undershoot produces sufficient IR drop to trigger latchup.

#### Counter Measures in Physical Design

- **Spacing**: Enlarging the spacing between NMOS and PMOS transistors increases the parasitic base width of $Q_{NPN}$ (lateral transistor), reducing $B_{NPN}$. Note: this does *not* help for the vertical $Q_{PNP}$, whose base width is determined by the n-well depth and cannot be altered by layout. Other device types can be placed in the gap.

- **Low-resistance backgate connections**: The most effective approach. Reduce $R_{well}$ and $R_{sub}$ so that the IR drop can never reach the forward-bias threshold of the parasitic base-emitter diodes (effectively short-circuiting them). This means always contacting the backgates (n-wells and p-sub) with metal as close as possible to the transistors. An excellent option is placing contacts directly at the interface between the NMOS and PMOS regions.

- **Guard rings**: The best prevention. Ring-fence NMOS transistors with substrate contacts (PSD) and PMOS transistors with n-well contacts (NSD). Many PDKs include device generators that automatically create these guard rings. The closed NSD and PSD rings should be fully contacted with Metal1 to minimize resistance -- metal is interrupted only for the drain contact.

- **Modern processes**: In advanced CMOS, the doping levels deployed mean there is no risk of latchup severely damaging the chip. However, even small fault currents can cause malfunctions in analog circuits, so suppression remains important.

### 7.1.4 Breakdown Voltage (Blocking Capability) of p-n Junctions

Most p-n junctions on a chip are reverse-biased during normal operation. Each has a specific breakdown voltage $V_{BD}$ that must not be exceeded. The circuit designer is primarily responsible for compliance, and the PDK defines device properties and design rules that assure blocking capabilities between devices.

The layout designer has limited room to maneuver, but in some cases can influence breakdown voltage through layout measures (see [[6.1-6.2 Isolation and Biasing Techniques]] for detailed guidelines). The fundamental rules are:

- **Breakdown voltage decreases with increasing dopant concentration** on either side of the junction
- **High blocking capability requires that at least one side of the junction be lightly doped**

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Section 7.1 establishes the foundational understanding of parasitic effects in bulk silicon that the layout designer must contend with -- substrate debiasing, minority carrier injection, latchup, and junction breakdown. These effects arise from the very same doping structures that enable device isolation (covered in [[6.1-6.2 Isolation and Biasing Techniques]]) and are therefore unavoidable; they can only be managed through careful physical design. The counter measures introduced here -- substrate contacts, guard rings, backgate connections, spacing rules, and the "currentless" SUB net -- form a design methodology that recurs throughout subsequent reliability topics. The next sections ([[7.2-7.3 Surface Effects and Interconnect Parasitics]]) extend this analysis from the silicon bulk to the chip surface and interconnect layers, where parasitic channels, hot-carrier effects, and interconnect-related parasitics present additional reliability challenges.
