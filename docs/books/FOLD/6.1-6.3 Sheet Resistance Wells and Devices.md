# 6.1-6.3 Sheet Resistance, Wells, and Devices

> **Chapter context**: Special Layout Techniques for Analog IC Design

## Key Concepts

### 6.1 Sheet Resistance: Calculating with Squares

Sheet resistance is the fundamental abstraction that lets layout designers estimate parasitic resistance without knowing layer thickness or resistivity directly. The idea: any conductive layer has a characteristic **sheet resistance** $R_\square$ (in ohms per square), and the resistance of a rectangular strip of that layer is:

$$R = R_\square \cdot \frac{l}{w}$$

where $l$ is the length (direction of current flow) and $w$ is the width (perpendicular to current flow). The ratio $l/w$ is dimensionless and represents the **number of squares** the strip contains. A square's physical size does not matter -- a 2x2 square and a 1x1 square both contribute one $R_\square$.

**Practical rules and caveats:**

- **Corners**: At right-angle bends, current flow is inhomogeneous. The convention is to count corner squares as **half a square**. For a wire with two 90-degree bends, the resistance is reduced by approximately one full square relative to a straight wire of the same total length and width.
- **Nominal values only**: This square-counting method gives nominal estimates. Real resistance values can deviate significantly due to process tolerances.
- **Contacts and vias**: Current flows vertically through contact holes and vias, creating inhomogeneous current distributions. The resistance contribution of these entry/exit points is handled separately and is typically specified in the PDK per-contact or per-via.

### 6.2 Wells

#### 6.2.1 Implementation

Wells are doped regions in an IC that provide **electrical isolation** for one or more devices. Starting from a lightly p-doped substrate, there are three main methodologies for creating isolated wells:

1. **Complementary doping** -- The existing substrate is doped with the opposite conductivity type at desired locations. This creates n-wells in a p-substrate (or p-wells in an n-substrate). Examples include simple n-wells, p-wells, and combined twin-well configurations.

2. **Enclosure with a barrier** -- A region is surrounded by a barrier of complementary doping. For example, a p-well can be isolated inside an n-type enclosure formed by an **n-buried layer (NBL)** at the bottom and a **Deep-n+ (sinker)** on the sides. This creates a "tank" or "tub" that can sit at any potential.

3. **Dielectric encapsulation (SOI)** -- Silicon-on-insulator technology uses buried oxide layers to galvanically isolate wells from their surroundings, providing the strongest isolation.

Methods (1) and (2) rely on **junction isolation (JI)** -- the resulting p-n junctions must be reverse-biased to maintain isolation. Modern processes commonly combine **STI (shallow trench isolation)** for lateral isolation with junction isolation at the bottom.

**Substrate biasing**: In a p-substrate process, the substrate is tied to the lowest potential (GND, 0 V). A dedicated **"SUB" net** with star routing topology is recommended -- separate from the current-carrying GND net -- to prevent IR-drop from locally raising the substrate potential. The star point connects SUB and GND directly at the bond pad, ensuring nearly zero current flows through the substrate tie-down network.

When an n-well holds a p-well (as in a tank/tub configuration), the n-well is typically connected to the highest potential (VDD) so the enclosed p-well can operate at any potential. Electrical connections to wells are made through heavily doped contact regions (NSD for n-type, PSD for p-type).

#### 6.2.2 Breakdown Voltage

The **breakdown voltage** of a well determines the maximum voltage difference it can sustain before the isolating junction fails. Two key rules for junction-isolated wells:

- **Breakdown voltage decreases with increasing dopant concentration.** Higher doping narrows the space-charge (depletion) region and concentrates the electric field, leading to earlier breakdown.
- **High blocking capability requires at least one lightly doped side.** When one side of the p-n junction is lightly doped, the space-charge region can spread into the lightly doped region, distributing the electric field over a larger volume and raising the breakdown voltage.

The physics: at a p-n junction, majority carriers diffuse across and recombine, leaving behind ionized dopant atoms that form a **space-charge region**. The electric field generated by these stationary charges opposes further diffusion and provides the isolating effect. Lighter doping means fewer ionized atoms per unit volume, so the depletion region must extend further to balance the charge -- this spreading raises the voltage the junction can withstand.

#### 6.2.3 Voltage-Dependent Spacing Rules

In processes using lateral junction isolation, the **spacing between wells depends on the operating voltage**. This arises because:

- Doping concentration is highest near the silicon surface and decreases with depth.
- The space-charge region spreads mainly in the lightly doped p-substrate surrounding the wells.
- Higher well voltages cause larger space-charge regions, which require greater inter-well spacing.

The **design rule spacing** between two n-wells is composed of:
1. Outdiffusion on each side (2x)
2. Space-charge region expansion on each side (voltage-class dependent)
3. An electrical safety spacing between the two space-charge regions

In automotive electronics, where higher-voltage processes are common, wells are classified by voltage and spacing rules are derived from these classifications. Wells with **trench isolation** (galvanic) can be packed much more densely because they do not require voltage-dependent spacing -- the deep vertical p-n junctions allow space-charge regions to expand vertically, and the dopant concentrations in these wells are generally much lower.

### 6.3 Devices: Layout, Connection, and Sizing

This section covers the layout, electrical connection, and sizing of the four most important device types in a standard CMOS process. The layouts shown are from the GPDK180 PDK (a generic 180 nm CMOS process).

#### 6.3.1 Field-Effect Transistors (MOS-FETs)

**Construction**: Both NMOS-FETs and PMOS-FETs consist of source and drain doped regions (NSD for NMOS, PSD for PMOS) separated by a polysilicon gate over a thin gate oxide. A conductive channel forms between source and drain when a suitable gate-to-bulk voltage is applied.

**Sizing parameters**:
- **Width $w$**: Defined by the "Active" layer dimension perpendicular to current flow (the field-oxide opening).
- **Length $l$**: Defined by the "Poly" layer dimension in the direction of current flow. The gate defines the source-drain spacing via a self-alignment process.

**Effective dimensions**: The electrically effective channel dimensions differ from the drawn dimensions:

$$l_{eff} = l - dl$$
$$w_{eff} = w - dw$$

The correction factor $dw$ is negligible for STI processes but significant in older LOCOS processes due to the bird's beak effect, where oxide grows laterally under the nitride mask, shortening the effective channel width by approximately twice the field-oxide thickness.

**Source/drain assignment**: Due to the symmetric layout, which terminal is source and which is drain is determined by the circuit wiring. Source and backgate are typically at the same potential, so their contacts are placed adjacent to each other.

##### Folding Field-Effect Transistors

FETs with very large $w/l$ ratios (needed for large currents or low on-resistance) would have extremely unfavorable aspect ratios if laid out as a single device. **Folding** solves this by splitting the transistor into $n$ subtransistors, each with the same length $l$ but width $w/n$, connected in parallel.

**Folding procedure** (example: $w=20$, $l=2$, $n=4$):
1. **Split** into $n$ subtransistors of width $w/n = 5$
2. **Flip** alternating subtransistors so adjacent source/drain regions face each other
3. **Pack** by merging (sharing) adjacent source or drain diffusions that are at the same potential
4. **Route** all gates, sources, and drains in parallel

The poly gates created by folding are called **fingers**. Terminology caution: the "width" of a finger refers to the channel length $l$, while the "finger length" corresponds to the channel width direction.

**Benefits of folding**:
- **Compact layout** with near-quadratic aspect ratio
- **Area savings** from shared diffusions
- **Reduced gate resistance**: Folding by $n$ reduces gate resistance by a factor of $1/n^2$

The gate resistance is estimated as:

$$R_G = R_{\square,poly} \cdot \frac{l_G}{l} \bigg/ 2$$

The division by 2 accounts for the linear decrease in charging current $I_G$ along the finger -- unlike a standard resistor, the gate current does not flow through the full finger length. With $R_{\square,poly}$ typically in the double-digit ohms range, even 20 squares can yield $R_G = 0.5\,\text{k}\Omega$. PDKs therefore constrain the maximum finger length or number of squares. Gate resistance can be further halved by energizing the gate from both ends.

**Layout best practices**: Source and drain terminals should be connected by metal with as many contacts as possible to ensure uniform current distribution and minimize the parasitic on-resistance $R_{DS,on}$.

#### 6.3.2 Resistors

##### Diffusion Resistors

Any conductive layer can be used to make passive resistors. The three main diffusion resistor types are:

- **NSD resistor**: Located in the p-well (tied to ground with the p-substrate). No additional isolation needed; no extra pin required in the schematic.
- **Nwell resistor**: Encapsulated by p-substrate. Has significantly **higher sheet resistance** than NSD/PSD resistors -- not just because of lighter doping, but because the field oxide (STI) depletes the upper, more heavily doped portion of the well, forcing current through the lower, more resistive region.
- **PSD resistor**: Situated in an n-well with a third pin ("Well") for biasing. The Nwell-PSD diode must be reverse-biased, so the well potential must be at least as high as the higher of the two resistor terminal potentials. Two approaches:
  - (i) Connect Nwell to VDD (highest circuit potential)
  - (ii) Connect Nwell to the higher-potential resistor terminal (simpler routing, more precisely defined sheet resistance since the depletion region becomes independent of the resistor voltage, but only works for DC)

##### Poly Resistors

Polysilicon resistors are the **most commonly used** type because they offer:
- **Galvanic isolation** from other devices (no p-n junction parasitics)
- **Higher breakdown voltage** (limited by the surrounding dielectric, not a junction)
- **Space savings** (no wells or space-charge regions needed)
- **Lower temperature coefficient** than diffusion resistors
- Many squares can be packed in a small area due to thin patterning

##### Sizing Resistors

The resistance of a real resistor includes the **resistor head** contribution -- the regions at both ends where current enters/exits vertically through contacts and must change direction, creating inhomogeneous flow:

$$R_i = R_\square \cdot \frac{l_i}{w_i} + 2 R_H(w_i)$$

where $l_i$ is the body length (homogeneous current region), $w_i$ is the width, and $R_H(w_i)$ is the resistance of one resistor head (a function of width, since more contacts are used for wider resistors). The factor of 2 accounts for both ends. PDK cell generators automatically handle this calculation.

#### 6.3.3 Capacitors

The capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor is:

$$C = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r \cdot \frac{A}{d}$$

On-chip area is precious, so thin dielectrics (small $d$) are essential for achieving useful capacitance values.

##### MOS-FET Capacitors (NMOS-Cap, PMOS-Cap)

The gate oxide is an excellent capacitor dielectric -- very thin and high quality. A MOS-FET can serve as a capacitor by using the gate-to-backgate capacitance. A common configuration short-circuits source, drain, and backgate contacts to form one electrode, with the gate as the other.

**Electrical limitations**:
- Should **not** be operated near the threshold voltage $V_{th}$ (depletion mode), as capacitance drops significantly
- Can operate in reverse mode (channel by accumulation of minority carriers) or accumulation mode (further accumulation of majority carriers), but reverse mode with NMOS-Cap is impractical since the p-substrate is tied to 0 V (would need negative voltages)
- Capacitance in reverse mode **collapses at high frequencies**
- Best performance when the bulk electrode is connected to AC ground

##### Poly-Nwell Capacitor

Uses only the Nwell layer as the counter electrode to polysilicon, with gate oxide as dielectric. Effectively a PMOS-FET with no source or drain. Main drawbacks: high parasitic body resistance of the Nwell electrode and a series parasitic capacitance to ground from the Nwell-substrate depletion region.

##### PIP and MIM Capacitors

Process extensions enable superior capacitors:
- **PIP caps (poly-insulator-poly)**: Additional thin oxide layers above gate oxide with additional poly electrodes
- **MIM caps (metal-insulator-metal)**: Located between metallization layers; require good planarization (CMP). Benefit from minimal parasitic effects due to using metal electrodes and distance from the silicon surface

**Sizing**: Capacitors are sized by defining the electrode area $A = w \times l$. Rectangular plates are preferred; the device is stretched horizontally and vertically to achieve the required area.

Modern processes also use **high-k dielectrics** (higher permittivity than SiO$_2$), though typically not to increase capacitance but to allow greater dielectric thickness $d$ (reducing leakage currents while maintaining capacitance).

#### 6.3.4 Bipolar Transistors

Bipolar transistors exploit phenomena at two consecutive p-n junctions separated by a very thin region (the base). Two types exist: NPN and PNP.

##### NPN Transistors

Modern NPN transistors are built by extending CMOS processes with additional layers: **Deep-n+**, **NBL** (n-type buried layer), and **Base**. These are called **BICMOS processes**.

**Operating principle**: When the base-emitter diode (NSD/Base junction) is forward-biased, the emitter injects electrons into the base. These minority carriers diffuse through the base and are swept by the electric field of the reverse-biased base-collector junction into the collector. They exit through the low-impedance path provided by the heavily doped NBL and Deep-n+ to the collector pin. This is the collector current $I_C$.

Some electrons recombine with holes in the base (and holes injected from base into emitter), producing the base current $I_B$. The **current gain** $B = I_C / I_B$ can exceed 100.

**Design principles for high current gain**:
- **Low base doping** -- fewer holes available for recombination
- **Short base width** -- defined by the difference between two diffusion depths (Base and NSD), which is a process parameter not affected by layout

The heavily doped Deep-n+ (sinker) and NBL minimize the parasitic collector body resistance, which would otherwise be very large in the lightly doped n-well environment.

**Sizing**: The NPN transistor's current capacity is proportional to the **emitter area**, defined by the active region opening. Due to the **current crowding effect** (more current is injected near the base contact where base-emitter voltage is slightly higher), the emitter should be stretched only perpendicular to the base contact direction. For larger currents, the basic emitter cell is **replicated** (integer sizing with multi-emitter layout) rather than continuously stretched, to avoid nonlinearities and to improve matching.

##### PNP Transistors

The substrate-PNP (collector = p-substrate) is seldom used because substrate currents alter the ground potential via IR drop.

A **horizontal PNP transistor** can be built without additional layers using concentric rings in the Nwell with p-doping (Base layer):
- **Inner ring**: Emitter
- **Outer ring**: Collector
- **Between rings**: Lightly doped n-well (base)
- **Base width**: Defined by the difference in ring radii (patterned with the same mask, so alignment tolerances do not affect it)
- The emitter and collector regions must **not** be separated by STI, since the main current (holes) flows radially

These transistors achieve current gains of $B \approx 50$ or higher (98-99 out of 100 holes reach the collector). This is remarkable because many holes are injected downward into the base. The **NBL plays a critical role**: a small space-charge region forms at the Nwell-NBL interface due to the extreme difference in dopant concentrations, and this region **repels** downward-diffusing holes back toward the collector ring.

**Sizing**: The basic ring layout (radiuses) must remain unchanged to preserve electrical behavior (especially current gain $B$). The transistor can only be sized for larger currents by **replicating** the entire basic layout cell.
