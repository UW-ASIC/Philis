# 2.1-2.3 IC Fabrication Fundamentals, Silicon, and Photolithography

> **Chapter context**: Technology Know-How: From Silicon to Devices

## Key Concepts

### 2.1 Fundamentals of IC Fabrication

Integrated circuits are manufactured on thin, monocrystalline slices of semiconductor material called **wafers**. Many ICs are produced simultaneously on a single wafer, arranged in rows and columns. At the end of fabrication, individual ICs are **singulated** -- cut apart by vertical cuts perpendicular to one another -- yielding small rectangular plates called **chips**. A single wafer can yield from hundreds to tens of thousands of chips, depending on wafer size and circuit dimensions.

Processing a wafer requires typically **several hundred** individual manufacturing steps executed in series. Fabrication in a wafer fabrication facility (a **fab**) can take months. All of these steps fall into three fundamental categories:

- **Doping**: Implanting dopants (acceptors or donors) into the wafer to create regions of p- or n-conductivity. Doping is generally performed selectively -- only certain parts of the wafer surface are affected. The results are laterally structured doped areas and vertical doping profiles.
- **Deposition**: Adding new layers (e.g., silicon dioxide, metal) onto the wafer surface. The wafer surface "grows" with each deposition. In most cases, deposition affects the entire wafer surface, with some exceptions such as local oxidation.
- **Removal**: Removing material, normally by **etching** (chemical means). A layer is structured in many cases by etching. Global removal can be done by bright etching (purely chemical) or by **CMP** (chemical-mechanical polishing), which levels the wafer surface using both chemical and mechanical action.

#### Structuring

When any of these three manipulations must be applied selectively to produce lateral structures, **structuring** is required. The manipulations are restricted to desired regions by protecting certain areas against the process effects -- these are called **masked processes**. The structuring for masking is produced by **photolithography**.

### 2.2 Base Material Silicon

Silicon (Si) is the base material for the vast majority of chips. Its dominance is due to several key properties:

- **Ideal band gap of 1.1 eV**: This is high enough that intrinsic conduction does not trigger until temperatures exceed **200 C (392 F)**, meaning silicon remains stable for typical technical applications. At the same time, the band gap is low enough that field-effect transistors with very low threshold voltages can be easily manufactured.
- **Excellent native oxide (SiO2)**: Silicon dioxide is a very stable, intrinsic oxide with good insulating properties and is easy to manufacture. SiO2 serves as an insulator between interconnects and as a dielectric for capacitors and field-effect transistors.
- **Good thermal conductivity**: Critical for miniaturization, where power losses from minuscule structures must be quickly dissipated to prevent overheating, intrinsic conduction, or catastrophic damage.
- **Easily grown as large monocrystals**: Atoms in monocrystals are arranged with absolute regularity in all directions, without breaks. This is essential because lattice irregularities (grain boundaries and lattice defects) would cause unwanted current paths.

#### From Sand to Wafer

Silicon occurs naturally only in an oxidized and impure state, primarily as common sand. Processing involves:

1. **Purification**: Freeing silicon from bonded oxygen and removing impurities.
2. **Crystal growth**: Growing the purified silicon as a monocrystal by melting it and maintaining the temperature just above its melting point of **1414 C (2577 F)**. A small seed crystal (monocrystal) is placed on the molten surface. Silicon atoms from the melt attach to the seed and adopt its crystal structure. The crystal grows as the seed is slowly withdrawn.
3. **Doping** (optional): Doping agents can be added to the melt during crystal growth.

#### Crystal Growth Methods

Two principal technologies exist:

- **Czochralski process** (invented by Jan Czochralski in 1915): The monocrystal is drawn from a silicon melt while being rotated about the pulling axis. The crystal effectively grows from the bottom as it is drawn vertically upward.
- **Zone melting** (crucible-free): A very thin strip of a polycrystalline bar is melted by an annular heater. Atoms align within the molten zone. The heater moves slowly along the bar while the two rigid portions rotate in opposite directions, producing the monocrystal in place.

Both methods must be performed under vacuum or in an inert-gas atmosphere. The resulting monocrystal is a round bar. Wafers approximately **1 mm thick** are cut from the bar by **internal diameter sawing (IDS)**. Typical wafer diameters range from **200 to 450 mm**.

### 2.3 Photolithography

#### 2.3.1 Fundamentals

Photolithography is used in all structuring process steps. Its purpose is to transfer a two-dimensional image of required structures onto the wafer surface so that subsequent processing (implantation, etching, etc.) can be applied to restricted areas.

The basic photolithography sequence:

1. **Coat** the wafer with a thin radiation-sensitive film called a **photoresist** (or resist).
2. **Expose** through a **photomask** to project a black-and-white image of the desired structure onto the photoresist.
3. **Develop** -- the solubility of the photoresist changes in exposed regions with respect to a specific fluid called a **developer**. Soluble areas are washed away; insoluble parts remain.
4. The remaining photoresist pattern serves as a **mask** for the actual wafer process step.

Masking can work in two ways:

- **Resist mask**: The remaining photoresist itself acts as the mask in some process steps.
- **Hard mask**: When photoresist cannot directly serve as the mask, the pattern in the developed photoresist is first transferred by etching to an underlying layer, which then acts as the mask for the actual process step.

#### 2.3.2 Photoresist

Photoresist is deposited on the wafer by **spin coating**: liquid coating material is applied to the wafer center while the wafer is rapidly rotated. Centrifugal force distributes the fluid evenly, and the solvent is vaporized to produce a layer of constant thickness. Spraying is an alternative deposition method.

Photoresists are **radiation-reactive polymers** available in two types:

| Property | Positive Photoresist | Negative Photoresist |
|---|---|---|
| Effect of light exposure | Deconstruction of molecular chains | Greater cross-linking of molecular chains |
| Solubility change | Greatly **increased** | Greatly **decreased** |
| After development | Exposed regions **removed** | Exposed regions **remain** |

Photoresists can be engineered to respond only within a specific, tightly bounded wavelength range. The characteristics of exposure (wavelength, intensity, duration), photoresist (layer thickness, light sensitivity), and developer must all be finely tuned for proper imaging.

#### 2.3.3 Photomasks and Exposure

A **photomask** is a sheet of glass with a black-and-white image of the target structures applied as an opaque layer of **chromium**. When exposed to light, the mask casts a shadow onto the photoresist-coated wafer. Two types of exposure exist:

##### Direct Exposure

The photomask is placed above and in close proximity to the wafer:

- **Contact exposure**: Photomask in direct contact with the photoresist. Produces a 1:1 (full-scale) image by simple shadowing. Requires **1X photomasks** (structure sizes on mask = structure sizes on wafer). Unsuitable for volume manufacturing because it damages the resist and mask.
- **Proximity exposure**: A gap of **10-40 um** is maintained between photomask and resist. Avoids contact damage but the gap causes resolution degradation. Resolution limit is approximately **3 um**.

When structure dimensions approach the exposure wavelength, significant **diffraction phenomena** occur, limiting the minimum feature size achievable by direct exposure. Modern semiconductor technologies require features far smaller than this limit, so direct exposure is no longer used in state-of-the-art fabs.

##### Projection Exposure

The photomask pattern is projected through lenses onto the wafer, providing two key benefits:

1. **Physical separation** of photomask and wafer (no damage).
2. **Optical reduction** of the image during projection, improving imaging accuracy.

Common photomask reduction ratios: **4X, 5X, and 10X** (i.e., structures on the mask are 4, 5, or 10 times larger than the target features on the wafer).

Because lens and mask sizes are limited, only a small portion of the wafer can be exposed per shot. Photomasks for projection exposure therefore contain structures for only one or a few chips and are called **reticles**. (Technically, a reticle contains data for only part of the final exposed area, but the term has become synonymous with "photomask" in modern usage.)

The wafer is exposed in many individual shots using the **step-and-repeat technique**:
- The wafer is transported on a **positioner table** under the projection optics.
- The exposure equipment is called a **wafer stepper** (or simply **stepper**).

#### Exposure Wavelengths and Resolution Limits

**Ultraviolet light** is used for higher optical resolution. The widely used **193 nm argon-fluoride (ArF) laser** enables feature sizes down to approximately **10 nm** through advanced techniques -- entering the realm of **nanoelectronics**.

Further resolution improvements include:
- **Shorter UV wavelengths**: Require mirror systems (rather than lenses) because lens materials become increasingly opaque at shorter wavelengths.
- **Electron beam (e-beam) direct write**: All structures are individually "written" on the wafer. Very time-consuming, but economical for small lot sizes since no photomasks are needed.

#### 2.3.4 Alignment and Alignment Marks

Integrated devices are built progressively through a series of interacting structuring steps. Each new layer's reticle must be **accurately aligned** with the structures already present on the wafer. For example, contact holes must land precisely on device connection areas.

Before every exposure in the step-and-repeat process, the wafer's location and attitude must be adjusted relative to the reticle. This is done using **alignment marks** -- geometrical figures (such as crosses) placed on the reticle. These marks are automatically detected by optical means.

Key details about alignment:
- Alignment marks produce structures on the wafer according to the current process step. If these structures can be optically detected, the wafer position can be aligned for subsequent photomask exposures.
- Some structuring steps (e.g., ion implantation) leave **no reliable optical traces**. In these cases, the same alignment mark from a previous step is reused, or a newer alignment mark from a subsequent step is adopted.
- Reusing the same alignment mark as much as possible prevents **accumulation of mechanical tolerance errors**.
- **Angular orientation** must also be checked. Alignment marks are therefore placed at two points **far apart** on the reticle to ensure correct wafer attitude.
- Alignment marks are not functional chip structures. They are placed in the **scribe lines** (also called "sawing trenches" or "saw streets") -- the clearance of **50-100 um** between chips that facilitates subsequent chip dicing.

#### 2.3.5 Reference to Physical Design

The extreme miniaturization of modern IC features (nanometer-scale structure sizes) demands enormously expensive photomasks. A single design flaw can render photomasks and wafers worthless, resulting in:
- Significant financial losses from wasted masks and wafers.
- Development delays of **six months or more** for troubleshooting and new production runs.
- Further losses from delayed time-to-market.

The critical takeaway: **the physical design of an IC must be absolutely flawless**. Automated verification algorithms (covered in Chapters 3 and 5) and layout quality assurance measures (Chapters 6 and 7) are essential safeguards.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Sections 2.1-2.3 establish the foundational manufacturing context that every physical designer must understand. The three basic process categories (doping, deposition, removal) and the structuring mechanism of photolithography are the physical operations that layout geometries ultimately control. Understanding how photomasks translate layout data into wafer structures -- and the resolution limits, alignment tolerances, and cost implications involved -- explains why design rules exist and why layout verification is non-negotiable. These fundamentals set the stage for the next sections on imaging errors and oxide layers ([[2.4-2.5 Imaging Errors and Oxide Layers]]), which examine the deviations that arise between intended layout geometries and actual fabricated structures.
