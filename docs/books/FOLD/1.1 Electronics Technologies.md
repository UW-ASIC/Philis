# 1.1 Electronics Technologies

> **Chapter context**: Introduction -- This section surveys the three main families of fabrication technologies (PCB, hybrid, semiconductor) that underpin every electronic system. Understanding them is a prerequisite for the physical-design steps discussed later in the book.

## Key Concepts

All electronic circuits share two fundamental building blocks: **electronic devices** (transistors, resistors, capacitors, etc.) and the **metallic interconnects** that electrically connect them. What differs is *how* these elements are physically realised. The fabrication technologies fall into three main groups:

| Group | Sub-types |
|---|---|
| Printed circuit board (PCB) | Through-hole technology (THT), Surface-mount technology (SMT) |
| Hybrid technology | Thick-film, Thin-film, LTCC |
| Semiconductor technology | Discrete components, Integrated circuits (ICs) |

---

### 1.1.1 Printed Circuit Board (PCB) Technology

PCBs are the most widely used technology for electronic packaging. A PCB both **mechanically supports** and **electrically connects** the devices soldered onto it.

#### The Substrate Core

The basic element is a substrate core -- an electrically insulating board typically made of **glass-fibre-reinforced epoxy resin** (the familiar green board). Older, low-spec boards used phenolic-resin-stabilised paper, but these are rarely used today.

The substrate core serves two purposes:
1. **Mechanical foundation** for mounting electronic devices.
2. **Surface for interconnects** that electrically link those devices.

Interconnects are etched from a **copper** layer applied to one or both sides of the board. Copper is chosen because it is an excellent electrical conductor, etches cleanly, and solders easily.

#### Fabricating Interconnects by Photolithography

The interconnect fabrication process (Fig. 1.1) proceeds as follows:

1. **Coat with copper** -- a copper layer is deposited on the substrate core.
2. **Apply photoresist** -- a light-sensitive polymer layer is added on top of the copper.
3. **Position a mask** -- a transparent glass plate carrying an opaque pattern of the desired interconnect geometry is aligned above the board.
4. **Expose (illuminate)** -- UV light passes through the transparent regions of the mask and hits the photoresist. Exposed resist becomes chemically soluble in developer solution.
5. **Develop** -- the exposed (soluble) resist is washed away, leaving only the unexposed resist that mirrors the interconnect pattern.
6. **Etch** -- an etching agent removes the unprotected copper. The remaining resist "masks" the copper underneath, protecting it.
7. **Strip resist** -- the remaining photoresist is washed off, revealing the finished copper interconnect pattern.

For low-volume prototyping, interconnects are sometimes formed by **mechanically milling** the metal layer rather than etching.

#### Multi-Layer PCBs

Multiple substrate cores can be stacked to create **multilayer PCBs**. A typical example (Fig. 1.2) has three cores and six routing layers (top and bottom of each core). Cores are bonded together with a **prepreg** (bonding agent) that also electrically isolates the opposing copper layers to prevent short circuits.

Electrical connections between routing layers are made through **vias** -- plated-through holes whose walls are coated with copper. Vias come in three varieties:

- **Through-hole vias** -- pass through the entire board.
- **Blind vias** -- connect an outer layer to one or more inner layers but do not penetrate the full stack.
- **Buried vias** -- connect inner layers only and are invisible from the outside.

#### Mounting Technologies: THT vs. SMT

| Feature | Through-Hole Technology (THT) | Surface-Mount Technology (SMT) |
|---|---|---|
| Device connection | Leads inserted into through-hole vias and soldered | Metal pads soldered directly to the board surface |
| Device name | Through-hole devices (THDs) | Surface-mount devices (SMDs) |
| Packing density | Lower | Higher (smaller devices, both-side mounting possible) |
| Automated handling | More difficult | Easier for pick-and-place machines |

SMT is the dominant approach today because of its superior packing density and manufacturability. The two technologies can be mixed on a single board. Integrated circuits mounted on PCBs generally require a **package** (enclosure); bare dies can be mounted directly, but differential thermal expansion between semiconductor and board material makes this connection fragile.

### 1.1.2 Hybrid Technology

In hybrid technology, **some** components are discrete devices mounted on the carrier substrate while **other** components (resistors, capacitors, inductors) are **fabricated directly** on the substrate during the manufacturing process -- hence "hybrid."

#### Carrier Substrates

Common substrate materials include **ceramic**, **glass**, and **quartz**. These substrates support SMDs but *not* THDs, because no through holes are drilled for mounting.

#### Interconnect Formation

Two approaches are used:

| Method | Process | Typical trace thickness |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick-film** | Conductive paste applied by **silk-screen printing**, then burnt in | Several micrometres |
| **Thin-film** | Conductive material **vapour-deposited or sputtered** onto the substrate, then patterned by photolithography | Sub-micrometre |

Both approaches allow tuning the **electrical conductivity** of deposited layers over a wide range, enabling the creation of **printed resistors** alongside interconnects. Resistance values can be fine-tuned by **laser trimming** -- cutting perpendicular to current flow to increase resistance to the target value.

**Interconnect crossings** and **capacitors** can be realised by alternately stacking conductive and insulating layers. Capacitors can also be created with interdigitated **comb-like** structures within a single metal layer (printed capacitors).

#### LTCC Technology (Low Temperature Co-Fired Ceramics)

LTCC is a widely used thick-film hybrid variant. Its fabrication process (Fig. 1.3) is representative of many hybrid technologies:

1. **(a) Green sheets** -- fabrication starts with flexible films of ceramic powder bonded with organic binders (not a pre-made rigid ceramic substrate).
2. **(b) Via punching** -- holes for vias are mechanically punched into each sheet.
3. **(c) Via filling** -- holes are filled with conductive paste.
4. **(d) Conductor printing** -- interconnect geometries and buried passive components (resistors, capacitors, inductors) are silk-screen printed with conductive paste.
5. **(e) Stacking and lamination** -- green sheets are stacked and lightly heated to bond them together.
6. **(f) Cutting, burnout, and sintering** -- the stack is cut to size, pressed, and fired in an oven. Organic binders burn out and the ceramic sinters into a rigid plate containing multiple interconnect layers. Pressure is maintained so that shrinkage occurs almost entirely in the **z-axis**, preserving lateral dimensions.
7. **(g) Post-firing** -- resistors and contact pads for SMDs/ICs are printed on the surface and burnt in.
8. **(h) Component mounting** -- SMDs are fixed with conductive glue or reflow soldering. ICs can be mounted as **bare dies** (unpackaged) because ceramic and silicon have similar thermal expansion coefficients. Bare dies are electrically connected via **bond wires** from chip pads to substrate pads.

#### Advantages of Hybrid over PCB Technology

- **Greater mechanical stability** -- withstands extreme shocks and vibrations (e.g., automotive applications).
- **Higher packing density** -- enabled by bare-die mounting.
- **Better thermal dissipation** -- hybrids can be mounted on heat sinks for full-surface thermal coupling; **thermal vias** (plated-through contacts designed specifically for heat transfer) further improve heat flow from top to bottom.

The main **disadvantage** is **higher manufacturing cost** compared to PCBs.

### 1.1.3 Semiconductor Technology

Unlike PCB and hybrid approaches, semiconductor technology builds the **entire electronic circuit as a single monolithic unit** -- all devices and interconnects are created during fabrication in a single semiconductor die ("chip"). This is the origin of the term **integrated circuit (IC)**.

Semiconductor technology also produces **discrete** (single) devices such as power diodes, transistors, and thyristors for high-current applications. Even these discrete devices are internally composed of many parallel sub-devices and often include hidden protective circuitry.

#### Physics of Semiconductors

**Conductivity and the band gap.** Electrical conduction requires free charge carriers (electrons). In solids, the relevant energy levels are organised into bands:

- **Valence band** -- the outermost occupied band; electrons here are bound.
- **Conduction band** -- the next higher band; electrons here can move freely and carry current.
- **Band gap** (E) -- the energy difference between the two bands.

| Material class | Band gap | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Conductors** (metals) | ~0 eV (bands overlap) | Many free electrons; excellent conductivity |
| **Semiconductors** | ~0.7--3.3 eV | Few free electrons at room temperature; conductivity increases exponentially with temperature |
| **Insulators** | >~4 eV | Virtually no free electrons; negligible conductivity |

Typical semiconductor band gaps at 300 K: **Ge** ~0.66 eV, **Si** ~1.1 eV, **GaAs** ~1.4 eV, **GaN** ~3.4 eV, **SiC** ~2.4--3.3 eV (lattice-dependent).

**Doping.** The conductivity of a semiconductor is controlled by introducing impurity atoms:

- **Donors** (5-valent atoms: P, As, Sb) -- contribute an extra electron to the conduction band. The energy level E_D sits just below E_C; at room temperature the electron easily enters the conduction band. Result: **n-doped** (negative charge carriers dominate).
- **Acceptors** (3-valent atoms: B, In, Al) -- create a hole in the valence band by accepting an electron from a neighbouring Si atom. The energy level E_A sits just above E_V. Result: **p-doped** (positive charge carriers -- holes -- dominate).

When n-doped and p-doped regions overlap, electrons and holes **recombine** and neutralise each other. The net conductivity type depends on which dopant is in surplus. The dominant charge carriers are called **majority carriers**; the less abundant are **minority carriers**.

#### From Wafer to Chip

Extremely pure, **monocrystalline** semiconductor material is required. It is grown as a crystal ingot, then sliced into thin **wafers**. A single wafer holds hundreds to tens of thousands of identical chips, all fabricated simultaneously. At the end of the process, individual rectangular **dies** are cut from the wafer.

**Silicon** is by far the most common material. Others are used for special purposes:

| Material | Application |
|---|---|
| GaAs, SiGe | RF circuits |
| GaN, SiC | Power electronics |

#### Integrated Devices and Interconnects: FEOL and BEOL

The fabrication of an IC (Fig. 1.7) has two main phases:

1. **Front-End-of-Line (FEOL)** -- Electronic devices are created by successive **doping operations** on the wafer surface. Simple processes use fewer than 10 doping steps; complex processes use more than 20. Between doping steps, additional layers of base material may be deposited by **epitaxy**. The doping process uses photolithographic masks just like PCB fabrication, but with feature sizes on the order of **nanometres**.

2. **Back-End-of-Line (BEOL)** -- Metallic interconnects are constructed by alternately depositing and patterning **insulating layers** and **metallic layers** on top of the doped surface. Through-contacts (**vias**) connect different metal layers; **contacts** connect the lowest metal layer to the doped regions below.

The electrically active portion of a chip occupies only about **1--2%** of the wafer thickness (the wafer itself is slightly less than 1 mm thick).

A critical difference from PCBs: devices on a chip are **never fully isolated** from one another. They are all part of the same monolithic semiconductor, so parasitic interactions between devices are inevitable and must be accounted for during design (covered in detail in Chapter 7).

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Section 1.1 lays the technological groundwork for the entire book. By surveying PCB, hybrid, and semiconductor fabrication, it establishes the physical substrate upon which all subsequent layout design decisions operate. The photolithographic process introduced here for PCBs reappears -- at nanometre scales -- in semiconductor fabrication, forming a conceptual bridge to the detailed IC technology discussion in [[1.2 Integrated Circuits]]. Understanding mounting technologies (THT/SMT), multilayer stacking, and the FEOL/BEOL distinction is essential before tackling the design rules, libraries, and design flows covered in Chapters 3--5. The parasitic device interactions mentioned at the end of this section foreshadow the reliability concerns addressed in Chapter 7.
