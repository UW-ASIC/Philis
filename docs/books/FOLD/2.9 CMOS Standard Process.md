# 2.9 CMOS Standard Process

> **Chapter context**: Technology Know-How: From Silicon to Devices

## Key Concepts

### 2.9.1 Field-Effect Transistor Fundamentals

The MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) is the workhorse of modern integrated circuits. Despite the name "MOS," the top gate layer has long been made of **polysilicon** rather than metal -- the historical name has simply persisted.

#### NMOS-FET Structure

The basic NMOS-FET consists of:

- **Source and Drain**: Two n-doped regions embedded in a p-type bulk (backgate). They are structurally identical; the naming is determined by which terminal is at lower potential.
- **Gate**: A conductive polysilicon layer situated above the backgate, separated from it by a thin **gate oxide (GOX)**.
- **Backgate (Bulk)**: The p-type semiconductor region between source and drain. Because the backgate has complementary conductivity to source/drain, two p-n junctions exist between source and drain, preventing current flow when no gate voltage is applied.

This is an **enhancement-type** (normally off) device -- no current flows without an applied gate voltage. It is by far the most widely used FET type.

#### CMOS Complementary Pair

- **NMOS-FET**: Current carried by electrons (n-type carriers). Backgate is p-doped.
- **PMOS-FET**: Current carried by holes (p-type carriers). Backgate is n-doped.
- **CMOS** ("Complementary MOS"): The technology provides both NMOS and PMOS transistors, complementary in their carrier types. These are **unipolar** transistors -- only one carrier type is responsible for current flow in each device.

#### Operating Principle: The Field Effect

When a voltage $V_{GB} > 0$ is applied between gate (G) and backgate (B), the gate-backgate structure acts like a **parallel-plate capacitor**. The electric field displaces holes from the p-type backgate surface, and electrons accumulate to form the countercharge. This behavior is governed by the **law of mass action**:

$$n \cdot p = n_i^2$$

where:
- $n$ = electron density
- $p$ = hole density
- $n_i \approx 10^{10}$ cm$^{-3}$ for silicon at room temperature (intrinsic carrier density)

This equation states that $n$ and $p$ are always inversely proportional. Visualized as a balance/scale: increasing one carrier type necessarily decreases the other. An undoped semiconductor has equal $n$ and $p$; doping tilts the balance.

#### Threshold Voltage and Channel Formation

As $V_{GB}$ increases, a critical point is reached -- the **threshold voltage $V_{th}$** -- where electrons outnumber holes at the surface. The surface layer has **inverted** from p-type to n-type, creating a conductive **channel** between source and drain. Applying a positive $V_{DS}$ then causes current to flow, and the channel resistance is modulated by $V_{GB}$.

The field strength in a parallel-plate capacitor is:

$$E = V / d$$

This leads to two critical design insights:

1. **Thinner gate oxide** $\Rightarrow$ stronger field for a given voltage $\Rightarrow$ lower $V_{th}$. This is why gate oxide thickness has been relentlessly scaled down in successive technology nodes.
2. **Lower backgate doping** $\Rightarrow$ less initial tilt of the carrier balance $\Rightarrow$ easier inversion $\Rightarrow$ lower $V_{th}$.

### 2.9.2 Process Options

To implement both NMOS and PMOS transistors, the process must provide both p-type and n-type bulk regions. Starting from a p-predoped substrate, three process options exist:

#### Single-Well Process

- Only one well type is created (e.g., an n-well in a p-substrate).
- The p-substrate serves directly as the NMOS backgate; the n-well is the PMOS backgate.
- **Problem**: Asymmetric threshold voltages. The n-well, created by redoping the p-substrate, necessarily has a higher dopant concentration than the original substrate (redoped areas always have higher net doping). This means $V_{th}$ for the PMOS is higher than for the NMOS.
- **Fix**: A blanket **threshold-adjust implantation** (no mask needed) across the entire wafer, adding just enough acceptors in a thin surface layer to equalize the threshold voltages. However, this increases dopant atom count in the PMOS channel, reducing hole mobility.

#### Twin-Well Process (Most Widely Used)

- Both n-well and p-well are created as separate wells in the substrate.
- Dopant concentrations for each backgate can be **independently tuned**.
- Requires one extra mask compared to single-well, but provides symmetrical $V_{th}$ values without mobility penalties.

#### Triple-Well Process

- One backgate is formed as a "well within a well" (double redoping).
- Creates p-n junctions between both backgate types and the substrate.
- **Key advantage**: Both backgates can be biased to arbitrary potentials (by reverse-biasing the isolation junctions), providing an additional degree of freedom.
- Particularly useful for **high-voltage applications** (e.g., automotive electronics) and mixed-signal designs requiring substrate noise isolation.

### 2.9.3 FEOL: Creating Devices

The Front-End-Of-Line (FEOL) encompasses all process steps that create the active devices. The example below follows a **twin-well CMOS process** starting on a p$^-$ substrate. Five masks are required, labeled (1) through (5).

> **Note**: p-doped substrates are preferred because the die substrate is then at the lowest potential in the circuit. Defining this as 0 V means all calculations use positive voltages.

#### Mask (1): "Nwell" -- Producing the n- and p-Wells

The process sequence is:

1. Deposit a **nitride layer** on the substrate (with a thin pad oxide for adhesion).
2. Etch the nitride using the Nwell mask, exposing areas for n-wells.
3. **Implant phosphorus** (donors) into the exposed regions to create n-wells. The pad oxide serves as a scattering oxide to prevent channeling.
4. Remove photoresist; **drive-in diffusion** pushes the dopants deeper into the substrate. Simultaneously, a thick **thermal oxide** grows via the LOCOS effect (only outside the nitride-masked areas, creating a bird's beak profile).
5. Remove nitride; the thick oxide now acts as a **self-aligned mask** for the p-well implant -- **boron** (acceptors) is implanted everywhere the oxide is absent. No separate mask is needed.
6. Drive-in diffusion for the p-well, followed by **CMP planarization** to remove all oxide and flatten the surface.

The entire wafer surface is now covered with adjacent n- and p-wells.

#### Mask (2): "STI" -- Shallow Trench Isolation

Active device areas must be laterally isolated from each other. STI (Shallow Trench Isolation) creates very narrow oxide-filled trenches:

1. Pattern photoresist with the STI mask.
2. **Etch trenches** into the silicon (typical depth: a few hundred nm).
3. Fill trenches with **deposited oxide**, providing dielectric isolation between n- and p-wells.
4. **CMP planarization** to remove excess oxide.

Optionally, a **channel-stop doping** can be implanted at the trench bottoms before oxide fill to suppress parasitic channel formation (at the cost of an additional mask).

#### Mask (3): "Poly" -- Producing the Polysilicon Structures

1. Grow a very thin **gate oxide** on the cleaned silicon surface by **dry oxidation**. This step requires extreme care -- the purity and thickness uniformity of the gate oxide are critical to device performance.
2. Deposit **polysilicon** by heteroepitaxy on top of the gate oxide.
3. Pattern and etch the polysilicon using the Poly mask to define the gate electrodes.

#### Masks (4) and (5): "NSD" and "PSD" -- Source/Drain Formation

This is a two-phase process using the same two masks twice:

**Phase 1: Lightly Doped Drain (LDD) Implant**

- **NSD mask** (NMOS Source/Drain): Opens over NMOS gates. A light n-type implant creates the source/drain edges. The **poly gate itself acts as a mask**, so the source/drain edges are **self-aligned** to the gate edges, minimizing parasitic overlap capacitances $C_{GS}$ and $C_{GD}$.
- **PSD mask** (PMOS Source/Drain): Same procedure for PMOS with a light p-type implant.
- The NSD implant also dopes the n-well bulk contacts; the PSD implant also dopes the p-well bulk contacts.

**Spacer Formation (between Phase 1 and Phase 2)**

1. Deposit a thin oxide layer across the entire surface using **CVD** (ensuring uniform coverage on both horizontal and vertical surfaces).
2. **Anisotropic dry etch** removes oxide from horizontal surfaces but leaves oxide on vertical edges of the poly gates, forming **spacers**.

**Phase 2: Heavy Doping**

- Repeat the NSD and PSD masks, but this time with **heavy doping** (n$^+$ and p$^+$).
- The spacers ensure that the regions immediately adjacent to the channel remain lightly doped (from Phase 1), while the outer source/drain regions are heavily doped.
- This creates the **LDD (Lightly Doped Drain) structure**, which is present on both sides of the gate because either side can function as the drain.

**Why LDD matters**: The lightly doped region at the channel edge increases the **breakdown voltage** at the drain-channel junction by reducing the peak electric field, improving device reliability (detailed in Chapter 7, Sect. 7.2.2).

**Why heavy doping matters**: The n$^+$/p$^+$ regions provide:
- Low-resistance contacts to the metal interconnects applied in BEOL.
- Sufficient majority carriers for transistor current flow.

After final diffusion, the surface is prepared for metallization contacts. This concludes the FEOL -- all active devices have been fabricated.

> **Additional capability**: The same FEOL process steps can also create analog components such as resistors, capacitors, and diodes (covered in Chapter 6, Sect. 6.3).

### 2.9.4 BEOL: Connecting Devices

The Back-End-Of-Line (BEOL) creates the metal interconnect stack that wires together the FEOL devices. It uses the **dual-Damascene process** (described in detail in [[2.8 Metallization]]):

- **Contacts and vias** (vertical through-connections) and **interconnects** (horizontal wiring) are produced by repeated application of dual-Damascene.
- **Two masks per metal layer**: one for through-contacts in the oxide layer, one for the interconnect pattern above it.

**Mask count summary**:
- FEOL: 5 masks (Nwell, STI, Poly, NSD, PSD)
- BEOL: 2 masks per metal layer
- Passivation: 1 additional mask

**Passivation and Bond Pads**: The completed chip is capped with a **passivation layer** (commonly silicon nitride, Si$_3$N$_4$) to protect against moisture. Openings are etched at **bond pad** locations using a final mask. Bond wires or solder bumps are attached at these pads depending on the packaging technology.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Section 2.9 synthesizes all the individual fabrication techniques from Chapter 2 (oxidation, doping, lithography, etching, metallization) into a complete, practical CMOS process flow. Understanding this end-to-end sequence -- from well formation through device patterning to metal interconnects -- is essential for layout designers because every design rule and layout constraint in subsequent chapters (Chapter 3 onward) traces directly back to a physical process step described here. The distinction between FEOL (device creation with 5 masks) and BEOL (interconnect creation with 2 masks per metal layer) also establishes the organizational framework used throughout the rest of the book when discussing layout layers, design rules, and parasitic effects.
