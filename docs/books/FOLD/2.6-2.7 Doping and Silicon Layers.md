# 2.6-2.7 Doping and Silicon Layers

> **Chapter context**: Technology Know-How: From Silicon to Devices

## Key Concepts

### 2.6 Doping

Doping is the deliberate introduction of impurity atoms (dopants) into a silicon crystal to change its electrical conductivity. Dopant atoms become electrically effective as donors (n-type) or acceptors (p-type) when they occupy regular lattice sites -- either by filling an empty site or by replacing a silicon atom at its site. There are two primary methods for doping: diffusion and ion implantation.

#### 2.6.2 Diffusion

In diffusion-based doping, the wafer is placed in a diffusion furnace and exposed to a carrier gas containing the dopant. Dopant atoms migrate from regions of high concentration (the gas) to regions of low concentration (the wafer surface) because the dopant concentration in the wafer is much lower than in the gas.

**Masking with oxide**: A structured oxide layer serves as a diffusion mask. Dopants cannot penetrate oxide, which is also resistant to the high temperatures required. Dopants only enter the silicon through the oxide openings.

**Doping profile -- "Diffusion with an inexhaustible source"**: The concentration at the silicon surface C(z=0) is held constant by the carrier gas over time. As time elapses:
- Concentration increases in all areas
- Concentration decreases with growing depth z
- The profile follows the characteristic complementary error function (erfc) shape

**Outdiffusion (lateral spreading)**: Because diffusion is **isotropic** (dopants diffuse in all directions), impurity atoms also move sideways underneath the masking oxide. This produces an **edge shift** -- the lateral expansion of a doped area is always greater than the corresponding oxide opening. This is a critical consideration for layout design.

**Silicon consumption**: Thermal oxidation "consumes" the silicon at the surface. In regions where oxidation follows doping, some of the most heavily doped surface material is lost. This becomes especially problematic when many doping procedures are performed in series with different masks, as silicon is repeatedly consumed by the oxidation needed for each new mask.

#### 2.6.3 Ion Implantation

Ion implantation was developed to overcome the limitations of diffusion-based doping. In this method, ionized dopant particles are accelerated to high speed in an electrical field and directed at the wafer.

**System architecture** (Fig. 2.17):
1. **Ion source** emits particles
2. **Mass separator** uses a magnetic field perpendicular to the ion beam; the Lorentz force bends positively charged ions into circular trajectories whose radius depends on particle mass
3. **Aperture diaphragm** blocks unwanted ion types
4. **Capacitor plates** (electrical field) steer the selected ion beam onto the wafer in a "writing" pattern

**Key characteristics**:
- Ions penetrate the wafer surface like projectiles; penetration depth is precisely controlled by the accelerating voltage
- Performed at **room temperature** -- developed photoresist can serve directly as a mask (**resist mask**), unlike diffusion which requires oxide masks
- Doping accuracy is approximately +/-5%
- Each wafer must be treated individually (unlike diffusion furnaces which process many wafers simultaneously)

**Channeling effect**: The regular lattice arrangement of silicon atoms creates channels. If dopant ions flow exactly between atomic nuclei along these channels, they are only slightly decelerated and penetrate deeper than intended. Two countermeasures:
- **Wafer tilting**: Incline wafers at a suitable angle so ions cannot enter the lattice channels. Drawback: low asymmetric doping concentration under the photoresist edge.
- **Scattering oxide**: A thin oxide layer on the wafer surface deflects the ions, preventing them from arriving in parallel streams.

**Crystal healing (tempering)**: The particle bombardment slightly damages the lattice structure. After removing the photoresist, the crystal must be annealed at **800-1000 C** (~1650 F). This tempering process re-embeds displaced silicon atoms and impurity atoms into the lattice, electrically activating the dopants. A slight diffusion and therefore a very minor edge shift occur during this step.

**Doping profile -- "Diffusion with an exhaustible source"**: If implanted dopants are subsequently driven deeper by diffusion, no new atoms are added (unlike furnace diffusion). The concentration near the surface decreases as atoms diffuse away, while it increases in deeper regions. The profile peak broadens and flattens over time.

**Advantages of ion implantation over diffusion**:
- No oxide steps and no loss of the most heavily doped surface region (because resist masks are used, not structured oxide)
- More accurate doping (~+/-5%)
- Only slight outdiffusion (minimal edge shift)
- Avoids post-diffusion of existing impurities from multiple doping steps, giving more degrees of freedom for configuring doping profiles

Despite higher equipment costs and lower throughput (one wafer at a time), implantation is used almost exclusively in cutting-edge semiconductor processes because the technological benefits enable continued feature size reduction.

#### 2.6.4 Reference to Physical Design

**Edge shifts**: The sideways expansion caused by outdiffusion means doped areas are always larger than the mask opening. The edge shift is typically **70-80% of the diffusion depth**. A characteristic value can be assigned to every diffusion procedure in a process, enabling edge shift compensation through pre-sizing.

**Important convention**: Doping layers in the layout show the **doping mask**, not the resulting doped region. Outdiffusion must be "added mentally" when reading a layout. This is crucial for physical design engineers.

**Spacing rules**: Outdiffusion (not directly visible in the layout) can require spacings defined in design rules that are larger than expected from either the feature size (single layer) or overlay accuracy (two layers). Additionally, in smart power processes with higher electric strength than pure CMOS logic, spacing values between doping areas are often determined by **electrical requirements** (e.g., avoiding short-circuits) rather than fabrication constraints. This is discussed further in Chapter 6 (Sect. 6.2).

**Vertical p-n transitions**: Diffusion and implantation always produce inhomogeneous doping -- dopant concentration decreases significantly with depth (note the logarithmic scales in Figs. 2.16 and 2.18). Key consequences:
- Cross-sectional views show regions where a doping type is **predominant**, not where it is exclusive
- Area boundaries indicate **p-n transitions** where acceptor and donor concentrations are balanced
- Transitions are **seamless, not abrupt**
- Doping accumulates irreversibly; vertical p-n transitions can only be produced by **redoping** (overcompensating the existing concentration with the complementary dopant)
- "Reliable" redoping requires the new dopant concentration to be noticeably greater than the existing one, so multiply-redoped regions are always heavily doped

**Convention**: n-type conductivity is shown in **blue**, p-type in **red**. Superscript "+" indicates high dopant concentration; superscript "-" indicates low concentration (e.g., n+, p-).

### 2.7 Growing and Structuring Silicon Layers

Silicon layers can be grown on the wafer surface by deposition methods collectively called **epitaxy**. The crystal structure of the new layer is determined by the wafer surface composition. Two types exist: **homoepitaxy** (same material as substrate) and **heteroepitaxy** (different material from substrate).

#### 2.7.1 Homoepitaxy

**Layer growth**: When new silicon atoms are deposited on a monocrystalline silicon surface, they adopt the existing atomic structure, propagating the monocrystalline lattice. This is homoepitaxy -- commonly just called "epitaxy" or "epi" in semiconductor jargon.

Epitaxially grown layers can be doped differently from the base material by implantation, creating clearly defined **abrupt vertical p-n transitions** that extend across the entire wafer. **Buried layers** can be created by selectively doping the base material surface before the epitaxial step.

**Process example** (Fig. 2.20): On a p-doped wafer, a lightly n-doped epitaxial layer is grown:
1. Selective doping of the substrate surface (by implantation or diffusion)
2. Epitaxial silicon growth at very high temperatures
3. The buried dopant diffuses slightly during growth, spreading into the epi layer

To minimize unwanted upward outdiffusion of buried layers, **arsenic** or **antimony** are used as donors because their larger atomic size makes them diffuse less than phosphorus.

**Structuring -- Trench isolation**: IC devices sit in a thin layer only a few micrometers thick on the monocrystalline wafer (~1-2% of wafer thickness). This layer may consist of one or many epitaxial layers, or no epitaxy at all in simpler processes.

In early IC technology, devices were electrically isolated on the surface by field oxide and underneath by **reverse-biased p-n junctions**. As miniaturization progressed, the relative space requirements of these passive field areas became a barrier to integration density. **Trench isolation** methods were developed to address this.

**Shallow Trench Isolation (STI)**:
- Trenches etched by reactive ion etching (RIE), masked by lithographically structured photoresist
- Trenches **completely filled with oxide** (thermal oxidation, or brief thermal oxidation + oxide deposition to conserve silicon)
- Surface planarized by chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP)
- Typical depth: a few hundred nm (e.g., CMOS logic chips)

**Deep Trench Isolation (DTI)**:
- Deeper trenches, also etched by RIE
- Only the **walls** are coated with oxide for electrical insulation
- Remainder filled with **polysilicon** (similar thermal expansion coefficient to monocrystalline silicon, minimizing mechanical strain from temperature changes)
- Surface planarized by CMP
- Typical depth: up to several micrometers (e.g., BCD chips for automobile electronics)

The etching depth is controlled by the etching period, since silicon cannot act as an etch stop when the substrate is also silicon.

Trench isolation only insulates **laterally**. For complete dielectric isolation on all sides, a buried oxide layer is needed -- these are **Silicon on Insulator (SOI)** techniques.

#### 2.7.2 Heteroepitaxy and Polysilicon

**Layer growth**: In heteroepitaxy, the substrate surface material differs from the deposited material. In nearly all practical cases, silicon is deposited onto **oxide** (SiO2), which is amorphous (non-monocrystalline). With no preferred lattice direction, many small crystallization cores form independently in different lattice alignments. These grow into **polycrystalline silicon (polysilicon, or "poly")**.

**Grain structure**: Polysilicon is composed of minute crystallites ("grains") whose size depends on process parameters and can be as small as a few hundred nm. Different lattice alignments meet at grain boundaries, causing current leakage. Consequently, polysilicon is **not suitable for p-n junctions** but works for basic current conduction.

**Applications of polysilicon**:
- **Gate electrodes** for field-effect transistors
- **Ohmic resistors**
- **Electrodes for capacitors**

**Structuring**: Polysilicon is patterned by photolithography followed by etching (RIE in state-of-the-art processes). Poly structures are fabricated **before metallization layers** and sit insulated by oxide between the monocrystalline silicon and the metal layers.

The poly layer is deposited on both the thick **field oxide** and the very thin **gate oxide (GOX)** -- the latter serves as a dielectric for capacitors and FETs. Structuring is done by photolithographically masked anisotropic RIE (see Fig. 2.23 steps a-f).

#### 2.7.3 Reference to Physical Design

Polysilicon above the field oxide can be used as an **interconnect**, but with great caution:
- Polysilicon's ohmic resistance is approximately **three orders of magnitude higher** (~1000x) than metal interconnect of the same dimensions
- Poly interconnects should only be used in **exceptional cases** (e.g., connecting a device to a nearby contact)
- Only recommended for **very small currents and short distances**
- The **IR drop** (voltage drop) caused by routing with polysilicon must be watched very carefully

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Sections 2.6-2.7 cover two foundational front-end-of-line (FEOL) process steps -- doping and silicon layer growth/structuring -- that directly determine the electrical properties and physical layout constraints of IC devices. Understanding doping mechanisms (diffusion vs. implantation) is essential because the resulting edge shifts, outdiffusion effects, and doping profiles dictate the **spacing rules** and **sizing conventions** used in physical design. Similarly, epitaxy and trench isolation define how devices are physically separated on the die, directly affecting achievable integration density. These FEOL steps create the device layer that the subsequent metallization (BEOL, covered in [[2.8 Metallization]]) must connect, making them the bridge between raw silicon processing ([[2.4-2.5 Imaging Errors and Oxide Layers]]) and the interconnect routing that ultimately implements the circuit.
