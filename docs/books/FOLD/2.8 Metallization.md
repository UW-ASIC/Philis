# 2.8 Metallization

> **Chapter context**: Technology Know-How: From Silicon to Devices

## Key Concepts

### 2.8.1 Fundamentals

Metallization is the BEOL (back-end-of-line) process that creates the metal interconnect layers and through-contacts that wire together the devices fabricated during FEOL. It is the physical foundation of what layout designers call **routing**.

#### Routing Layer Structure

Each metallization layer in the layout requires **two designed layers**:

1. A layer defining the **through-contacts** (holes etched in an insulating oxide), and
2. A layer defining the **interconnect patterns** deposited above that oxide.

These come in pairs:

| Layer Pair | Through-Contact Layer | Metal Layer |
|---|---|---|
| 1st routing layer pair | Contact | Metal-1 |
| 2nd routing layer pair | Via-1 | Metal-2 |
| 3rd routing layer pair | Via-2 | Metal-3 |
| nth routing layer pair | Via-(n-1) | Metal-n |

- **Contacts** are the bottom-most through-connections linking Metal-1 down to the silicon surface (and thus to the transistor terminals).
- **Vias** are the through-connections between neighboring metal layers (Via-1 connects Metal-1 to Metal-2, etc.).
- Metal layers are numbered bottom-to-top in fabrication order.
- BEOL layer naming is largely standardized across manufacturers, unlike FEOL layers which vary significantly.

#### Materials: Aluminum vs. Copper

The interconnect material must satisfy several competing requirements: high electrical conductivity, high current-carrying capacity, good contact with silicon, good contact with external packaging, corrosion resistance, mechanical stability, and suitability for multi-layer stacking.

**Aluminum (Al)** was the historical material of choice. It can be enriched with small percentages of silicon and copper (AlSiCu) to improve specific properties. Aluminum is easy to deposit (vapor deposition), adheres well to oxide, and can be patterned by dry etching to produce well-defined edges.

**Copper (Cu)** has increasingly replaced aluminum in advanced process nodes for several critical reasons:

- **Lower resistivity**: Copper's specific resistance is roughly **2x lower** than pure aluminum, which directly reduces parasitic IR drops in shrinking interconnect cross-sections.
- **Better electromigration resistance**: Copper tolerates comparatively higher current densities, enabling much smaller (narrower) interconnects.
- **Lower parasitic capacitance**: Because copper interconnects can be made smaller, the sidewall area and thus capacitance per unit length decreases, reducing crosstalk between neighboring nets.
- **Faster signal propagation**: Signal delay is proportional to the RC product of the conductor. Both R and C are reduced with copper, yielding significantly faster response times.

However, copper introduces serious fabrication challenges:

- **Diffusion contamination**: Copper diffuses rapidly into surrounding oxide, contaminating it. It must be enclosed in diffusion barrier layers (Ta, TaN, TiN).
- **Corrosion**: Unlike aluminum (which forms a self-passivating surface oxide), copper corrodes throughout its bulk. Protective coatings are mandatory.
- **Cannot be dry-etched easily**: New patterning techniques (the Damascene process) had to be developed because copper resists conventional dry etching.

The transition from aluminum to copper occurred across the **350 nm to 90 nm** technology nodes. Both materials coexist in this range. In smart power chips, the top metallization layer is often a thick copper layer for high-current interconnects (on the order of amperes), while lower layers may still use aluminum.

### 2.8.2 Metallization Structures Without Planarization

This older approach (now largely outmoded for submicron processes) builds metallization layers without leveling the wafer surface between layers. It has only been used with **aluminum**.

#### Process Steps (repeated for each metal layer)

1. **Deposit insulating oxide layer** over the entire wafer surface.
2. **Etch contact/via holes** selectively through the oxide using photolithographic masking.
3. **Deposit metal (aluminum)** across the entire surface, including into the contact holes. Deposition is primarily by vapor deposition (material arrives normal to the surface).
4. **Etch the interconnect pattern** selectively from the metal layer to define the wiring.

This is an alternating deposit-and-etch process. The through-contacts are metalized simultaneously with the interconnect layer during the metal deposition step.

#### The Step-Formation Problem

As layers accumulate, the wafer surface becomes increasingly **uneven**. Each etch creates a step in the topography. This causes several problems:

- **Poor metal coverage at oxide edges**: Vapor-deposited metal has difficulty conforming to steep step edges, creating weak points in the electrical path -- particularly at via sidewalls.
- **Stacked vias are forbidden**: If vias are placed directly above one another, step heights compound, making electrical contact unreliable or broken entirely. Design rules typically prohibit "via stacks" in non-planarized processes.
- **Degraded photolithography**: Increasing surface unevenness worsens image sharpness during lithographic exposure of upper layers. Consequently, design rules prescribe **larger minimum widths and spacings** for the top metallization layers.

#### Mitigation: Beveled Edges and Tungsten Plugs

- **Beveled via edges** improve metal coverage but consume area, hindering miniaturization.
- **Sputtering** (instead of vapor deposition) can deposit metal on vertical edges, but still struggles with very small via diameters.
- **Tungsten plugs**: Contact holes are first filled with tungsten using CVD (chemical vapor deposition), then aluminum is deposited for the interconnects. The tungsten can be deposited across the entire surface or selectively grown on the silicon surface within the contact hole, with excess material etched back. This process remains in use today for contacting aluminum interconnects, as the aluminum can still be dry-etched to produce fine structures with clean edges.

### 2.8.3 Metallization Structures with Planarization

Modern submicron processes require the wafer surface to be flattened (planarized) between metallization layers to enable reliable multi-layer stacking.

#### Additive Approaches (Limited)

Before CMP, two additive methods were used to partially smooth the surface:

- **Reflow technology**: Doped glass material is deposited and then liquefied in a high-temperature step; it flows into recesses. Limited to smoothing poly structures only, because aluminum would melt at the required temperatures.
- **Spin-on glass (SOG)**: Dissolved silicon-oxide compounds applied at room temperature and hardened below aluminum's melting point. Can smooth aluminum features.

Both methods only address **local** roughness due to the high viscosity of the glass materials. They relieve step-formation issues but cannot fully eliminate them.

#### Chemical-Mechanical Polishing (CMP)

CMP was the key breakthrough -- a **subtractive** planarization process that levels the **entire wafer surface**. The process combines chemical etching with mechanical material removal:

- Wafers are pressed face-down against a **rotating polishing table**.
- **Etching agent and abrasive polish** (slurry) are continuously supplied.
- Surface elevations are removed until a continuous flat surface is achieved across the entire wafer.
- Specific material layers can be partially or entirely abraded as needed.

#### The Damascene Process

Because CMP removes any material protruding above the surface, conventional deposit-then-etch metallization (where metal sits on top) would be destroyed. The **Damascene process** inverts the approach: instead of depositing metal and etching away unwanted portions, trenches are first etched into an insulating layer, then filled with metal, and the excess is removed by CMP. The interconnect is left **embedded** in the oxide trench.

The name "Damascene" comes from a historical metalworking technique where gold or silver is inlaid into darkly oxidized steel to create intricate patterns.

**Damascene process for copper interconnects (single layer):**

1. Etch trenches in the oxide layer (patterned by photoresist).
2. Deposit a **diffusion barrier / liner** (Ta, TaN, or TiN) to prevent copper from contaminating the oxide.
3. Deposit a thin **copper seed layer** on the barrier (required for electrochemical copper deposition).
4. **Electrochemically deposit copper** to fill the trenches.
5. **CMP** to remove excess copper and planarize.
6. Deposit a **protective dielectric barrier** (Si3N4 or amorphous SiNx) to cap the copper against diffusion and oxidation.

**Full Damascene process flow for one metallization layer pair** (contacts/vias + interconnects) requires this sequence twice:

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Deposit first insulating layer (for contacts/vias) |
| 2 | Etch holes for contacts/vias |
| 3 | Deposit diffusion barrier + copper seed layer |
| 4 | Deposit copper to fill contact/via holes |
| 5 | CMP to remove excess copper and planarize |
| 6 | Deposit protective barrier layer |
| 7 | Deposit second insulating oxide layer (for interconnects) |
| 8 | Etch trenches for interconnects |
| 9 | Deposit diffusion barrier + copper seed layer |
| 10 | Deposit copper to fill trenches |
| 11 | CMP to remove excess copper and planarize |
| 12 | Deposit protective barrier layer |

All structuring steps etch **insulating** layers (steps 2 and 8), into which metal is then embedded (steps 4 and 10). This is why the process is especially suitable for copper, which cannot be dry-etched effectively.

Note: "insulating layer" is used rather than "oxide layer" because (1) advanced processes may use **low-k dielectrics** (materials with dielectric constants lower than SiO2) to reduce parasitic coupling capacitance, and (2) the additional protective barriers must also be etched when using copper.

The Damascene process works for both aluminum and copper. With aluminum, vias are made of tungsten; with copper, vias can also be copper. Only at the first layer (contact to silicon) must copper and silicon be separated by tungsten.

#### The Dual-Damascene Process

The dual-Damascene process is an optimization that fills **both** contact/via holes and interconnect trenches in a **single** copper deposition step, requiring only **one** CMP step per layer pair (instead of two). One possible sequence:

1. Deposit first insulating oxide layer.
2. Deposit a nitride layer.
3. Pattern the nitride layer into a mask for via etching.
4. Deposit second insulating oxide layer.
5. Etch interconnect trenches in the top oxide **and** (using the nitride mask) etch via holes in the bottom oxide -- both in a single etch sequence.
6. Etch through remaining nitride.
7. Deposit diffusion barrier + copper seed layer.
8. Deposit copper to fill both vias and trenches simultaneously.
9. CMP to remove excess copper and planarize.
10. Deposit nitride barrier against copper diffusion and oxidation.

The key advantage is process simplification: fewer deposition and CMP steps per layer pair, reducing fabrication cost and time.

### 2.8.4 Reference to Physical Design

The metallization process has several direct impacts on layout design rules and routing decisions.

#### Spacing Rules (Non-Planarized Processes)

- **Stacked vias are forbidden**: Vias placed directly above one another produce step heights too steep for metal coverage. Via stack features in layout tools must not be used.
- **Inter-layer via spacing rules**: Beyond the normal within-layer spacing rules for contacts and vias, additional design rules prescribe minimum spacings between vias in **different** layers, adding complexity to the routing task.
- **Increasing minimum dimensions for upper layers**: As surface unevenness grows with each added layer, photolithographic resolution degrades. Design rules therefore prescribe **greater minimum widths and spacings** for the top metallization layers.

#### Density Rules (CMP / Damascene Processes)

CMP ablation depth depends on the **local material composition** of the layer being polished. Since the layer is typically a mixture (e.g., metal and oxide), inhomogeneous distribution of materials causes:

- **Dishing** (indentations in large metal areas), and
- **Hillocks** (elevated regions where too little material was removed).

To prevent this, **density rules** prescribe a mean material density as a function of surface area. Compliance often requires significant additional layout work:

- **Too little metal**: Electrically non-functional **filler structures** (dummy metal fill) must be inserted to increase density. Semi-automated algorithms can sometimes handle this, but not always.
- **Too much metal**: **Slots** (gaps) must be cut in very wide interconnects. Spacings between structures may need to be increased, consuming additional chip area.
- Density rules should be considered in **early layout phases** to avoid costly late-stage rework, even though full verification can only occur at the end of physical design.

#### Current-Carrying Capacity

Interconnects must be sized to carry their required current reliably over the chip's entire lifetime. Key considerations:

- Contacts and vias are especially critical -- they are typically the smallest structures and (in non-planarized processes) suffer from poor edge coverage.
- Simply ensuring adequate track width is not sufficient; when current transitions between metal layers, **enough vias must be provided** to ensure reliability.

#### Via Doubling (Redundant Vias)

With shrinking feature sizes, the sheer number of vias per chip has increased dramatically, raising the probability of a single defective via causing chip failure and reducing yield. To mitigate this:

- **At least two vias** are recommended for every inter-layer connection, even if one via meets electrical requirements.
- Redundant vias ensure that a single via failure does not affect yield.

#### Metal-Semiconductor Contact (Schottky vs. Ohmic)

At the semiconductor-metal interface, a depletion zone forms due to differing band structures, creating an energy barrier (a **Schottky diode**). This produces unwanted diode-like (non-linear) I-V behavior. The solution:

- **Heavily dope** the semiconductor at the contact interface. This makes the depletion zone so thin that charge carriers can **quantum-mechanically tunnel** through the barrier.
- The result is a linear (ohmic) I-V response. Typical single-contact resistance values are in the **single to double-digit ohm** range.

#### Number of Metal Layers as an Optimization Goal

In modern processes, the number of metallization layers is a design choice, often finalized during physical design. The guiding principle: **"as many as needed, but as few as possible."**

- **Engineering requirement**: Enough layers must be available to complete all routing (all nets connected in accordance with design rules).
- **Economic constraint**: Each additional metal layer increases manufacturing cost.
- Finding the optimum is non-trivial -- eliminating one routing layer might be technically possible but could require additional layout effort and chip area.

Typical layer counts:

| Application | Metal Layers |
|---|---|
| Mixed-signal | 3-5 |
| Smart power | 3-5 (top layer often extra-thick copper for high currents) |
| Digital / microprocessors | Many more (driven by circuit complexity) |

The top metallization layer in smart power processes often has non-standard design rules with larger minimum widths and spacings to support high-current interconnects.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Metallization is the culmination of the fabrication process described throughout Chapter 2 -- it takes the individual devices created during FEOL (through the doping, oxidation, and patterning steps of [[2.6-2.7 Doping and Silicon Layers]]) and wires them into functional circuits. The design rules arising from metallization processes -- spacing rules, density rules, via doubling, current-carrying capacity constraints -- directly govern the **routing** phase of physical design, which is one of the most computationally intensive steps in the layout flow. Understanding the distinction between planarized (Damascene/CMP) and non-planarized metallization is essential because it determines which design rules apply (e.g., whether stacked vias are permitted, whether density fill is required). This section sets the stage for [[2.9 CMOS Standard Process]], which integrates all the FEOL and BEOL steps into a complete fabrication flow.
