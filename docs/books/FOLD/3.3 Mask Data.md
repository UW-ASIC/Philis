# 3.3 Mask Data and Layout Post Processing

> **Chapter context**: Bridges to Technology: Interfaces, Design Rules, and Libraries

## Key Concepts

Layout post processing is the multi-stage pipeline that transforms a completed chip layout into the mask data consumed by fabrication equipment. It sits between the design team's final layout and the foundry's mask production, and is subdivided into three major phases: **chip finishing**, **reticle layout**, and **layout-to-mask preparation**. Each phase adds non-electrical or fabrication-specific information that the layout designer does not (and should not) manage during physical design.

### 3.3.1 Overview of the Post-Processing Pipeline

The full flow (Fig. 3.14) proceeds through eight numbered steps grouped into three phases:

| Phase | Steps | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Chip finishing** (Sect. 3.3.2) | 1-3 | Add product metadata and DfM structures |
| **Reticle layout** (Sect. 3.3.3) | 4-5 | Tile dies into a matrix, add test patterns and alignment marks |
| **Layout-to-mask preparation** (Sect. 3.3.4) | 6-8 | Graphics operations, RET, fracturing |

The input is the chip layout data (structures with electrical functions). The output is mask data ready for mask production. Along the way, two categories of non-electrical structures are injected: **product data** (company logo, chip ID, layer revisions) and **fabrication data** (seal ring, filler structures, test patterns, scribe line monitors, alignment marks).

### 3.3.2 Chip Finishing

Chip finishing augments the electrically-functional layout with structures that serve identification, protection, and manufacturability purposes.

#### Product Identification Structures (Step 1)

These are placed at the edge of the active area and include:

- **Company logo** -- branding / IP identification
- **Chip ID and version** -- unique design identifier
- **Layer revision markers** -- indicate which photomask layers have been updated in a given revision (important because a new design revision does not necessarily change every layer; sometimes only a subset of routing-layer pairs is modified)

Revision structures can be human-readable (numbers) or encoded in a company-specific scheme.

#### Seal Ring (Step 2)

The **seal ring** (also called a **scribe seal**) is a protective barrier that encloses the entire active design region. Its purposes are:

- **Moisture barrier** -- the chip boundary after sawing/breaking is extremely uneven and susceptible to moisture ingress from the sides; the seal ring blocks this path
- **Mechanical protection** -- shields active structures from damage during die singulation (sawing)

Internally, the seal ring is complex -- it typically contains a **stack of all processed metal layers**. Its exact layout is specified by the fab and is confidential. The seal ring sits between the design region and the scribe edge, with corner regions at each die corner (Fig. 3.15).

#### Filler Structures / Dummy Fill (Step 3)

Filler structures (dummy fill) are inserted across the entire chip area in a fully automated process to improve **CMP (chemical mechanical polishing) planarity**. The underlying problem is that CMP requires a reasonably homogeneous material density across each planarized layer. Without dummy fill, three failure modes arise:

- **(a) Dishing** -- in regions with very wide metal (e.g., wide copper interconnects), the CMP removes too much metal from the center of the feature. The remedy is **slotting**: inserting narrow oxide ribs into wide metal lines to break up the copper area.
- **(b) Excessive erosion** -- where copper density is too high, too much pressure is applied to the thin oxide ribs between metal lines, stripping them below the target level.
- **(c) Desired erosion** -- the target: uniform material removal across the surface.
- **(d) Insufficient erosion** -- in regions with no copper, the erosion is too weak. The remedy is inserting **dummy fill copper structures** to bring the local metal density up.

Critical constraints on dummy fill:

- Filler structures must have **no electrical function** -- they must not create short circuits or parasitic coupling that degrades active structures
- After chip finishing, a full **DRC and LVS check** must be re-run to verify nothing was broken
- Filler data should be stored in **separate cells** from the electrical layout data, following naming guidelines. This enables automated removal during redesigns and clean data management

### 3.3.3 Reticle Layout

The reticle layout is the arrangement of multiple identical (or sometimes different) dies into a matrix for exposure onto the wafer. The matrix size is determined by the **maximum exposable surface area** dictated by the reticle size.

#### Die Tiling and Scribe Lines

Dies are arranged in rows and columns, separated by **scribe lines** (also called sawing trenches or saw streets). The spacing between neighboring dies is **50 to 100 um**, depending on the singulation method (diamond saw or laser beam).

For **prototype runs** with low volume, different chip designs can share a single reticle to split mask costs.

#### Test Patterns (Step 4)

Test patterns are placed within the scribe lines and come in two types:

1. **Process monitoring patterns** (blue hatched in Fig. 3.17) -- features for checking individual process steps against manufacturing tolerances during fabrication. Checks include optical measurement of feature sizes and electrical resistance measurement with needle probes. Faults found here may sometimes be rectifiable.

2. **Electrical device/cell test patterns** (brown hatched in Fig. 3.17) -- complete basic devices or cells that can only be analyzed after FEOL and the first metal layer are completed. Tests are performed on the entire wafer before singulation.

The two types are typically assigned to different orientations: one to vertical scribe lines, the other to horizontal scribe lines.

For very large dies where scribe line space is insufficient, two options exist:
- **(i)** Widen the sawing trench to create more space (at the cost of wafer area)
- **(ii)** Omit some test patterns to maximize chip yield per wafer

#### Alignment Marks (Step 5)

Alignment marks are placed in the scribe lines on **opposite sides of the reticle**. They enable precise alignment of mask structures to wafer structures during each exposure step (see Chap. 2, Sect. 2.3.4).

Additionally, markers for the diamond saw or laser beam are introduced to ensure the cutting tool always tracks exactly within the sawing trench.

### 3.3.4 Layout-to-Mask Preparation

This final phase converts the reticle layout into mask data through three automated stages.

#### Graphics Operations (Step 6)

Graphics operations transform the data in four key ways:

1. **Flattening** -- the hierarchical data structure (blocks, cells) is removed. All shapes are sorted by layer only, producing "flat" data. Hierarchy is irrelevant for mask fabrication; only layer association matters.

2. **Derived layers** -- some mask layers are automatically computed from other layout layers using Boolean and sizing operations, reducing the designer's workload. For example (Fig. 3.18, Process B), three mask layers can be derived from just two layout layers:
   - `Active = n-Active OR p-Active` (Boolean OR / union)
   - `NSD = SIZE(n-Active, k)` (oversize by value k)
   - `PSD = SIZE(p-Active, k)` (oversize by value k)
   
   The value **k** is based on the **maximum overlay error** for the process (photomask alignment tolerance). This achieves the same result as manually drawing all three layers (Process A) but with one fewer layer and fewer shapes.

3. **Logical layers** -- layers present in the layout that are **not** used for mask generation but are needed for DRC. They encode the **electrical function** of structures. A "Poly" shape, for instance, could be a gate, resistor, capacitor electrode, or interconnect. Three methods to designate function (Fig. 3.19):
   - **(a)** A logical layer "Res" with a shape enclosing the Poly structure
   - **(b)** A "Text" layer with an explanatory label (texts are not shapes and take no space on masks)
   - **(c)** Separate physical layers per function ("Polyres", "Polygate", "Polycap", "Polyline"), unified later: `Poly = Polyres OR Polygate OR Polycap OR Polyline`
   
   Logical layers also encode **voltage class** information for potential-dependent design rules (discussed further in Chap. 6, Sect. 6.2).

4. **Altered layers (pre-sizing)** -- during fabrication, shapes on a mask are enlarged or reduced when transferred to the wafer. This systematic edge shift by a value **k** is compensated in layout-to-mask preparation by shifting shape edges by **-k** (the inverse). This "pre-sizing" ensures that structures in the layout appear as they will on the processed wafer.

#### Resolution Enhancement Techniques (Step 7)

In advanced nodes, the **exposure wavelength exceeds the minimum feature size** -- a fundamental physical contradiction. RET overcome this through several techniques:

- **Optical Proximity Correction (OPC)** -- layout structures are deliberately distorted so that the light intensity distribution in the photoresist produces features matching the intended layout shapes as closely as possible (detailed in Chap. 2, Sect. 2.4.3)
- **Phase-shift masks** -- manipulate the phase of transmitted light to improve edge contrast
- **Multiple exposure techniques** -- expose different parts of the pattern in separate passes (Chap. 5, Sect. 5.5)
- **EUV lithography** -- uses extreme short-wave UV radiation with reflecting masks and mirrors instead of lenses

All necessary graphics data manipulations for these techniques are performed during the layout-to-mask preparation step.

#### Fracturing (Step 8)

Fracturing converts the polygonal shapes used in layout design into formats required by mask-writing hardware. Two primary mask-writing techniques exist:

1. **Adjustable aperture systems** -- two orthogonal apertures form rectangles. Polygons must be decomposed into simple rectangles, and the apertures are adjusted to match rectangle dimensions.

2. **Direct electron-beam writing** -- the e-beam sweeps the mask line by line, switching on and off. Polygons must be converted to **scan lines** to guide the beam.

In both cases, the arbitrary polygons of the layout are "fractured" into primitive shapes that the hardware can reproduce.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Mask data preparation is the final bridge between the design world and the fabrication world -- it is where the abstract, hierarchical, electrically-meaningful layout is transformed into flat, layer-sorted, optically-corrected data that mask-writing equipment can consume. This section completes the "Bridges to Technology" chapter by showing how layout data from [[3.1-3.2 Circuit and Layout Data]] is ultimately handed off to fabrication. The concepts of derived layers, logical layers, and pre-sizing directly set the stage for understanding [[3.4-3.5 Design Rules and Libraries]], since design rules must account for these transformations (e.g., logical layers encode electrical function for DRC, and pre-sizing compensates for fabrication-induced dimensional changes). Without this post-processing pipeline, even a perfectly verified layout could not be manufactured.
