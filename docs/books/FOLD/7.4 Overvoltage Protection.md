# 7.4 Overvoltage Protection

> **Chapter context**: Addressing Reliability in Physical Design

This section covers two distinct overvoltage phenomena that threaten IC reliability: **electrostatic discharge (ESD)** during chip handling and the **antenna effect** during fabrication. Both can damage thin gate oxides and destroy circuits, and both require specific physical design countermeasures.

---

## 7.4.1 ESD Protection

### The ESD Threat

Electrostatic discharge occurs when a statically charged object (a human body, a machine, or the chip package itself) discharges through the chip's pins. The voltage impulse can reach thousands of volts and must be safely shunted away from vulnerable circuitry -- especially the thin gate oxides of MOS transistors.

### The ESD Design Window

Every ESD protection device must operate within a well-defined **ESD design window** in the current-voltage (I-V) plane. The key parameters are:

- **Operating range**: The protection device must remain inactive (high impedance) during normal operation, i.e., for voltages up to VDD.
- **Safety margin**: The trigger point should be 10-20% above VDD.
- **Breakdown voltage V_BD**: When an ESD event drives the voltage above the safety margin, the ESD device triggers at V_BD, creating a low-impedance shunt path (with small on-resistance R_on) that "clamps" the voltage.
- **Destruction voltage V_des**: The entire I-V curve of the ESD device must remain below V_des, the voltage at which the protected circuitry would be damaged.
- **Maximum current I_max**: The ESD device must survive the peak current of the ESD event without failing.

Some ESD devices exhibit a **snap-back characteristic**: after triggering at V_t1 (trigger voltage), the voltage drops to a lower **holding voltage V_h < V_BD** as current increases. This reduces thermal power dissipation and improves clamping.

### Standardized ESD Models

Three standard models represent typical ESD events:

| Model | Abbreviation | Scenario |
|---|---|---|
| Human-Body Model | HBM | Discharge from a statically charged person through a chip pin |
| Machine Model | MM | Discharge from a charged machine element through a chip pin |
| Charged-Device Model | CDM | Discharge of a statically charged packaged chip through a pin |

The CDM is particularly challenging because the entry point of charge into the circuit is unknown. This often requires placing ESD protection devices in close proximity to the protected circuitry.

### Basic ESD Protection Circuit Topology

The standard ESD protection circuit uses multiple diodes arranged around each I/O pin:

- **Supply clamp D_DS**: A diode (designed per the ESD design window) placed between VDD and VSS. It clamps the supply voltage difference during an ESD event.
- **Signal-pin diodes D_D and D_S**: Each I/O pin P_i is connected to VDD by a diode D_Di and to VSS by a diode D_Si. In normal operation these are reverse-biased. If V_i rises above VDD or drops below VSS, one diode becomes forward-biased and clamps the pin voltage.

**Six zapping modes** cover all possible ESD scenarios between any two pins. The naming convention uses the first letter for polarity (P = positive, N = negative) and the second letter for the grounded pin (D = VDD, S = VSS, P = signal pin):

| Mode | Polarity | Grounded Pin |
|---|---|---|
| PS | Positive | VSS |
| PD | Positive | VDD |
| PP | Positive | Signal pin |
| NS | Negative | VSS |
| ND | Negative | VDD |
| NP | Negative | Signal pin |

In all these modes, the signal-pin diodes (D_D, D_S) are forward-biased when current flows through them, while the supply clamp D_DS is reverse-biased. The maximum pin-to-pin voltage is:

$$V_{pp} \leq \begin{cases} V_{BD} + 2V_{on} & \text{without snap-back} \\ V_h + 2V_{on} & \text{with snap-back} \end{cases}$$

where V_on is the diode forward voltage (~0.7 V).

### Pad Cell Layout

ESD protection devices are typically provided as library elements in PDKs, often integrated into **pad cells** that combine the bond pad and ESD protection into a single layout element. In a typical pad cell:

- Diodes D_D and D_S are placed **under the power rails** to save space
- These diodes are small because they are forward-biased during ESD events (only ~0.7 V drop, low power dissipation)
- The supply clamp D_DS needs a **large surface area** because most of the power dissipation occurs there

### Two-Stage ESD Protection for Sensitive Inputs

Gate oxides are the most vulnerable features on a chip. They can withstand approximately **1 V/nm** of field strength, but in advanced processes (<20 nm), gate oxides are only a few nanometers thick, making them vulnerable at just a few volts.

Fortunately, the gate oxide breakdown voltage V_or ("oxide rupture") depends on the duration of the applied field. For a typical ESD event duration of ~100 ns, V_or is approximately **3x the supply voltage VDD**. For example, in a 100 nm process at VDD = 1.1 V, V_or for ESD events is approximately 4.5 V, leaving a useful ESD design window of about 3 V.

A **two-stage ESD protection circuit** consists of:

1. **Primary ESD device D1**: Clamps the input to an intermediate voltage V_1
2. **Series resistor R**: Limits the current I_2 to the secondary device (approximately two orders of magnitude less than I_1). R must be at least one order of magnitude greater than the D2 track resistance.
3. **Secondary ESD device D2**: Clamps the voltage to a safe value V_2 within the gate oxide ESD window. Because I_2 is much smaller, D2 can be designed much smaller than D1.

#### The ggNMOS (Grounded Gate NMOS-FET)

A widely used ESD protection device with snap-back characteristic is the **grounded gate NMOS-FET (ggNMOS)**. Its source (S), backgate (B), and gate (G) are tied to ground, and the drain (D) connects to the input pad.

**How it works:**
1. The drain-backgate p-n junction ("DB diode") is reverse-biased until avalanche breakdown
2. The breakdown current (holes) flows through the backgate, past the source, creating an IR drop in the parasitic lateral backgate resistor R_sub
3. This IR drop forward-biases the source-backgate diode ("SB diode")
4. Since the SB diode is also the base-emitter diode of the parasitic NPN transistor Q, the transistor switches on, triggering snap-back

**Ballasting resistors R_bal** are critical: they are created by placing drain contacts at a distance from the gate, forcing current to travel through the drain. This limits the voltage at the DB diode of each finger, ensuring current spreads across the entire device area before local hotspots cause thermal instability (second breakdown).

#### Layout Considerations for Two-Stage ESD

- The series resistor R is typically made of polysilicon
- It must be wide enough and have sufficient vias for ESD robustness (must carry many tens of milliamps)
- The resistor should **not be bent** to avoid high field strengths at corners
- Outer edges of Metal1 interconnects should be **chamfered** for the same reason
- If the protected device is far from bond pads, D2 should be placed near the device (with conductors sized for I_2) to mitigate substrate debiasing

### Placing and Routing ESD Protection Circuits

When PDK constraints are not available, follow these guidelines:

- **Total resistance** between any two chip points should not exceed approximately **2 ohms**
- **Interconnect width**: Design for at least **100 mA** permanent current (ESD current is high but very brief)
- **Chamfer outer corners** to minimize local field peaks
- **Lateral spacing** to adjacent metals: at least equal to the oxide layer thickness between metal layers; recommended minimum **2 um**
- Run supply lines at the **chip periphery** for direct access from all bond pads
- Consider distributing multiple **supply clamps** throughout the chip

### Power Domain Considerations

A popular methodology places the **ground line outside** the bond pads and the **supply line inside**, both as closed rings. The seal ring can serve as a ground line if the manufacturer permits, providing a very low-resistance contact (uses all metal layers).

For **multiple power domains** (common in mixed-signal and smart power ICs):
- Separate ground lines can be coupled by **two antiparallel diodes** (or stacked diodes) to allow ESD current to use all ground lines
- Pin assignment must account for power domains early in the design -- grouping pins by domain enables locally separated supply lines and avoids routing supply lines multiple times around the chip
- The power supply concept and ESD concept must be defined **at the beginning of the layout phase**, as they impact the entire floorplan

## 7.4.2 Antenna Effect

### What is the Antenna Effect?

The antenna effect (also called **plasma-induced damage, PID**) occurs during fabrication, not during chip operation. A conductor (polysilicon or metal line) that belongs to a net which is only **partially complete** during manufacturing can act as a charge-collecting "antenna." Since the upper layers have not yet been processed, the conductor is neither electrically connected to silicon nor grounded. Charge accumulates on these temporary dead-end connections and can generate leakage currents or permanently damage thin gate oxide.

### Physical Mechanism

Gate dielectrics can be damaged by impulse voltages during process steps involving charged particles. **Reactive ion etching (RIE)** is the primary culprit: the etching plasma contains different charges that are partially absorbed by the wafer surface and flow through conductive layers, creating potential differences that can destroy gate oxide.

**Poly layers** suffer the strongest antenna effect because they are directly structured by RIE. **Metal layers** (in the modern Damascene process) have a reduced antenna effect because RIE structuring occurs in the isolating oxide layer -- charge is only absorbed through via surfaces to the next higher metal.

### RIE Charging Phases

The structuring of polysilicon with RIE proceeds through four critical phases:

1. **Phase 1 (Poly etch begins)**: Poly is continuous; charges flow freely across the entire wafer. No critical voltages occur.
2. **Phase 2 (Poly etched through)**: Poly is separated into distinct structures. Each structure absorbs charges at its **lateral edges**. This phase continues until etching depth is reached everywhere on the wafer.
3. **Phase 3 (Resist ashing begins)**: Photoresist hardened during etching is removed by another RIE step in an oxygen environment. Charges continue accumulating on poly structures.
4. **Phase 4 (Resist removed)**: With photoresist gone, charges accumulate across the **entire surface** of the poly structures.

Phases (2)-(4) are the critical antenna-forming phases.

### Quantitative Analysis

When poly connects to a gate, two parasitic capacitors form:
- **C_GB**: Between gate and bulk (backgate) -- through the thin gate oxide (GOX)
- **C_PS**: Between poly and substrate -- through the thick field oxide (FOX/STI)

The charge per unit area on the gate oxide capacitor relative to the poly-substrate capacitor is:

$$q_{GB} = \beta \cdot q_{PS} \quad \text{with} \quad \beta = \frac{d_{FOX}}{d_{GOX}}$$

where d_FOX is the field oxide thickness (typically many 100 nm) and d_GOX is the gate oxide thickness (only a few nm). This enormous ratio beta explains why charge concentrates dangerously on the gate.

The resulting voltage at the gate oxide is:

$$V = \frac{e_{ant}}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta - 1} \cdot d_{FOX}$$

where:
- $\alpha = A_{ant} / A_{gate}$ is the ratio of antenna area to gate area
- $e_{ant} = Q / A_{ant}$ is the antenna efficiency (charge per unit area absorbed)
- $\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r$ is the permittivity of the dielectrics

**Key insight**: The voltage scales **almost linearly** with the ratio alpha (antenna area to gate area). This is what makes the antenna effect so dangerous. The linear scaling only saturates when alpha approaches beta (the FOX/GOX thickness ratio).

In practice, it is more the **perimeter P_ant** of the antenna (not its area) that determines charge absorption in phases (2) and (3), while the entire surface area acts in phase (4). Process design rules therefore prescribe maximum values for:
- $A_{ant} / A_{gate}$ (area ratio)
- $P_{ant} / A_{gate}$ (perimeter-to-area ratio)

These boundary values depend heavily on the specific back-end-of-line (BEOL) process steps.

### Countermeasure 1: Jumpers

Gates should be connected by metallic interconnects as closely as possible to minimize parasitic contact resistance and prevent dangerous poly antennas. When this is impossible:

- **Poly antenna protection**: Insert a **Metal1 jumper** (bridge) near the gate pin. This interrupts the poly antenna because metals do not exist yet when poly is being structured.
- **Metal antenna protection**: The antenna effect also applies to all metallic interconnects connected to gates. Interrupt these with jumpers in a **higher metal layer**. The long interconnect is isolated during etching, leaving only a short antenna in the upper metal layer.

### Countermeasure 2: Leakers (Antenna Diodes)

Leaker diodes discharge antenna charges as they accumulate. Two configurations:

**(a) NSD/p-sub diode** (for negative antenna charges):
- Reverse-biased in normal operation (acts as insulator)
- Discharges negative charge in forward-biased mode
- If the antenna connects to another NMOS transistor's source/drain, no additional diode is needed (charge discharges through source/drain into substrate)
- For positive charges, the diode's breakdown voltage V_BD can be used -- but in advanced processes, V_BD is too high for thin GOX protection

**(b) Extended structure: NSD/p-sub + PSD/Nwell** (for positive antenna charges):
- The Nwell/p-sub diode is reverse-biased in normal operation
- When the antenna is positively charged, the PSD/Nwell diode becomes forward-biased
- The **photoelectric effect** from the glowing RIE plasma excites additional charge carriers in the reverse-biased Nwell/p-sub diode, sufficiently increasing leakage current to discharge the antenna
- **Important**: A sufficiently large portion of the Nwell/p-sub diode must **not be covered by metals** so it receives adequate plasma light exposure

### Practical Considerations for Leakers vs. Jumpers

| Aspect | Leakers | Jumpers |
|---|---|---|
| Works with | Metal layers only | Both poly and metal |
| Area cost | Additional cell area needed | None (uses routing resources) |
| Power impact | Increases leakage current and IC power consumption | None |
| Schematic impact | Must be entered in schematic for LVS compliance | No schematic changes |
| Trend | Less favored in advanced processes | Increasingly popular in advanced processes |

Leakers are generally easier to use and preferred by many layout designers. However, jumpers are becoming more popular in advanced processes because leakers increase overall IC power consumption. Numerous algorithms have been proposed to automate antenna-effect mitigation in commercial routing tools.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Overvoltage protection is a critical reliability concern that must be addressed early in the physical design flow. The ESD protection concept directly impacts **floorplanning** (pin assignment, power rail routing) and **cell library design** (pad cells), connecting back to the design flow topics of [[5.3 Layout Techniques]]. The antenna effect links fabrication process knowledge from [[2.4-2.5 Photolithography and Etching]] to layout design rules, and its countermeasures (jumpers, leakers) must be considered during routing. Together with the surface and parasitic effects covered in [[7.2-7.3 Surface Effects and Interconnect Parasitics]], this section builds toward the migration effects in [[7.5 Migration Effects in Metal]], completing the picture of reliability-driven physical design constraints.
