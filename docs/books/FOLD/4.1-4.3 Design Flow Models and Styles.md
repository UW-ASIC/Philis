# 4.1-4.3 Design Flow, Models, and Styles

> **Chapter context**: Methodologies for Physical Design

## 4.1 The VLSI Design Flow

The development of any electronic product follows four primary stages: **Specification**, **Design**, **Fabrication**, and **Final Testing (Validation)**. The design stage is by far the most complex, and two strategies have proven essential for managing that complexity:

1. **Splitting** the overall design process into individual design steps.
2. **Appending a verification step** to each design step.

The result is a looped sequence: each design step is followed by a verification step, and if errors are found, the designer iterates back. This loop continues across all design steps until the layout is ready for fabrication.

### Stages of the VLSI Design Flow

The modern VLSI circuit design flow proceeds through the following stages, each at a progressively lower level of abstraction:

- **System Specification** -- Chip architects, circuit designers, marketers, and operations managers collectively define high-level goals: functionality, performance, physical dimensions, and fabrication technology. For ASICs, the customer is also involved.

- **Architectural Design** -- The basic architecture is selected to meet the system specification. Key decisions include:
  - Hardware/software partitioning
  - Integration of analog and mixed-signal blocks
  - Memory management (serial or parallel) and addressing scheme
  - Number and types of computational cores (CPUs, DSP units) and specific DSP algorithms
  - Internal/external communication and standard protocol support
  - Usage of hard and soft intellectual-property (IP) blocks
  - Pinout, packaging, and die-package interface
  - Power requirements
  - Choice of process technology and layer stacks

- **Functional and Logic Design** -- Functionality and connectivity for each module is defined. Functional design captures only the high-level response (input, output, timing behavior). Logic design is performed at the **register-transfer level (RTL)** using hardware description languages (HDLs) -- principally **VHDL** (established ~1983 by U.S. DoD) and **Verilog** (established ~1985 by Gateway Design Automation). HDL modules are thoroughly simulated before logic synthesis. **Logic synthesis tools** then convert HDL into gate-level netlists by mapping the described functionality onto specific circuit elements (standard cells, transistors) from a technology library. When synthesis is used, the circuit design step can be skipped.

- **Circuit Design** -- Critical low-level digital elements and all analog units are designed at the transistor level. This includes analog circuits, static RAM blocks, I/O, high-speed functions (multipliers), and ESD protection circuits. Correctness is verified with circuit simulation tools such as **SPICE**.

- **Physical Design** -- The input netlist is instantiated with geometric representations. All macros, cells, gates, and transistors are assigned shapes and sizes per fabrication layer, given spatial locations (placement), and have routing connections completed in metal layers. Physical design is **rules-based**: minimum wire distances, minimum widths, and other constraints reflect the physical limitations of the fabrication medium. Physical design directly impacts **circuit performance, area, reliability, power, and manufacturing yield**.

### Sub-steps of Physical Design

Physical design for digital ICs is itself divided into key sub-steps:

1. **Partitioning** -- Divides the circuit into smaller subcircuits or modules that can be designed or analyzed individually.
2. **Floorplanning** (chip planning) -- Determines shapes and arrangement of subcircuits/modules, locations of external ports and IP/macro blocks. Includes power (VDD) and ground (VSS/GND) routing throughout the chip.
3. **Placement** -- Determines spatial locations and orientations of all cells within each block.
4. **Clock Tree Synthesis** -- Determines buffering, gating (for power management), and routing of the clock signal to meet prescribed skew and delay requirements.
5. **Signal Routing**:
   - *Global routing* -- Allocates routing resources (routing tracks in global routing cells / gcells).
   - *Detailed routing* -- Assigns routes to specific metal layers and routing tracks within the global routing resources.
6. **Timing Closure** -- Optimizes circuit performance through specialized placement and routing techniques.

After detailed routing, parasitic resistances (R), capacitances (C), and inductances (L) are extracted from the completed layout and passed to timing analysis tools to verify functional behavior. If the analysis reveals errors or insufficient design margin (guardband), the design is incrementally updated.

For **analog circuits**, physical design uses a fundamentally different approach: geometric representations are created in real time using **layout generators** or by **manually drawing** them, rather than selecting predesigned cells from a library. Generators use specified electrical parameters (e.g., resistance value) to automatically produce the appropriate geometry (e.g., a resistor with calculated length and width). Additional requirements such as device matching for symmetry must be considered in placement, and wire widths must provide sufficient cross-sectional area for currents.

### Physical Verification

After physical design, the layout is verified through:

- **Design Rule Checking (DRC)** -- Verifies technology-imposed constraints:
  - Geometrical rules (minimum/maximum widths, spacings, extensions, intrusions, enclosures)
  - Layer density rules (for CMP planarity)
  - Antenna rule checking (prevents gate oxide damage during plasma etching from excess charge accumulation)
- **Layout vs. Schematic (LVS)** -- Extracts a netlist from the layout and compares it against the original netlist from logic synthesis or circuit design.
- **Parasitic Extraction (PEX)** -- Derives electrical parameters (R, C, L) from geometric representations for use in circuit verification.
- **Electrical Rule Checking (ERC)** -- Verifies power/ground connections, signal transition times (slew), capacitive loads, and fanouts.

### Layout Post Processing and Fabrication

The DRC-/LVS-/PEX-/ERC-clean layout undergoes post-processing: (1) chip finishing, (2) producing a reticle layout, and (3) layout-to-mask preparation. The design handoff to manufacturing is called **tapeout** (a historical term from the era of magnetic-tape delivery).

At the fab, the design is patterned onto layers using photolithographic processes with photomasks. Chips are fabricated on round silicon wafers, then tested and sorted into bins by functional/parametric results. The wafer is cut into **dies**. Functional dies are then packaged (DIP, PGA, BGA, etc.) with pin connections made by wire bonding or solder bumps (flip-chip). For multi-chip modules (MCMs), bare dies are integrated into the MCM and packaged separately.

## 4.2 Design Models

Design models provide a systematic framework for linking available design styles together, offering an overview of the various degrees of abstraction and key parameters at each level.

### 4.2.1 The Three-Dimensional (3D) Design Space

The design space for an integrated circuit can be organized along three orthogonal dimensions:

**1. Hierarchy** -- Manages structural complexity by decomposing the circuit from a general representation through intermediate steps to a fully detailed description. Moving from one hierarchical level to the next lower level splits problems into subproblems that can be solved by multiple experts in parallel -- a classic "divide and conquer" approach. For example, a microprocessor is subdivided into control unit, RAM, ROM, ALU; the ALU is further divided into multiplier, shifter, logical operations; the multiplier is organized so its adders can be accessed separately; and the adder is constructed with simple logic gates.

Benefits of hierarchy include:
- Reuse of small circuit parts (designed once, used many times)
- Faster design through parallel work
- Design clarity at each level

Drawback: design quality may suffer because optimization opportunities across hierarchy boundaries remain unused.

> **Note**: For digital circuits, hierarchical levels can be universally defined. For analog circuits, there is no standard classification -- each circuit may require a different set of hierarchical levels.

**2. Version** -- Describes the design history as the design evolves over time. Versions form a tree structure. Managing and monitoring design version consistency has long been neglected in EDA systems but is a major source of errors and an absolute must in modern design environments.

**3. View** -- Represents the circuit in different domains (e.g., schematic/circuit diagram and layout). Both representations describe the same design but in completely different ways. The number of different views can be high and depends on the attributes of the underlying design system. Views include: specification, testbenches, behavior, structure, simulation results, layout, mask data, schematic, netlist, list of devices, SPICE, EDIF, test programs, etc.

### 4.2.2 The Gajski-Kuhn Y-Chart

The Y-chart (introduced by Gajski and Kuhn in 1983) is one of the most widely used design models. It maps the "hierarchy" dimension to **concentric abstraction levels** (outer = more abstract, inner = more detailed) and splits the "view" dimension into **three domains** arranged as three axes:

**Behavioral Description (Functional View)** -- Describes the design's behavior: operations performed and dynamic response. Contains equations, functions, and algorithms.

**Structural Description (Structural View)** -- Designates the logical structure: the abstract implementation as a topographical configuration of components and their connections.

**Geometrical/Physical Realization (Physical View)** -- Describes how the design object is physically implemented: complete and precise geometrical layout and configuration of all components and connectivity.

#### The Five Abstraction Levels (outer to inner)

| Level | Behavioral View | Structural View | Physical View |
|---|---|---|---|
| **System (Architectural)** | Specifications, block diagrams with signal abstractions | Block symbols for CPUs, memory chips, etc. | Physical partitions |
| **Algorithmic** | Concurrent algorithms (signals, loops, variables, assignments) | ALUs and similar blocks | Floorplan, clusters |
| **Functional Block (Register Transfer)** | Data structures and data flows, module descriptions (RTL) | Registers, ALUs | Cells |
| **Logical** | Boolean equations | Gates, flip-flops | Standard cells (polygons) |
| **Circuit** | Differential equations | Transistors, capacitors | Polygons (actual geometries) |

**Navigating the Y-chart**:
- Moving **along a branch** (toward center) = refining or abstracting designs
- Moving **along a concentric circle** (to another branch) = changing the representation/view at the same abstraction level

The Y-chart also visualizes the terms for **synthesis** (behavioral synthesis, logic synthesis, circuit synthesis) and **generators** (layout generators), as well as the individual **design steps** (system design, logic design, transistor design, layout design).

The hierarchy dimension from the 3D design space complements the abstraction-level concept: an abstraction level derives from a modeling concept for a given domain, while a hierarchy embodies composition/decomposition ("dividing into smaller pieces" while staying on one domain branch).

## 4.3 Design Styles

A **design step** is a mapping between two design states. A **design style** is characterized by a sequence of design states, terminating in a geometrical representation (layout). Any design style can be mapped onto the Y-chart: each design state corresponds to the intersection of an axis and a concentric circle.

### 4.3.1 Full-Custom vs. Semi-Custom Design

Selecting the appropriate circuit-design style is critical as it affects **time-to-market** and **design cost**.

#### Full-Custom Design

- Subject to the **fewest design-methodology constraints** during layout generation -- blocks can be placed anywhere on the chip without restriction.
- Produces **very compact chips** with **highly optimized electrical properties**.
- **Laborious, time-consuming, and error-prone** due to low automation.
- Primarily used for **microprocessors** (high design cost amortized over large production volumes) and **analog circuits** (where extreme care is needed for matched layout and stringent electrical specs).
- Requires experienced layout designers; the design style is mainly manual.

> Analog circuits are **always** full-custom designed because they require many more degrees of freedom to handle their vast diversity of constraints.

#### Semi-Custom Design

Semi-custom design simplifies the process and reduces time-to-market and cost. It is divided into two methodologies:

##### Standard-Cell Design

A digital standard cell is a **predefined element with a fixed size** in layout that realizes a standard Boolean function (e.g., INV, NAND, NOR). Key characteristics:

- Designed in **multiples of a fixed cell height** with fixed VDD and GND port locations.
- Cell **widths vary** depending on the transistor network implemented.
- Placeable in **rows** with power and ground supply distributed by horizontal abutment.
- Signal ports are located throughout the cell area.
- Enables a **fully automated layout design flow** including placement and routing.
- Cell libraries are often provided at no cost by foundries and are pre-qualified for manufacturing.
- Trade-off: decreased power efficiency, layout density, or operating frequency compared to full-custom.
- Serves different market segments (e.g., ASICs) than full-custom (e.g., microprocessors).

Two standard-cell layout styles exist:
- Each row with its **own** power and ground rails
- **Shared** power and ground rails between adjacent rows (requires alternating cell orientations)

##### Macro Cells

- Usually **large pieces of logic** with standard, reusable functionality.
- Range from simple cells (a few standard cells) to complex subcircuits (embedded processor, memory block).
- Wide range of shapes and sizes; can be placed **anywhere** in the layout area.
- Enable **top-level assembly**: analog blocks, standard-cell blocks, "glue" logic, and buffers are combined to form the highest hierarchical level.

##### Gate Arrays

- Silicon chips with **pre-fabricated basic logic elements** (NAND, NOR) but **no connections**.
- Only the **interconnect (routing) layers** are added later after chip-specific requirements are known.
- Can be **mass-produced** as blanks, then customized.
- Only two routing tasks: *intracell routing* (connecting transistors within a cell to implement logic) and *intercell routing* (connecting logic blocks per the netlist).
- **Cheaper and faster** for low-volume production than standard-cell or macro-cell designs.
- Layout is greatly restricted, which simplifies routing algorithms.

##### Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs)

- Logic elements and interconnects are **prefabricated** and **user-configurable** via transistor switches.
- **Lookup tables (LUTs)** implement logic elements -- each can represent any k-input Boolean function (e.g., k = 4 or k = 5).
- **Switchboxes** join wires in adjacent routing channels to configure interconnects.
- LUT and switchbox configurations are read from external storage and stored locally in memory cells.
- **Main advantage**: customization without a fabrication facility -- dramatically reduces up-front investment and time-to-market.
- Design flow effectively ends at "netlist level".
- **Disadvantages**: typically run much slower and dissipate more power than ASICs; above certain production volumes (millions of chips), FPGAs become more expensive than ASICs.

#### Comparison of Design Styles

| Design Style | Design Cost | Mask Cost | Fab Cost | Performance | Economic Volume |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full custom | High | High | Low | High | High |
| Standard cell | Low | High | Medium | Medium | Wide range |
| Macro cell | High (low if reuse) | High | Low | High | Wide range |
| Gate array | Low | Medium | High | Low | Low |
| FPGA | Very low | None | High | Low | Low |

### 4.3.2 Top-Down, Bottom-Up, and Meet-in-the-Middle Design

Three design-direction styles exist:

**Top-Down** -- The design is progressively refined from the specification down to the finished layout. Very effective for dealing with complexity, but available degrees of freedom at lower levels cannot be fully leveraged since they are unknown at higher levels.

**Bottom-Up** -- A small circuit (e.g., a simple current mirror with two active devices) is designed starting at the electrical level of the Y-chart. A library of different circuit types is compiled. Macro blocks are created at the next higher level and stored for reuse. This process repeats until a circuit matching the specification is produced.

**Meet-in-the-Middle** -- Combines both styles: bottom-up at low levels and top-down at higher levels.

#### How these are applied in practice:

- **Modern digital circuits**: Standard elements (standard cells) are created bottom-up and stored in a library *before and independently of* the circuit design. The actual design of the circuit then uses these fixed library cells in a **fully top-down** flow. This is effectively a meet-in-the-middle approach.
- **Analog circuits**: The meet-in-the-middle style is the standard method. All basic elements including primitive devices are created bottom-up *during* the design phase (in real time) by hand or using layout generators guided by the designer. The entire analog/mixed-signal system is designed top-down, treating analog functional entities as building blocks.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

This sub-chapter establishes the foundational framework for understanding all subsequent physical design content. The VLSI design flow (Sec. 4.1) provides the sequential context in which every physical design task -- partitioning, floorplanning, placement, routing, and verification -- operates, and these tasks are detailed individually in [[4.4-4.5 Design Tasks and Optimization]]. The design models (Sec. 4.2), particularly the Gajski-Kuhn Y-chart, give a conceptual vocabulary for reasoning about where any design step sits in terms of abstraction level and domain view, which is essential for understanding the synthesis and analysis tools covered later. The design styles (Sec. 4.3) directly determine the physical design methodology: whether a design uses standard cells, gate arrays, or FPGAs dictates which placement and routing algorithms apply, and whether the flow is top-down, bottom-up, or meet-in-the-middle affects how the designer interacts with the tools described in Chapters 5 and 6.
