# 7.5 Migration Effects in Metal

> **Chapter context**: Addressing Reliability in Physical Design

## Key Concepts

Besides overvoltage events (covered in [[7.4 Overvoltage Protection]]), **material transport (migration)** in metallic interconnects can cause irreversible damage in ICs. Three types of migration threaten circuit reliability in metal connectivity architectures: **electromigration**, **thermal migration**, and **stress migration**. Although discussed separately, these are closely coupled processes -- their driving forces are linked with each other and with the resultant change in migration.

---

### 7.5.1 Electromigration (EM)

Current flow through a conductor produces two forces on the metal ions in the lattice:

1. **Electrostatic force** $F_{\text{field}}$: Caused by the electric field in the interconnect. Since the positive metal ions are shielded by negative conduction electrons, this force is negligible in most cases.
2. **Electron wind force** $F_{\text{wind}}$: Generated by momentum transfer between conduction electrons and metal ions. This is the dominant force and the primary cause of EM. It acts in the direction of current flow.

If the resulting force in the direction of the electron wind exceeds a material-specific **activation energy** $E_a$, a directed migration process begins. Material transport occurs from cathode (--) to anode (+), i.e., in the direction of electron motion.

**Migration paths** are material-dependent, determined by the relative activation energies for:
- (i) Migration within the crystal bulk
- (ii) Migration along grain boundaries
- (iii) Migration on surfaces

The dominant mechanism depends on which activation energy is lowest.

#### EM Failure Mechanisms

Inhomogeneities in fabricated interconnects cause divergences in migration flow, producing:
- **Tensile stress** leading to **voids** (metal depletions)
- **Compressive stress** leading to **hillocks** (metal accumulations)
- **Whiskering**: spontaneous growth of tiny filiform hairs from metallic surfaces

Modern process failures are mostly due to **voids**, which develop in two phases:
1. **Void nucleation phase**: Tensile stress increases over time but no void has formed yet. When stress reaches a critical threshold, nucleation occurs.
2. **Void growth phase**: Visible as increased line resistance. The line does not fully open in most cases due to residual conduction through the surrounding metal liner.

Two categories of void damage exist:
- **Line depletion**: Electron flow from a via to an attached line causes material accumulation at a barrier (cap/liner layers obstruct flow).
- **Via depletion** (via voiding): Electron flow from a line into a via. As the line-width-to-via-width ratio increases, the via carries proportionally more current, making it more susceptible.

#### Susceptibility by Wire Type

| Wire Type | Current Direction | EM Risk |
|-----------|------------------|---------|
| Power / analog lines | Unidirectional | **Most susceptible** |
| Digital signal / clock lines | Bidirectional | Lower risk (bidirectional "self-healing" due to reversed, compensatory material migration) |

### 7.5.2 Thermal Migration (TM)

Thermal migration (also called **thermomigration**) is driven by **temperature gradients**. Higher temperatures increase average atomic speeds, so atoms in hotter regions have a greater probability of dislocation than those in cooler regions. The result is net diffusion (mass transport) in the direction of the **negative temperature gradient** -- from hot to cold.

**Main causes of temperature gradients in metal wires:**
- **Joule heating** inside the wire caused by high currents
- **External heating** from nearby high-performance transistors
- **External cooling** from through-silicon vias (TSVs) connected to heat sinks, combined with low thermal conduction of narrow wires embedded in thermally insulating dielectrics

A notable property of TM: it **self-moderates**. Because heat is coupled to the transported atoms, thermal migration itself contributes to thermal transport, directly reducing its own driving force. This contrasts with EM, where current density is only indirectly reduced (via increased resistance from damage).

---

### 7.5.3 Stress Migration (SM)

Stress migration (also called **stress voiding** or **stress induced voiding, SIV**) is atomic diffusion driven by **mechanical stress gradients**. Atoms flow into regions of tensile stress and out of regions under compressive stress, i.e., diffusion follows the negative mechanical tension gradient. The vacancy concentration adjusts to match the mechanical tension distribution.

**Main driving forces for SM:**
- **Thermal expansion mismatch**: Different coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) between metal, dielectric, and die material, combined with temperature changes from fabrication to storage/operation
- **Electromigration**: EM-induced stress gradients trigger SM as a counteracting process
- **Deformation through packaging**: External mechanical loading

#### The Role of Vacancies

Metal lattices contain vacancies (unoccupied atomic positions). Although aligned with the lattice grid, vacancies occupy less volume than atoms would at the same positions, so a crystal with vacancies has a smaller volume than one with atoms filling all positions. This makes vacancies central to the SM mechanism.

The stress gradient drives atoms from high-pressure (compressive) regions to tensile regions and pushes vacancies in the opposite direction. Temperature is critical because it enables the "place-changing" of atoms that allows vacancies to move.

#### External vs. Internal Stress

- **External stress**: The lattice is stretched or compressed. Atoms migrate to stretched regions; atoms in compressed regions are "pushed out," increasing vacancies there. The atomic flux continues until a stress-free static state is reached.
- **Internal stress (from EM)**: Greater vacancy concentration appears in tensile regions. SM balances this to a steady state where the EM-driven atomic flux is compensated by SM.

#### Void Nucleation

If vacancies from external or internal (EM) stress exceed a threshold, **vacancy supersaturation** occurs and vacancies coalesce to form a void (void nucleation). The resulting crack eliminates the tensile stress.

### 7.5.4 Mitigating Electromigration

EM cannot be prevented -- it can only be **offset** or **restricted in its effect**. The fundamental parameter is **current density**:

$$J = \frac{I}{A}$$

where $I$ is the flowing current and $A$ is the cross-sectional area of the interconnect (Eq. 7.6). Both factors trend in the wrong direction as technology scales: $I$ increases and $A$ decreases, so $J$ grows.

Two main models characterize EM risk:
- **Black's model**: Calculates reliability of a single wire segment based on current density $J$
- **Korhonen model**: Tracks material flow in all branches of a net within one metallization layer using hydrostatic stress $\sigma$. A void nucleates when $\sigma$ exceeds a predefined threshold $\sigma_{\text{threshold}}$

**Three strategies** for EM mitigation:
1. **(i) Reduce material transport** in the wire
2. **(ii) Raise tolerated boundary values** (increase permissible current density limits)
3. **(iii) Reduce required current density** in the layout

#### Mitigation Measure 1: Length Limitations (Blech Length)

Any interconnect shorter than a threshold length (the **Blech length**) will not fail by EM, because mechanical stress buildup causes reverse stress migration (SM) that compensates the EM flow. This makes such segments "EM immortal."

**Implementation approaches:**
- **Segment subdivision**: Divide a long interconnect into segments below the Blech length. All segments become EM immortal and void formation is prevented. The disadvantage is significantly more routing resources due to additional layer changes (vias).
- **Segment length balancing**: Shift existing vias (without adding new ones) to equalize segment lengths. This reduces hydrostatic stress in the highest-risk (longest) segment while conserving routing resources.

#### Mitigation Measure 2: Reservoirs

Enlarged via overlaps and similar features provide extra metal material for migration, preventing void growth from damaging the interconnect. The reservoir effect shifts the prevailing equilibrium during void growth saturation.

**Caveat**: Reservoirs can have an **adverse effect** in nets with current-flow reversals, as they reduce the beneficial stress migration in those cases.

#### Mitigation Measure 3: Via Configurations (Via-Above vs. Via-Below)

In Cu dual-Damascene interconnects, the orientation of vias matters:
- **Via-below** configurations are preferred for EM robustness: voids nucleate on the top surface of Cu, and via-below structures allow higher permissible void volumes before failure
- **Via-above** configurations are more susceptible because the void forms directly at the via connection point

#### Mitigation Measure 4: Via Arrays

Multiple (redundant) vias increase EM robustness. Critical placement rule: vias should be placed **in line with the current direction** so all current paths have equal length. This ensures uniform current distribution and avoids localized current-density spikes between vias. If vias connect perpendicularly arranged interconnects, the "inner" via(s) can suffer current overload from the direction change.

#### Mitigation Measure 5: Corner Bends

90-degree corner bends must be avoided because current density at right-angle bends is **significantly higher** than at oblique angles. 135-degree bends are preferred, as the current-density distribution is much more uniform.

#### Mitigation Measure 6: Current-Optimized Net Topology

Standard routing topologies (RSMT -- Rectilinear Steiner Minimum Tree, trunk tree) minimize wire length and congestion but are not EM-optimal. EM-aware net topologies split currents into different paths, reducing EM-induced mechanical stress.

For a four-pin net (one output driving three inputs), the tradeoffs are:
| Topology | Wire Length | EM Stress ($\sigma$) |
|----------|------------|---------------------|
| RSMT | Lowest | Highest |
| Trunk tree | Slightly more | Slightly less |
| Current-optimized | Most | **Lowest** (most EM robust) |

**EDA tool support**: Current-driven routing, wire resizing, and stress-aware routing methodologies implement these measures. For accurate analysis (especially in analog and power circuits), finite element method (FEM) simulation is needed to capture the spatial impact of current density and hydrostatic stress.

### 7.5.5 Mitigating Thermal and Stress Migration

TM and SM are closely coupled through thermal expansion and often act in the same direction, so their mitigation strategies overlap.

**Step 1 -- Apply EM mitigation measures first**: Reducing EM also reduces SM (as a counteracting process to EM) and TM (if local current densities and Joule heating decrease). Reducing current density of alternating currents in signal nets helps prevent TM.

**Step 2 -- Placement of high-power transistors**: Distribute transistors with high loads over a large die area to avoid local hot spots.

**Step 3 -- Routing for low current density and thermal gradients**:
- Use wide wires for high currents (e.g., higher metal layers in the stack)
- Avoid adjacent wires carrying high current simultaneously
- Route along isothermal lines where feasible -- stay along regions of equal temperature until leaving the high-gradient zone, then lead the wire away from the hotspot (cf. Fig. 6.30 in [[6.6 Layout Techniques for Analog Circuits]])

**Step 4 -- Thermal wires and thermal vias**: Experienced layout designers add extra metal wires and vias purely as heat conductors (not for electrical current). These significantly improve heat conductivity and reduce thermal hot spots and gradients.

**Step 5 -- Reducing power consumption**: Lower transistor power consumption directly reduces temperature gradients.

#### SM-Specific Measures

- **Normalize material distribution**: Add assistant features in the metal stack to create a uniform CTE distribution. However, these features may conflict with thermal wires/vias (which tend to accumulate metal locally).
- **Use "soft" (low-stiffness) dielectrics**: Enables unhindered thermal expansion of interconnects, so almost no mechanical stress is introduced. Fully "relaxed" wires experience negligible SM.
- **Reduce interconnect material density**: Use fewer available interconnect materials (which are stiffer than dielectric) or introduce less dense regions around mechanically stressed wires. **Caveat**: This contradicts EM mitigation, because SM is often used to counteract EM and extend wire lifetime.

#### Special Considerations for 3D-ICs

Three-dimensional circuits with multiple active layers require special attention:
- **TSVs** are large obstacles with different CTE than bulk substrate, introducing mechanical stress at TSV locations during fabrication and operation
- Wires near TSVs are especially affected by SM
- **Keep-out zones** around TSVs prevent SM damage in nearby wires and minimize stress-related mobility changes in active devices
- **Thermal TSVs** (thermal through-silicon vias) can lower thermal gradients, mitigating TM in 3D-ICs

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Migration effects in metal represent one of the most critical long-term reliability concerns in physical design, sitting alongside overvoltage protection ([[7.4 Overvoltage Protection]]) and parasitic effects ([[7.1 Parasitic Effects in Silicon]]) within the broader reliability chapter. Understanding these failure mechanisms is essential because they constrain fundamental routing decisions -- wire widths, via placement, net topologies, and even the placement of high-power transistors. As technology nodes shrink, current densities rise and cross-sectional areas decrease, making EM-aware design increasingly non-optional. The mitigation measures described here (Blech length segmentation, via configurations, thermal wires, keep-out zones) directly inform the routing and verification steps covered in [[5.3 Steps of Physical Design]] and the analog layout techniques in [[6.6 Layout Techniques for Analog Circuits]].
