# 5.3 Primary Steps: Partitioning, Placement, and Routing

> **Chapter context**: Steps in Physical Design

This section covers the core algorithmic stages that transform a netlist into a physical layout: partitioning and floorplanning, placement, and routing. It also addresses three specialized design flows -- symbolic compaction, standard-cell design, and PCB physical design.

---

## 5.3.1 Partitioning and Floorplanning

### Partitioning

Partitioning reduces design complexity by dividing a large circuit into smaller, independently manageable modules. Partitioning can occur at multiple levels of abstraction:

- **System level** -- a complex system is broken into subsystems, each realized as a separate PCB that can be designed and manufactured independently.
- **Board level** -- a PCB's functionality is split into modules that become individual ICs or multi-chip modules (MCMs).
- **Chip level** -- an IC is divided into circuit blocks that can be designed independently, reducing the combinatorial complexity of placement and routing.

The **primary optimization goal** of partitioning is to minimize the number of connections between subcircuits (inter-partition nets), since these connections consume routing resources and introduce parasitic delay. Each partition must also satisfy all design constraints (area, timing, power).

### Blocks: Hard vs. Soft

Once a partition's shape is considered, it is called a **block**:

- **Soft block** -- dimensions are variable; the block is defined only by its contents (cells and connections). Its shape and size are determined during floorplanning.
- **Hard block** -- dimensions and area are fixed. Typical examples include pre-verified IP blocks that are reused across designs. These have fixed physical characteristics that cannot be altered.

### Floorplanning

The arrangement of all blocks, including their locations, is the **floorplan**. Floorplanning transforms soft partitions into sized, placed blocks and assigns every externally-connected pin to a specific net (pin assignment). Floorplanning determines the **external** physical characteristics of each partition (shape, dimensions, pin locations), while placement and routing (the next steps) determine their **internal** characteristics.

Floorplanning is still largely a **manual process** augmented by automatic tools. The challenge is its multi-objective optimization nature -- simultaneous block sizing, block placement, congestion management, and timing closure must all be balanced. Experienced designers typically refine automatically generated floorplans.

#### Power and Ground Planning

The floorplanning area (top cell) is enclosed by one or more sets of **power (VDD) and ground (GND) rings**. Horizontal and vertical power/ground segments are connected through vias. When these stripes run at regular intervals in both directions, the style is called a **power mesh**.

Important checks after power/ground network creation:
- Verify design rule compliance and connectivity.
- Check for **wide metal** exceeding manufacturing tolerances -- wide metal lines cannot be produced with uniform thickness due to CMP (chemical-mechanical polishing) effects: they tend to become thin in the middle and thick at the edges.

#### Mixed-Signal Considerations

When analog and digital blocks coexist on the same chip:
- Noise injection from digital blocks into sensitive analog blocks through shared power/ground must be avoided.
- Analog and digital power/ground connections should be designed and planned **separately** (dedicated power supply concept for every mixed-signal IC).

#### Macro Placement

Macros are large, pre-defined blocks (memories, analog subcircuits, etc.). Proper macro placement has an outsized impact on final IC quality.

Key rules of thumb:
- Place macros so that the remaining **standard cell area is continuous** (not segmented). A segmented cell area forces routing connections around macros, increasing wire length significantly.
- A standard cell area with close to a **1:1 aspect ratio** allows placers to work most efficiently and minimizes total wire length.
- Macro ports should **face the standard cells** or the core area.
- Macro orientation should be **aligned with the respective routing layers**.

#### Pin Assignment

Pin assignment is performed after block relative placement is defined. All signals are allocated to unique pin locations to optimize routability and minimize electrical parasitics both inside and outside the blocks.

#### Clock Planning

Clock distribution networks deliver clock signals to all clocked objects with **minimized clock skew**. Two common structures:

- **Mesh structure** -- provides highly uniform clock distribution.
- **Tree structure** (e.g., H-tree, X-tree) -- equalizes routing path lengths to all leaf cells.

High-performance designs often depend on manually implemented clock networks because they must account for line resistance and capacitance. In hierarchical design, the main clock network is manually drafted at chip level, and the clock delay for each block is calculated to plan the clock tree accordingly.

#### Special Rules for Mixed-Signal and Smart Power Chips

- **Sensor-scanning circuits** should be placed at the chip periphery, directly adjacent to their bond pads. Analog sensor inputs are low-amplitude and susceptible to interference, so paths should be as short as possible -- converting to digital signals at their point of entry.
- **Power stages** (large DMOS transistors) should be placed at current entry/exit points. Bond pads are placed directly on the source/drain of power DMOS transistors so current does not flow laterally on the chip. Multiple bond wires may operate in parallel for currents up to **10 A**.

## 5.3.2 Placement

After floorplanning determines block outlines, pin locations, and power/ground supply lines, **placement** determines the locations of basic devices (standard cells) within each block. The primary optimization objective is minimizing the estimated total wire length.

### IC Placement: Global and Detailed

**Global placement** assigns general locations to movable objects. It:
- Often neglects specific shapes and sizes of placeable objects.
- Does not attempt to align objects with valid grid rows/columns.
- Allows some overlaps between placed objects.
- Focuses on judicious global positioning and overall density distribution.

**Detailed placement** (including **legalization**) refines global placement by:
- Aligning objects with rows and columns.
- Removing overlap.
- Minimizing displacements from global placement positions.
- Incrementally improving locations via local operations (e.g., cell swapping, row shifting).

Global and detailed placement typically have comparable runtimes, but global placement often requires **much more memory** and is harder to parallelize.

### Clusters and Regions

- **Cluster** -- a set of cells placed near each other; location is undefined until all cells within it are placed. Used to control closeness of timing-critical components.
- **Region** -- a pre-defined placement area; location is defined before cell placement begins.

After clusters and regions are defined, global placement distributes cells uniformly for minimum wire lengths and optimized congestion, followed by detailed placement refinement.

### Congestion-Driven Placement

Congestion-driven placement accounts for required routing resources and aims to **equalize expected routing congestion**. Without it, uniformly distributed cells can create areas where routing demand exceeds capacity (unrouteable congestion). Almost all modern automatic placers consider routing congestion.

### Timing-Driven Placement

Two approaches:

- **Path-based** -- constraints applied to delay paths of entire (sub)circuits. A path is a sequence of nets through which a signal propagates.
- **Net-based** -- individual nets on critical paths are assigned higher weights, hoping that handling critical nets well implicitly optimizes path delay.

Modern placers use a **hybrid approach**, interleaving weighted connectivity-driven placements with timing analysis.

### PCB Placement

For PCBs, placement is divided into two phases:
1. Placing all components with **fixed locations** (connectors, pads).
2. Placing remaining devices, iteratively improving initial placement with regard to wire lengths and other objectives.

## 5.3.3 Routing

Routing connects pins that share the same electrical potential using wire segments. It is one of the most complex and time-consuming physical design steps. Even after successful placement, routing can fail, run for unacceptable times, or deliver only partial solutions.

### Special Nets: Clock, Power/Ground, Differential Pairs

Special nets are embedded into the placement structure **before** common signal nets:

- **Clock nets** -- routed using structures that minimize skew: balanced trees, H-trees, or X-trees. Each structure equalizes routing lengths to all clocked cells.
- **Power/ground networks** -- routed using power/ground meshes (on different layers) or planar tree structures. A tree structure is chosen when an extra-thick metal layer is required for current-carrying capacity and only one such layer is available.
- **Differential-pair routing** -- two wires are routed "equally" to achieve identical electrical impedances (R, C) on both lines. This provides: (i) noise immunity because common-mode noise affects both conductors identically and the receiver detects only the difference, and (ii) minimized electromagnetic interference (EMI) from the tightly coupled pair.

### Digital Circuit Routing: Global and Detailed

The enormous complexity of routing millions of nets in digital ICs requires splitting into two stages:

**Global routing:**
- The chip area is represented by a **coarse routing grid**.
- Available routing resources are represented by edges with capacities in a grid graph.
- Nets are tentatively assigned to routing regions called **gcells** (global routing cells).
- gcell capacity is defined by: number of routing layers, cell dimensions, minimum wire widths and spacings (per layer).
- After global routing, routers report over/underflow of gcells in a **congestion map**. Detailed routing should only be attempted if the overflow ratio does not exceed 1 in too many places.

**Detailed routing:**
- Refines global routes by assigning wire segments to **specific routing tracks**.
- Does not alter the net configuration from global routing -- if the global routing solution is poor, detailed routing quality will suffer.
- Key challenges include **net ordering** (which nets are routed first) and **pin ordering** (in what order pins within a net are connected). Both have dramatic impacts because routing is inherently sequential -- resources used by one net are unavailable to later nets.
- **Net weight** assigns a numerical priority. High-priority nets (timing-critical, many pins, special function) avoid unnecessary detours even at the cost of detouring less important nets.

### Area Routing (Analog, MCM, PCB)

For analog circuits, MCMs, and PCBs, the smaller number of nets makes global routing unnecessary. **Area routing** embeds nets directly in a single step:
1. Determine net ordering.
2. Assign nets to routing layers.
3. Perform path search for each net within assigned layers.
4. Apply **rip-up and reroute** for nets blocked by earlier routes.

**Grid-based routers** impose evenly spaced tracks (defined by design rules) across the routing area, guaranteeing DRC-clean routes by construction.

**Grid-less routers** allow variable wire widths and spacings, producing superior solutions at significantly higher runtime cost. Best applied to small circuits or specific critical nets (clock, high-current).

### Rip-Up and Reroute

When an early-routed net blocks a later connection, the blocking net is removed ("ripped up"), the current connection is embedded, and the removed net is rerouted on a different path. This is time-consuming and error-prone, so it should be applied only to a few remaining open nets.

After detailed routing, a **search-and-repair** phase resolves remaining DRC violations (wire shorts, fill notches, metal spacing).

### Via Optimization

- **Via minimization** -- removes unnecessary vias by reducing jogs in wire connections, since vias impose performance and reliability losses.
- **Via doubling** -- single vias are doubled where space permits, providing: (i) increased via yield, (ii) reduced electrical resistance, and (iii) better immunity against electromigration.

## 5.3.4 Physical Design Using Symbolic Compaction

**Compaction** rearranges a placed-and-routed topological group of objects to minimize layout area while maintaining minimum spacing and design rule correctness.

**Symbolic compaction** is used when a symbolic layout methodology is adopted. Designers create layouts using **stick diagrams** -- abstract representations of layout objects -- rather than actual geometric shapes (wires, contacts). After the symbolic layout is completed through partitioning, placement, and routing, a compaction tool generates the actual **mask layout** when a specific technology is assigned.

The key advantage: **design-rule independence**. As long as the basic device construction principle does not change, the same symbolic layout can be compiled into mask layouts for different technology nodes simply by applying different design-rule sets. This makes it an efficient technique for adjusting standard-cell libraries across technology nodes.

## 5.3.5 Physical Design Using Standard Cells

Standard cells have **fixed cell height** and defined VDD/GND port locations; cell widths vary by transistor network complexity. This restricted layout style enables row-based placement.

### Standard-Cell Layout Structure

- Cells are placed in rows with **power (VDD) and ground (GND) rails** at the top and bottom of each row.
- Power/ground rails connect to **vertical power bars**, which connect to outer power/ground pads.
- The user can request a specific **height-width ratio** for the standard-cell core; the automatic placer attempts to achieve this ratio.

### Routing in Standard-Cell Designs

- **Two-layer structure**: routing occurs in **channels** (spaces between cell rows). Signal ports connect vertically into the channel for routing to other cells. Multi-channel connections require feedthrough cells to cross standard-cell rows. Channel height adjusts to accommodate the number of nets.
- **More than two layers**: routing can be performed **over the cell** (OTC routing). Channels can be eliminated and rows placed side by side, with alternating rows flipped to share power/ground structures.

The finished standard-cell layout can serve as a standalone chip or as a **digital macro cell** within a larger mixed-signal floorplan.

## 5.3.6 Physical Design of Printed Circuit Boards

PCB design follows the general physical design flow but has specialized considerations due to component diversity, EMC constraints, and manufacturing requirements.

### PCB Design Flow

The three main steps are:
1. **Schematic entry** -- symbolic component representations are wired into visual diagrams. Schematics support ERC (electrical rule check) for catching unconnected pins, duplicate references, or illegal power net connections. Simulation tools evaluate circuit behavior virtually.
2. **Layout design** -- defines board outline, places components, and generates connections.
3. **Post processing** -- generates manufacturing documentation files.

### Landpatterns (Footprints)

Each schematic symbol is associated with a **landpattern** (footprint) that represents the physical dimensions of the component. A landpattern includes pads, solder areas, and holes needed to connect the component to the PCB.

### PCB Placement

- **Manual placement** -- designer decides component locations, guided by rubber-band display of net connections and overflow/routability information. Automatic rule checking prevents invalid placements.
- **Automatic placement** -- used for non-critical components after critical or pre-assigned components are manually placed and "fixed" (protected from modification).

### PCB Routing

Routing creates **traces** connecting component pins using specified routing layers:

- **Power layers** contain VDD and GND nets.
- **Signal layers** contain signal traces.
- Some PCBs allow mixed power/signal on the same layer.
- **Vias** connect between layers: through-hole, blind, and buried vias (the latter two are more expensive).

**Autorouters** automatically assign conductor tracks in compliance with design rules. They work best for digital circuit parts (e.g., bus systems with many parallel data lines). Analog and mixed-signal portions still require manual routing for best results.

### Keepout Areas

Keepout areas are regions free of components and traces, placed as layer-specific or all-layer barriers. Common example: keepout rings around screw holes for PCB mounting, ensuring components/traces do not overlap with screw heads.

### EMC-Compliant Layout Design

PCB layout is highly susceptible to electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) problems. Key rules:

1. **Avoid current loops** in signal and power lines. Run signal and return conductors close together -- interference coupling is approximately proportional to loop area.
2. **Implement defined return paths** -- current takes the path of least impedance. Ground planes are superior to separate return lines. On PCBs without ground planes, run the return line near the signal line on a different layer.
3. **Route around ground plane breaks** -- if a break exists in the ground plane, route the forward current trace around it (not across it).
4. **Split traces near destinations** -- when one output drives several ICs (e.g., clock distribution), split the trace as close to the destination modules as possible.
5. **Capacitive decoupling** -- insert a grounded trace between two signal traces that need decoupling. Route high-switching-rate signals away from sensitive analog inputs.
6. **Separate analog and digital grounds**.

### Manufacturing Documentation

After successful DRC, manufacturing documentation is generated containing:
- **Documentation data** (technology-independent): schematic, netlist, component list.
- **Manufacturing data** (technology-specific): mask descriptions for each layer, drilling tables, assembly data, stencil information for solder paste, solder resist mask, silk screen overlay.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Section 5.3 is the core of the physical design flow described in Chapter 5. It sits between [[5.1-5.2 Netlist and Design Models|netlist generation and design modeling]] (which produce the input data structures) and [[5.4-5.5 Verification and Post Processing|verification and post-processing]] (which validate the physical layout and prepare it for manufacturing). The partitioning-placement-routing sequence is the fundamental pipeline that every physical layout must pass through, regardless of whether the target is an IC or PCB. The quality of each step directly constrains what the next step can achieve -- poor floorplanning leads to placement difficulties, poor placement leads to routing congestion, and routing failures often require iterating back to earlier stages. Understanding these dependencies is essential for diagnosing layout closure failures and for making informed trade-offs during the design process.
