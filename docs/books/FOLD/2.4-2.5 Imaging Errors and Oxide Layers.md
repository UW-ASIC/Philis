# 2.4-2.5 Imaging Errors and Oxide Layers

> **Chapter context**: Technology Know-How: From Silicon to Devices

## Key Concepts

### 2.4 Imaging Errors

Imaging errors are deviations between the intended layout geometry and the structures actually produced on the wafer during photolithography. They fall into two broad categories: **deterministic** (predictable and correctable) and **stochastic** (unpredictable, handled by design margins). Understanding these errors is essential for layout design because they directly dictate the design rules that constrain how geometries are drawn.

#### 2.4.1 Overlay Errors

Overlay errors arise because each photomask layer is aligned independently to the wafer. Multiple sources contribute:

- **Temperature fluctuations** during exposure cause the wafer to expand or contract non-uniformly. Keeping exposure temperatures constant reduces this effect, but it cannot be completely eliminated.
- **Irreversible wafer deformation** from high-temperature process steps causes displacements and scaling that vary from wafer to wafer.
- The cumulative effect of all overlay error sources can produce **shifting, rotation, scaling, and perspective distortion** of one mask layer relative to another (see Fig. 2.5).

The critical constraint is that the **maximum permissible overlay error must not exceed the minimum feature size** -- and should typically be much less.

**Impact on layout design**: Consider contacts that must be fully covered by metal. Since the Contact and Metal1 layers are patterned with different photomasks, an *enclosure design rule* is specified: structures in the Metal1 layer must overlap structures in the Contact layer on all sides by at least the maximum permissible overlay error. This guarantees full coverage even in worst-case alignment scenarios (Fig. 2.6).

Design rules derived from overlay errors include:
1. **Extension and intrusion rules** -- minimum overlap for two overlapping geometrical elements on different layers (to ensure features also overlap on the wafer).
2. **Enclosure rules** -- minimum amount one element must enclose another (e.g., metal enclosing a contact).
3. **Spacing rules** -- minimum distance between elements on different layers (to ensure clearance on the wafer).

#### 2.4.2 Edge Shifts

Edge shifts are **additive** changes to the boundary lines of structures -- the edges shift outward (positive shift, enlarging the structure) or inward (negative shift, shrinking it). This is distinct from scaling: with edge shifts, a fixed value is added to or subtracted from every edge, regardless of the structure's size. A small square and a large rectangle each lose the same absolute amount from every edge.

- Edge shifts are **layer-specific** and defined for every semiconductor process.
- They are **deterministic** and therefore correctable.
- Correction is performed by **pre-sizing** during mask preparation: if the process causes an edge shift of value *k*, the layout geometries' edges are shifted by *-k* before transfer to the photomask, so that the final wafer structure matches the intended layout (Fig. 2.7).

This pre-sizing step is automated during the layout-to-mask preparation process (layout post-processing).

#### 2.4.3 Diffraction Effects

As feature sizes shrink relative to the exposure wavelength, **diffraction at the chromium edges of the photomask** increasingly distorts the transferred image. Two key effects emerge:

- **Line-end shortening (LES)**: lines become shorter than intended at their ends.
- **Corner rounding (CR)**: sharp corners are rounded off.

When the feature size exceeds the wavelength, these effects are negligible. Once the ratio drops below 1 (sub-wavelength lithography), the distortions become significant.

**Optical Proximity Correction (OPC)** compensates for diffraction by modifying the photomask geometry:

- **Rule-based OPC** (for moderate distortion): simple geometric corrections applied based on structural shape rules:
  - *Hammer heads* are attached to the ends of thin lines to counteract line-end shortening.
  - *Added serifs* (small squares) are placed at outer corners to sharpen them (more light exposure).
  - *Punched serifs / jogs* are cut from inner corners to reduce overexposure.
  - Line width changes from interference between parallel lines can also be corrected.
- **Model-based OPC** (for severe distortion at very small feature sizes): the exposure result depends on surrounding features, so corrections must be calculated individually for all structures using wave-optical simulation models. These calculations are very CPU-intensive but are embarrassingly parallel (independent per structure), so they can be distributed across many processors.

#### 2.4.4 Reference to Physical Design

The two categories of imaging error are handled differently in the design flow:

**Deterministic errors** (edge shifts, diffraction effects):
- Known in advance in type and magnitude.
- Corrected automatically during **layout post-processing** before photomask production.
- The layout designer works with ideal geometries; the corrections are applied transparently.
- Caveat: not all edge shifts are neutralized by pre-sizing. Some layout features will look different from the wafer result -- the PDK documentation should be consulted.
- Model-based OPC computation time can be significant and should be planned for in the project schedule.

**Stochastic errors** (overlay errors):
- Cannot be predicted precisely, only bounded.
- Handled by specifying **design rules** that build in sufficient margin: minimum width, minimum spacing, enclosure, extension/intrusion rules.
- These rules ensure correct functionality even in worst-case alignment scenarios.

### 2.5 Applying and Structuring Oxide Layers

Silicon dioxide (SiO2) -- commonly called "oxide" -- is one of silicon's greatest advantages over other semiconductors. Its properties make it indispensable:

- **Excellent electrical insulator** and dielectric for capacitors.
- **Mechanically stable**, supporting robust layer stacks.
- **Easy to fabricate** via multiple methods.
- **Serves as a masking layer** for many process steps.
- **Transparent**, allowing alignment marks to be seen through it and enabling optical applications (LEDs, solar cells, photodiodes).

#### 2.5.1 Thermal Oxidation

In thermal oxidation, oxygen reacts with the silicon at the wafer surface to grow SiO2. Once an oxide layer forms, continued growth requires oxygen atoms to **diffuse through the existing oxide** to reach the silicon underneath, so the growth rate decreases with increasing oxide thickness.

**Key dimensional relationship**: The oxide grows from the original silicon surface approximately **44% downward** into the silicon (consuming that silicon) and **56% upward** above the original surface (Fig. 2.9). This 44/56 split is important for understanding oxide step heights and LOCOS extensions.

Two thermal oxidation processes exist:

| Property | Dry Oxidation | Wet Oxidation |
|---|---|---|
| **Reactants** | Pure O2 | O2 + steam (H2O) |
| **Reaction** | Si + O2 -> SiO2 | Si + 2H2O -> SiO2 + 2H2 |
| **Temperature** | 1000-1200 C (~2000 F) | 950-1000 C (~1800 F) |
| **Growth rate** | Very slow | Much faster |
| **Oxide quality** | High (few vacancy defects) | Lower (harder to control) |
| **Typical use** | Gate oxide (GOX), capacitor dielectrics | Field oxide (FOX) |

- **Gate oxide (GOX)**: the very thin oxide under the gate of a FET -- requires highest quality, so dry oxidation is used.
- **Field oxide (FOX)**: the first thick oxide layer on silicon, used to **laterally insulate regions** from each other. Named after the "field" (non-active) regions of the chip. Produced by wet oxidation for speed.

#### 2.5.2 Oxidation by Deposition

When the silicon surface is already covered by other layers, thermal oxidation cannot be used because there is no exposed silicon to consume. Instead, oxide is **deposited** from external sources of both silicon and oxygen. Various deposition methods exist (CVD, sputtering, etc.). This type of oxide is used primarily to **electrically isolate metallization layers** from one another in the back-end-of-line (BEOL).

#### 2.5.3 Oxide Structuring by Etching

To create patterned oxide structures, etching removes oxide from selected areas. The process follows the standard photolithographic sequence: deposit photoresist, expose through photomask, develop, etch exposed oxide, remove photoresist (Fig. 2.10).

**Wet Etching**
- Uses a **fluid chemical etching agent** to dissolve oxide.
- Simple, commonly used, with a tunable etching rate (R = T/t, where T is thickness etched and t is time).
- **Isotropic** -- etches in all directions equally, causing **undercuts** beneath the photoresist.
- The undercut rate is typically **~80% of the vertical etch depth**.
- Produces an **edge shift** (oxide openings are always larger than photoresist openings).
- No longer suitable for patterning features at advanced nodes due to undercutting; now only used for removing entire layers.

**Dry Etching (Reactive Ion Etching -- RIE)**
- Etching agent is ionized and applied as a **gas plasma**.
- Ions oscillate normal to the wafer surface via an alternating electric field, etching **vertically only** (anisotropic).
- **No edge shift** -- the main advantage over wet etching (Fig. 2.11).
- Combines physical bombardment (directional) with chemical etching.
- Can create very fine structures and **deep trenches** (high aspect ratio).

**Oxide Steps**

When oxide is etched but not fully removed, a step-like elevation remains on the surface (Fig. 2.12). These steps accumulate through repeated thermal oxidation and structuring cycles:

1. After structured etching of field oxide, the silicon surface sits at two levels (level 1 under remaining oxide, level 2 where oxide was removed).
2. A subsequent thermal oxidation grows oxide faster in the opening (starting at the lower level 2), partially reducing the step -- but the surrounding oxide's "head start" means a step persists at the surface.
3. A step also forms at the silicon interface below, growing over time.
4. Even after a full bright etch (complete oxide removal), the silicon surface retains these steps.

**Problem**: Repeated oxidation-and-etching cycles create an increasingly uneven wafer surface. This makes accurate **focusing during photolithography difficult**, degrading imaging quality and preventing progress to smaller feature sizes. This is why simple thermal-oxidation-plus-etching cannot be used in cutting-edge nodes.

#### 2.5.4 Local Oxidation (LOCOS)

**LOCOS** (Local Oxidation of Silicon) was developed to solve the oxide step problem. Instead of growing oxide everywhere and then etching it away where not needed, LOCOS **grows oxide only where it is required** -- masking the deposition rather than the removal.

**Process flow** (Fig. 2.13):
1. **(a)** Grow a thin **pad oxide** thermally (needed because silicon nitride adheres poorly to bare silicon). Deposit a **silicon nitride (Si3N4)** layer on top.
2. **(b)** Pattern and etch the nitride layer using photolithography, leaving nitride only over regions where oxide openings are desired (i.e., active areas).
3. **(c)** Perform thermal oxidation. Oxide grows only in the exposed (nitride-free) regions. However, since thermal oxidation is isotropic, oxide also **spreads laterally under the nitride edges**, lifting them. This creates a tapered oxide profile at the boundary.
4. **(d)** Chemically dissolve the nitride. The remaining field oxide has a characteristic peaked boundary called the **"bird's beak"**.

**Advantages of LOCOS over thermal-oxidation-plus-etching**:
- **No silicon consumed** inside oxide openings (the active device areas).
- Oxide step height is only **~50%** of the step produced by thermal oxidation plus etching.
- The step is **inclined rather than steep**, so subsequent layers (polysilicon, metal) cover the edge more reliably.
- The step can be further reduced or eliminated with LOCOS extensions.

**LOCOS Extensions** (Fig. 2.14):
The idea is to **pre-lower the silicon surface** before oxidation so that after oxide growth, the surface returns to its original height. Since oxide grows 56% outward, the silicon must be lowered by exactly **56% of the desired oxide thickness**. Two methods to achieve this:
1. Mask with nitride and **etch the silicon** to the required depth.
2. Use **two sequential local oxidations**: the first creates a step at the correct height, then bright-etch to remove all oxide, then perform the second oxidation which fills to the original surface level.

#### 2.5.5 Reference to Physical Design

**Edge shifts from oxide structuring**:
- **Wet-etched field oxide**: undercuts make oxide openings *larger* than the mask.
- **LOCOS field oxide**: the bird's beak makes oxide openings *smaller* than the mask.
- Both are deterministic and correctable via pre-sizing during mask generation.
- Because practices differ between processes, the layout may or may not correspond to the real chip topography -- always consult the **Process Design Kit (PDK)**.

**Through contacts (contacts and vias)**:
- In modern processes, exclusively **dry etched (RIE)** -- no edge shifts.
- In legacy processes with wet-etched contacts, edge shifts were compensated by pre-sizing in layout post-processing.
- Through contacts therefore **always show real dimensions** in the layout across all semiconductor processes.

**Oxide steps with through contacts**:
- Etching contacts/vias always produces oxide steps, regardless of etching method.
- This causes significant problems in metallization layer fabrication, addressed by techniques discussed in Sect. 2.8 (metal coating).

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

This sub-chapter bridges the gap between raw fabrication steps (covered in [[2.1-2.3 IC Fabrication Fundamentals]]) and the design rules that constrain layout engineers. Imaging errors -- whether deterministic (edge shifts, diffraction) or stochastic (overlay) -- are the physical root cause of minimum width, minimum spacing, enclosure, and extension design rules that govern every polygon placed in a layout. Meanwhile, oxide layer formation and structuring techniques (thermal oxidation, etching, LOCOS) establish the insulating structures that separate active devices and interconnect layers. The oxide step problem motivates the transition from simple etch-based structuring to LOCOS and its extensions, which in turn sets the stage for understanding shallow trench isolation (STI) in more advanced nodes. These concepts feed directly into doping and layer deposition topics in [[2.6-2.7 Doping and Silicon Layers]], where the oxide layers serve as masking and insulating structures for subsequent process steps.
