# 1.3-1.4 Physical Design and Book Structure

> **Chapter context**: Introduction

## Key Concepts

### 1.3.1 Main Design Steps

The design flow for electronic systems follows a three-stage pipeline: **Specification --> Circuit Design --> Physical Design --> Fabrication**. Each stage transforms the design from a more abstract representation into a more concrete, physically realizable one.

#### Specification

The specification defines *what* the system must do, focusing on inputs, outputs, and performance requirements. It uses time-domain and frequency-domain signal representations, supplemented by text, figures, and tables. Critically, the specification describes the task to be performed but does **not** define *how* it is to be done -- that is determined in subsequent design steps.

Example specification excerpt: "The system shall receive two digital inputs, using pins 0-7 and 8-15, at a frequency of 1.2 GHz, and produce their multiplicative product as a single 16-bit digital output using pins 16-31, in no more than 5 clock cycles."

#### Circuit Design

Circuit design transforms the functional specification into an electrical circuit that correctly implements the required functionality. The output is a **structural description** of the electronic system, which takes one of two equivalent forms:

- **Netlist**: A text-based listing of all functional units and the electrical connections (nets) between them. Typically organized hierarchically in a tree structure containing standard electronic devices as well as circuit/function blocks.
- **Circuit diagram** (schematic): A graphical representation where functional units are depicted as symbols and nets as lines.

For **digital circuits**, circuit design involves top-down decomposition -- breaking higher-level functionality iteratively into simpler lower-level functions (AND gates, adders, comparators, registers). Synthesis procedures typically generate the netlist automatically; a circuit diagram is often not needed.

For **analog circuits**, circuit diagrams are produced manually in a schematic editor. Symbols from libraries (resistors, transistors, etc.) are placed and connected. The circuit structure is stored internally as a netlist.

#### Physical Design (Layout Design)

The purpose of physical design is to produce a **fabrication specification** from the structural description. This transformation must meet:

- **Process-specific boundary conditions**: Options and constraints of the fabrication technology (included as "technology data" in the design flow). These apply to *all* designs in a given technology.
- **Project-specific boundary conditions** (constraints): Instructions specific to the product being developed, produced during circuit design. These concern proper functioning and required reliability.

The result of physical design defines the exterior appearance and dimensions of the electronic system. It is called a **layout** because it is a physical interpretation/arrangement of the abstract structural description.

### 1.3.2 Physical Design of Integrated Circuits

In semiconductor technology, all structures are built on a wafer using photolithography with **masks**. The purpose of IC physical design is to generate the geometrical structures used to create these masks.

The IC layout is a complete image of the chip, composed of stacked/layered mask images. It defines:

1. **Internal structure of devices**
2. **Device placement/arrangement** on the chip
3. **Interconnects** (wiring between devices)
4. **Contacts and vias** (vertical connections between layers)
5. **Bond pads** (at chip margins, for external electrical connections)

Each graphics element is assigned to a **layer**, which generally corresponds to a mask. Layer-specific colors, line thicknesses, and fill patterns distinguish elements in the graphics editor. For example, a "Contact" layer shows mask features for holes in the insulating layer above the silicon surface; these holes are filled with metal to create electrical connections between silicon and the bottom metal layer.

#### Digital IC Physical Design

Digital physical design is **highly automated** (layout synthesis):

- **Standard cells** (logic gates, memories) with immutable internal structure are stored in libraries. Larger **macro cells** provide higher-level functions (adders, multipliers, protocol implementations).
- **Placement**: Cell instances are placed on unoccupied surface areas.
- **Routing**: Interconnects making electrical connections between cells are designed.
- Both steps use a netlist as input and are almost fully automated.

#### Analog IC Physical Design

Analog physical design is **primarily manual**:

- Circuit functionality depends heavily on individual device designs and their physical placement relative to one another.
- The layout engineer must understand the circuit to make correct physical design decisions.
- **Device implementation**: Each device is sized per electrical parameters in the circuit diagram and adapted to structural criteria. **Generators** (parameterized scripts) can automatically produce layout variants.
- **Placement**: Devices are arranged almost entirely manually using a graphics editor due to complexity.
- **Routing**: Also largely manual, as designer expertise is needed for critical layout sections.
- **Matching** -- ensuring symmetric devices behave identically -- is a key analog-specific technique (covered in detail in [[6 Analog Layout Techniques]]).

About 90% of development costs for mixed-signal and smart-power ICs come from the analog parts, even though analog devices typically constitute less than 10% of the chip by device count. Analog design productivity is **two to three orders of magnitude lower** than digital design productivity.

#### Verification

After physical design is complete, two obligatory verification steps must be performed before fabrication:

1. **Design Rule Check (DRC)**: Verifies compliance with technological constraints (design rules), confirming manufacturability.
2. **Layout Versus Schematic (LVS)**: Electrical verification that the structural description has been correctly realized in the layout. Checks:
   - Devices are properly connected electrically
   - Correct device types are present
   - Devices are correctly parameterized

### 1.3.3 Physical Design of Printed Circuit Boards

PCB physical design focuses on designing a wiring substrate for mounting and electrically connecting devices. Unlike IC design, the devices are **not** fabricated as part of the PCB process -- they are independently sourced components.

PCBs consist of multiple conductive and non-conductive layers connected by vias. Additional layers are required for fabrication purposes: solder resist masks, solder paste masks, and placement imprints.

#### PCB Layout Steps

1. **Specifying device placement**: Devices are extracted from the circuit diagram and matching **footprints** (also called land patterns) are loaded from footprint libraries. A footprint describes pads (contact surfaces), placement imprint as polygons, necessary holes (for through-hole devices), and through contacts (vias). Footprints are placed to define device locations. Unrouted connections appear as symbolic "rubber bands."

2. **Defining board size and routing layer count.**

3. **Routing**: The shape, location, and layer assignment of interconnect tracks are defined, along with via positions.

#### PCB Verification and Fabrication Data

After placement and routing, the layout is verified by:
- **Electrical Rule Check (ERC)**: Checks for short circuits.
- **Design Rule Check (DRC)**: Checks compliance with constraints for proper functioning and manufacture.

The fabrication output consists of multiple file types (unlike IC layouts stored in a single graphics file):
- **Gerber files**: Describe conductor tracks for individual layers, solder resist/paste masks, and placement imprint polygons.
- **Drill file**: Contains diameters and coordinates of all drill holes.
- **Pick-and-place file**: Contains location and alignment of devices for automated assembly.

### 1.4 Motivation and Structure of the Book

Physical layout directly affects five critical aspects of circuit quality:
1. **Circuit performance**
2. **Area** (die size or board size)
3. **Reliability**
4. **Power consumption**
5. **Manufacturing yield**

Continuing miniaturization worsens parasitic disturbances and increases technological constraints, driving demand for experienced layout designers and new methodologies/tools.

#### Book Structure Overview

| Chapter | Topic | Key Content |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| **Ch. 1** | Introduction | Technologies, tasks, methodologies for layout design |
| **Ch. 2** | Silicon to ICs | Engineering know-how for transforming silicon into devices; semiconductor technology boundary conditions |
| **Ch. 3** | Data Interfaces | Netlists, layers, polygons; mask data, design rules, libraries; layout post-processing (chip finishing, reticle layout, graphic manipulation) |
| **Ch. 4** | Physical Design Flow | Flow, constraints, strategies; design models and styles; analog-digital design gap |
| **Ch. 5** | Physical Design Steps | Partitioning, floorplanning, placement, routing; HDL-based vs. schematic-based netlists; physical verification; resolution enhancement techniques (RET) |
| **Ch. 6** | Analog Layout Techniques | Common analog devices, cell generators, symmetry and matching principles |
| **Ch. 7** | Reliability | Parasitic effects (bulk silicon, surface, interconnect); overvoltage events; electromigration, thermal and stress migration |

The book covers both digital and analog, IC and PCB layout, with a deliberate emphasis on **analog layout design** because analog design requires more manual work and the layout designer's expert knowledge is more critical than in fully-automated digital flows.

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

This sub-chapter establishes the fundamental framework for the entire textbook: the two-step transformation from specification to fabrication (circuit design followed by physical design). Understanding the distinction between structural descriptions and fabrication specifications, and the difference between digital (automated) and analog (manual) physical design workflows, is essential context for everything that follows. The verification steps introduced here (DRC, LVS, ERC) will be examined in depth in [[5 Physical Design Steps and Verification]], while the PCB and IC layout processes outlined here are elaborated with concrete algorithms and strategies in [[4 Physical Design Flow]] and [[6 Analog Layout Techniques]]. The book structure overview in Section 1.4 serves as a roadmap, showing how each subsequent chapter builds on foundational concepts to address increasingly specific challenges in layout design.
