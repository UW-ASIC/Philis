# 5.1-5.2 Netlist Generation

> **Chapter context**: Steps in Physical Design

## Key Concepts

Netlist generation is the critical bridge between design capture (what the circuit should do) and physical design (how it is manufactured). There are two primary methods for generating a netlist: using **hardware description languages (HDLs)** for digital and mixed-signal designs, and using **symbolic (schematic) design entry** for analog circuits and PCBs.

### 5.1 Generating a Netlist Using Hardware Description Languages

#### Historical Context and Evolution

The shift toward HDLs began in the late 1960s, driven by the need for standard text-based descriptions of electronic systems that were not tied to a specific technology. HDLs provided a formal, precise description of electronic circuits enabling automated analysis and simulation. They also introduced the concept of **Register Transfer Level (RTL)** -- a design abstraction that models a synchronous digital circuit in terms of the flow of digital signals between hardware registers and the logical operations performed on those signals.

Key milestones in HDL evolution:

- **1985**: **Verilog** introduced by Gateway Design Automation. Cadence Design Systems later acquired Verilog-XL, which became the de facto standard simulator for a decade.
- **1980**: The US Department of Defense launched the **VHSIC** (Very High Speed Integrated Circuit) program, advancing IC materials, lithography, packaging, testing, and algorithms.
- **1987**: **VHDL** (VHSIC Hardware Description Language) developed as part of that program. VHDL borrows heavily from the Ada programming language in both concepts and syntax.
- **1990s**: Analog extensions emerged -- **VHDL-AMS** and **Verilog-AMS** added continuous-time and event-driven modeling semantics for mixed-signal circuit verification.

Initially, Verilog and VHDL were used only to *document and simulate* circuits that had already been captured as schematics. The real paradigm shift came with **logic synthesis** -- tools that compile HDL source files (written at the RTL level) into a manufacturable netlist description in terms of gates and transistors. This pushed HDLs from documentation aids to the primary method of digital design entry, increasing design capacity from hundreds of transistors to thousands and beyond.

#### HDL Elements and Syntax (VHDL Example)

A VHDL description consists of two fundamental parts:

1. **Entity declaration** -- defines the *interface* (ports, i.e., inputs and outputs)
2. **Architecture body** -- defines the *implementation* (behavior, structure, or dataflow)

For example, a half adder (which adds two binary digits producing a sum via XOR and a carry via AND) is described as:

```vhdl
-- Interface
entity HALFADDER is
  port(A, B     : in bit;
       SUM, CARRY: out bit);
end entity HALFADDER;

-- Implementation
architecture RTL of HALFADDER is
begin
  SUM   <= A xor B;
  CARRY <= A and B;
end architecture RTL;
```

**Key distinctions between HDLs and conventional programming languages:**

- **Concurrency**: HDL statements are *concurrent* -- they describe pieces of hardware executing in parallel, not sequential steps. Concurrent statements can appear in any order.
- **Time**: HDLs explicitly include the notion of time, which is essential for modeling signal propagation delays and clock behavior.
- **Coding workflow**: Designers often start from high-level algorithmic descriptions (e.g., C++ models), then use scripting languages like Python to generate repetitive HDL structures automatically. Specialized text editors provide syntax coloration, auto-indentation, and macro-based expansion.

#### HDL Design Flow

The design flow using HDLs proceeds through three synthesis levels:

1. **Behavior Level Synthesis** -- Functional design at the system level (often in C++ or similar). Simulation at this stage enables architectural exploration: designers write multiple variations of a base design and compare their behavior. This is the cheapest stage to explore design tradeoffs.

2. **Logic Level Synthesis** -- The RTL code undergoes a *code review/audit* where automated checkers resolve errors before synthesis. During **logic synthesis**, HDL descriptions are automatically converted into a netlist -- a list of signal nets and specific circuit elements (e.g., standard cells). Simulation and formal verification are performed at this stage.

3. **Physical Synthesis** -- Modern synthesis tools blur the line between logic synthesis and physical design. They can include place-and-route characteristics by providing a "pre-placed netlist," using techniques like buffer sizing and placement restrictions to meet timing requirements. This is necessary because routing has a major influence on internal timing delays of high-speed ICs.

At each level, simulation validates the design, and the flow proceeds from abstract behavioral models down to concrete layout data. HDLs do not play a significant role in physical design itself because the steps become progressively more laden with technology-specific information that cannot be stored in a generic HDL description.

### 5.2 Generating a Netlist Using Symbolic Design Entry

#### Overview

A netlist can also be generated from a **circuit schematic** (also called *symbolic design entry* or *schematic entry*). This approach is common for **analog circuits** and **PCB designs**. The designer uses a **schematic editor** to position device symbols and connect their pins with lines representing electrical connections.

Important: the topological arrangement in a schematic bears **no relation** to the geometrical properties of the final circuit layout. The schematic's primary purpose is to graphically document the devices and their connections. The schematic is then converted to a netlist containing the same information -- a list of electronic components and the nets they connect to.

**Hierarchical design** is used to manage complexity in larger analog and PCB circuits:
- Higher design levels contain **block symbols** representing design modules at lower levels
- Block symbols may be instantiated multiple times at higher hierarchy levels
- Each hierarchy level is maintained as a separate file
- Symbol data is stored in a **symbol library**; schematics store only pointers to library elements
- Individual device instances are differentiated by **instance names**, typically assigned by automatic numbering

#### Elements of Circuit Diagrams

Circuit diagrams contain the following elements:

- **Symbols** -- represent functional units, from basic components (transistors, resistors) to complex sub-designs (block symbols). Both types are handled identically during schematic entry.
- **Device labels** -- identification letter + consecutive number (e.g., C4 = capacitor no. 4, D1 = digital gate no. 1, R12 = resistor no. 12). These follow the SPICE netlist naming conventions.
- **Device type or rating** -- for generic components (resistors, capacitors, coils), the value is quoted with the unit omitted (e.g., "2.2u" instead of "2.2 uF"). For specific components, the type is specified as it appears in the library (e.g., "NAND gate 74ACT00").
- **Electrical connections**:
  - **Wires**: pin-to-pin interconnects; signal name is optional
  - **Bus systems**: bundle many signal paths; signal name is obligatory with each signal having the same name and a different index
  - **Lines**: decorative only (e.g., borders), no electrical significance
- **Back-annotation data** (optional) -- details gathered during subsequent design steps (e.g., current values from layout simulation, capacitance values on outputs, DC analysis results) written back into the schematic for future reference
- **Frame and title block**

**Logic gate symbols** come in two standard sets:
- **IEEE/IEC format** (ANSI/IEEE Std 91-1984, IEC 60617) -- rectangular symbols with function labels (e.g., "&" for AND, ">=1" for OR)
- **Distinctive/traditional shape** -- derived from 1950s/1960s military standards, using curved shapes

Digital gates within the same IC package share the same identification letter and number, distinguished by appended letters (e.g., D1A, D1B, D1C, D1D for four NAND gates in a 74ACT00 package). Pins on logic ICs are numbered counter-clockwise starting from the mark.

**Power supply pins** (VDD/PWR and VSS/GND) are typically *hidden pins* -- not visible in the schematic but connected automatically during netlist generation using a **global node mechanism**. Exceptions are analog designs and PCB schematics, where voltage supply and ground symbols are explicitly shown.

#### Netlist Generation from Schematics

Before generating a netlist, the schematic undergoes error checking:
- **Electrical Rule Check (ERC)** -- checks for unconnected inputs, duplicate instance names, etc.
- **Circuit simulation** (e.g., SPICE) can also verify correctness

Netlists can be generated as:
- **Flat** -- all hierarchy levels expanded into a single level
- **Hierarchical** -- preserves hierarchy using path structures in device and net names

**Two netlist orientations exist:**

| Orientation | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Net-oriented** | Each net lists its connected device pins | (N1: a, x.IN1, y.IN1) |
| **Pin-oriented** | Each device lists its associated nets | (x: IN1 N1, IN2 N2, OUT N3) |

The information in a netlist varies by purpose:
- **For simulation**: contains node/device information *plus* control information (e.g., SPICE model parameters)
- **For physical design**: contains circuit information only -- a collection of all signal nets and the device pins they connect to

## Connections to the Bigger Picture

Netlist generation is the essential first output of the design capture phase and serves as the primary input to all subsequent physical design steps covered in [[5.3 Primary Steps in Physical Design]]. Whether produced from HDL synthesis or schematic entry, the netlist encodes the complete connectivity information that placement, routing, and verification tools require. The choice between HDL-based and schematic-based netlist generation reflects the analog vs. digital design flow distinction introduced in [[4.6-4.7 Analog and Digital Design Flows]] -- digital designs overwhelmingly use HDL synthesis while analog and PCB designs rely on schematic entry. Understanding both methods is critical because modern mixed-signal designs often require both flows to converge into a unified netlist before physical implementation can begin.
