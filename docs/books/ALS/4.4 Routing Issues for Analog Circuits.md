# 4.4 Routing Issues and Techniques for Analog Circuits

> **Chapter 4**: Routing Analog Circuits (Dundar & Unutulmaz)
> **Part**: Part II: Routing

Performances of analog circuits are extremely sensitive to layout parasitics, which are undesired effects due to physical properties of elements. Some of the parasitic effects introduced due to routing and techniques for reducing these effects are discussed in the literature. Although it is impossible to eliminate the existence of routing parasitics, it is possible to reduce their effects with proper techniques. **Splitting paths** that are carrying high currents from those carrying low currents or **routing nets symmetrically** are such techniques. This section and the next cover routing issues (net splitting, symmetric routing, crosstalk/shielding) and routing strategies (digital-inspired, cost minimization, parasitic bounds).

---

## 4.4.1 Net Splitting

### The Problem: Current Density Variation

Current densities in different portions of analog circuits may vary excessively. There may be an **order of magnitude difference** between the current densities of two paths in the same net. The resistance of a wire can be modeled as:

$$R_{wire} = \frac{\Delta l \cdot R_{sh}}{\Delta w}$$

where:
- $\Delta l$ is the length of the wire
- $\Delta w$ is the width of the wire
- $R_{sh}$ is the **sheet resistance**

If this model is used for the grounded wires in Fig. 4.12a, there will be a voltage drop of $V_{drop} = R_{wire} \cdot I$ on the wire between the dashed lines. Because of the voltage on the wire, the potential at the top end of the wire will be different from the ground potential. This potential difference may vary with time and affect the operation of the analog circuit. Therefore, it must be minimized.

### Solutions

Two main approaches to reduce potential drops from net splitting:

1. **Separate the paths** (Fig. 4.12b) -- physically split the net so that high-current paths are separated from low-current paths, preventing voltage drops from propagating across the net
2. **Increase wire widths** (Fig. 4.12c) -- use wider wires for the high-current paths to reduce $R_{wire}$, thereby reducing the voltage drop

## 4.4.2 Symmetric Routing

### Motivation

Analog circuit designers frequently introduce **topological symmetries** in differential circuits to optimize offset, differential gain, and noise. As analog layout tools have progressed, routers with the capability of symmetric routing were developed.

### Historical Development

- **Early tools**: ANAGRAM II and ROAD had the capability of routing symmetrically in the presence of **nonsymmetric obstacles**
- **Initial approaches**: Only considered symmetric routing when the terminals of the paths being routed are symmetric with respect to a **symmetry axis** (Fig. 4.13a), where terminals are in gray and the symmetry axis is the dashed line. This splits the layout into two halves

### Routing Strategies

1. **Without nonsymmetric obstacles**: The tools route one half of the layout and then mirror the new paths to the other half
2. **With nonsymmetric obstacles**: A blockage that exists only on one side of the symmetry line effectively becomes a blockage for the other side as well. Another approach (ROAD) is to mirror the obstacles, then do routing for one side only and mirror the constructed path

### Key Challenges

- An acceptable symmetric routing will **not be possible** with the above approaches if the terminals are on different sides of the symmetry axis and the paths cross (Fig. 4.14a)
- **Parasitic matching** is critical: although resistances and capacitances of two matched nets are matched, there may still be differences between the parasitics of the symmetric nets due to **capacitive and inductive coupling** with other nets
- The structure in a connector (Fig. 4.14b) provides much better matching than simple crossing of symmetric wires

## 4.4.3 Crosstalk and Shielding

### Crosstalk Extraction Models

Crosstalk between nets may severely degrade the performance of an analog circuit. It is required to extract these effects during routing. There are **1-D, 2-D, 2.5-D, and 3-D** extraction methods for parasitic capacitances.

#### 1-D Extraction

Uses the equation:

$$C_{1D} = A \cdot C_a + S \cdot C_p$$

where:
- $A$ is the area of the overlapping region between two wires
- $S$ is the perimeter of this region
- $C_a$ is the **capacitance per unit area**
- $C_p$ is the **fringing capacitance per unit length**

The overlapping area is the dashed rectangle in Fig. 4.15. This model is commonly used in routing due to its simplicity.

#### 2-D Extraction

Also includes capacitances due to **nonoverlapping wires**. For the first vertical wire in Fig. 4.15, total capacitance is:

$$C_{2D} = C_{1D} + \frac{\Delta l \cdot C_p}{d}$$

where $C_p$ is the crosstalk capacitance per unit length, $\Delta l$ is the length of overlap in the vertical lines, and $d$ is the separation between the wires.

#### 2.5-D and 3-D Extraction

- **2.5-D**: Fringing effects are considered in advance through the cross-section of the real 3-D structure
- **3-D** (parameterized): A library including parameterized 3-D geometric structures is constructed, and extracted geometries from the layout are matched with the ones in the library. More accurate but may be cumbersome for path searching in routing due to time complexity
- **EM simulation**: For RF circuits, inductive coupling may also be critical. Even EM simulations should be carried out to observe parasitic inductive effects more accurately

### Shielding Methods

Three different shielding methods to reduce crosstalk (Fig. 4.16):

1. **Same-layer shielding** (Fig. 4.16a) -- place a grounded shield wire on the same layer between the coupled wires. Adds space between the wires and provides a grounded conductor to absorb coupling
2. **Different-layer shielding** (Fig. 4.16b) -- use shielding on different metal layers above/below the sensitive wire to block vertical coupling
3. **Bulk shielding** (Fig. 4.16c) -- shield through the bulk (substrate) to avoid crosstalk. Squares in the figure represent contacts to bulk

> **Important**: The shielding wire must be connected to a **dc potential** or it must be **grounded**. Otherwise, a floating shield can worsen the effects.

The router ROAD has the capability to add a shield line between wires (Fig. 4.16). The tools MIGHTY and ILAC also use net ordering to route sensitive and noisy nets separately to reduce crosstalk.

## 4.5 Routing Strategies for Analog Circuits

### 4.5.1 Digitally Inspired Early Routing Strategies

The earliest analog routing tools (late 1980s) augmented digital routing methods with additional analog constraints. Main characteristics:

- **Two-step routing**: global routing followed by detailed routing (like digital)
- **Heuristic cost functions** used in a mostly **feedforward** manner
- **Channel routing** preferred over maze routing -- breaking the problem into smaller problems of routing individual channels results in faster routing compared to general area-routing algorithms

#### ROSA Router (LADIES System)

- Routing areas are extracted and decomposed into **rectangles**
- An **adjacency graph** is formed from these rectangles
- **Global routing**: find the shortest path on the adjacency graph
- **Detailed routing**: performed using the **river routing** algorithm
- Analog constraints are not seriously taken into account

#### MIGHTY

- Uses a **net ordering** approach: nets are classified into categories (input nets, output nets, etc.)
- The order that nets are routed is determined a priori
- More critical nets are routed first, occupying more privileged locations
- Uses a **rip-up and reroute** strategy for routing
- Feasible due to the relatively few nets in analog design

#### ILAC

- Companion layout tool of OPASYN
- Nets classified into four categories: **sensitive nets**, **noisy nets**, **noncritical signal nets**, and **power supply nets**
- Net ordering strategy: power nets first, then sensitive nets, then noncritical nets
- Noisy nets routed last; power and noncritical nets provide **shielding** between critical and noisy nets
- **Global router**: maze router handling net couplings, undesired crossings, planarity (for power nets), and congestion
- **Detailed router**: scan-line-based incremental channel router

#### SALIM

- Two-step router where **symbolic routing** is carried out to obtain **zero-width tracks**
- Electrical parameters (resistivity, capacitance to ground, electrical symmetry constraints) are extracted and met as much as possible
- Once constraints satisfied, **detailed routing** fulfills design rules
- Uses expert information to place critical blocks and order connections in the same order as placement
- Electrical symmetry is achieved by use of expert knowledge

#### SLAM

- Routing approach similar to MIGHTY
- Performs a **critical net analysis** to determine various node types (noisy vs. sensitive)
- A **distance constraint** is assigned for each circuit node with different priorities
- The circuit node with the highest-priority distance constraint should require the **shortest wire connections**
- Large spacing is reserved for noisy nodes to minimize wire crossover or adjacent wire crosstalk

#### Hierarchical Router (Global Router by [44])

- Creates a **slicing tree** deep enough that each module is left alone in its appropriate box
- A **"hierarchical channel graph"** determines the routing areas (spaces between modules)
- An approximate **rectilinear-Steiner-tree (RST)** algorithm is applied iteratively up the hierarchy
- Nets classified as **sensitive** or **noisy**; special care taken for net-crossing-free routing for noisy nets
- Uses **traffic light routing algorithm** and **terminal grouping**

### Limitations of Early Approaches

- Heuristic estimation of costs was obvious and limited
- No explicit link between the performance constraints of the layout and the cost that the router is trying to minimize
- Individual minimization of costs does not guarantee satisfactory overall layout solution
- Evaluation of routing parasitics did not go beyond qualitative analysis

### 4.5.2 Routing Based on Cost Minimization

The limitations of digitally inspired approaches led (late 1980s and early 1990s) to the development of more "analog" routing approaches. The common themes: **area routing** and **minimization of some cost function**.

#### ANAGRAM

- Uses a **line-expansion** style router
- A uniform grid-based routing graph is defined over the entire routing space
- A cost function is defined on each edge of this graph:

$$Cost = Cost(P) + MaterialCost(C) + ParasiticCost(C) + RoutabilityCost(C) + CostToTarget(C, T)$$

where:
- $Cost(P)$ -- cost to reach this cell from the source
- $MaterialCost(C)$ -- incremental length added by cell $C$ and cost of routing on $C$'s layer or via
- $ParasiticCost(C)$ -- incremental parasitic to each interacting nearby net
- $RoutabilityCost(C)$ -- how difficult it may be to embed this cell in this region of the layout (wire crowding)
- $CostToTarget(C, T)$ -- **lower bound** on the cost of the remainder of the path (to be estimated)

**Search complexity**: grows as a **quadratic function** of the circuit size. Each net is composed of a set of partial paths with associated costs, stored in a **cost-ordered structure**. The line-expansion router pops the most promising partial path from a **heap**, expanding lines from the front to the next interesting feature in the layout.

**Key features**:
- $CostToTarget(C, T)$ ensures the search is biased toward the target -- effective when routing from **distributed terminals** (e.g., perimeter of a capacitor plate)
- Uses a **rip-up-and-reroute** phase to eliminate crosstalk violations resulting from net ordering
- Wires are classified into three categories: **neutral**, **noisy**, and **sensitive**
- Simple equations model interactions between noisy and sensitive wires for crosstalk cost calculation
- Main problem: cost function for routing parasitics is used with **no explicit reference to performance specifications** -- no provision for constraint-driven synthesis

#### ANAGRAM II

- Handles **over-the-device wiring** and **arbitrary-width wiring**
- Path-segments explored during line expansion are **actual rectangles of arbitrary shape** (not line segments on abstract grid)
- The $RoutabilityCost(C)$ term measures the cost associated with ripping up nets in the neighborhood to advance a path
- Makes an explicit tradeoff between **detouring around an obstacle** and **simply removing it** (if cost sufficiently low) to reschedule for later rerouting

#### Modified Lee's Algorithm Approach ([30])

- A modified version of **Lee's algorithm** is proposed for routing
- Cost function for each net based on: **weighted distance** between nodes, a **layer resistivity** parameter, a **layer-to-bulk capacity** parameter, **proximity** parameter dependent on distance from already existing wires, a **congestion** parameter, and an estimate of **final crowding** based on a fast first-attempt path-search
- Weights for each component of the cost function set by the user on a **net basis**
- **Backtracing** on wires also performed to clean up extra corners
- Accommodates **preconnected pins** (e.g., stacked transistor structures)
- Considers **symmetry** as an issue
- Proposes a **scheduler** that determines the routing order of nets using symmetry information, user information, and congestion information
- The scheduler does **not solve the ordering problem completely** and provisions for rip-up and reroute are present

### 4.5.3 Routing Based on Parasitic Bounds

The routers above (ANAGRAM, ANAGRAM II) minimize crosstalk but without any specific, quantitative performance targets. On the other hand, routers like **ROAD** and **ANAGRAM III** use improved cost-based schemes that route instead to **minimize the deviation from acceptable parasitic bounds** supplied by the designers or derived from **sensitivity analysis** (see Fig. 4.18 for the routing flow).

#### 4.5.3.1 Constraint Generation and Sensitivity Calculation

The link between the **performance of a circuit** and the **parasitic bounds on a node** can be determined either:
1. By the **designer** (manual specification)
2. By **sensitivity analysis**, which acts as a constraint generator

**Performance constraints during routing**: the maximum changes allowed in performance functions because of routing parasitics. All possible combinations of interconnect parasitics that meet performance constraints define a **feasible region** in the space described by the parasitics, which can be treated as design parameters for routing. Thus, analog routing can be treated as a **constraint-driven optimization problem**.

- **Objective function**: minimize the chip area
- **Constraints**: performance constraints of the circuit

**Parasitic constraint types** imposed on the router:
1. **Bounding constraints** -- upper/lower bounds on parasitic values
2. **Matching constraints** -- require matched parasitics between symmetric paths

Based on the performance sensitivities, performance constraints, and a-priori estimates of maximum values of parasitics, the set of parasitics that can collectively cause negligible performance degradation are ignored; the remainder are called **critical parasitics**. Only bounding constraints are generated for these critical parasitics.

**Sensitivity** $S_{ij}$ of a performance function $W_i$ with respect to a parasitic $p_j$ at the nominal value of $W_i$ is defined as:

$$S_{ij} = \left[\frac{\partial W_i}{\partial p_j}\right]_{p_j = 0}$$

**Computing sensitivity**: typically done using the **perturbation method** -- a parameter is perturbed and the performance is re-evaluated using circuit simulation. This is very time-consuming since a re-simulation of the circuit is required for every parasitic of interest.

## Algorithms & Methods

### Net Ordering Algorithm (MIGHTY / ILAC)

1. Classify all nets into categories (sensitive, noisy, noncritical, power)
2. Assign routing priority based on category
3. Route highest-priority nets first (they get the most privileged locations)
4. Route subsequent nets around already-placed nets
5. Power and noncritical nets can serve as natural shields between sensitive and noisy nets

### ANAGRAM Line-Expansion Routing

1. Define a uniform grid-based routing graph over the routing space
2. Initialize cost function components for each edge
3. For each net (in priority order):
   a. Create initial partial paths from source terminals
   b. Store in a **min-heap** ordered by total cost
   c. Pop the most promising partial path from the heap
   d. Expand lines from its front to the next interesting layout feature
   e. Add new partial paths to the heap
   f. Repeat until target terminal is reached (minimum cost path found)
4. After all nets routed, perform **rip-up-and-reroute** phase to fix crosstalk violations

### Sensitivity-Based Routing Flow (Fig. 4.18)

1. **Input**: Netlist
2. **Extract**: Performance constraints and connectivity information
3. **Run sensitivity analysis** on the circuit to produce sensitivity values $S_{ij}$
4. **Generate parasitic constraints** using sensitivity values and performance constraints
5. **Feed into router**: connectivity information + parasitic constraints + sensitivity values
6. **Router produces**: Layout with routing that satisfies parasitic bounds

## Connections to Bigger Picture

This sub-chapter bridges the gap between purely digital routing techniques and the performance-aware routing required for analog circuits. Sections 4.4.1--4.4.3 identify the three fundamental analog-specific routing issues (net splitting, symmetry, crosstalk) that any analog router must handle beyond what digital routers address. Sections 4.5.1--4.5.3 then trace the evolution of routing strategies from simple digital augmentation (ROSA, MIGHTY) through cost-function-based area routers (ANAGRAM) to the most sophisticated constraint-driven approaches (ROAD, ANAGRAM III) that explicitly link circuit performance to routing decisions via sensitivity analysis. This progression mirrors the broader theme of the book: moving from topology-driven heuristics toward optimization-driven, performance-aware analog layout synthesis. The sensitivity-based parasitic bounds approach in Section 4.5.3 directly connects to the placement constraints discussed in [[3.0 Placement]] and foreshadows the integrated place-and-route methodologies covered later in the book.
