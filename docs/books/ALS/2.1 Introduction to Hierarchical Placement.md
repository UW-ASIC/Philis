# 2.1 Introduction to Hierarchical Placement

> **Chapter 2**: Hierarchical Placement with Layout Constraints (Lin & Chang)
> **Part**: Part I: Placement

## Key Concepts

### 2.1 Introduction

In analog layout design, devices must be placed with **matching**, **symmetry**, and **proximity constraints** to reduce parasitic coupling effects and improve circuit performance. According to the text, the basic analog layout constraints include:

- **Common-centroid constraint**: Usually applied to a subcircuit of a current mirror or a differential pair to reduce process-induced mismatches among the devices. The symmetry constraint is always required in the layout design of the whole differential subcircuit. It helps reduce parasitic mismatches between two identical signal flows in the differential subcircuit.
- **Symmetry constraint**: Devices are placed symmetrically with respect to a common axis. Symmetry pairs and self-symmetric modules must be mirrored across the symmetry axis.
- **Proximity constraint**: Widely used in the subcircuit of a common device model or a certain circuit functionality. It helps form a **connected placement** of a subcircuit so that the subcircuit can share a connected substrate/well region or be surrounded by a common guard ring to reduce the layout area, the interconnecting wire length, and the substrate coupling effect. The placement outline of each subcircuit with the proximity constraint can be irregularly rectilinear for better area utilization.

Beyond the basic layout constraints, there exist **hierarchical symmetry** and **hierarchical proximity constraints** due to the **layout design hierarchy**. The layout design hierarchy may contain both exact and virtual hierarchies of analog circuit design:
- The **exact hierarchy** is the same as the circuit hierarchy.
- The **virtual hierarchy** consists of hierarchical clusters. Each cluster contains some devices and subcircuits, which are gathered based on device models, subcircuit functionality, and/or other specific constraints.

Each subcircuit in the hierarchy corresponds to a specific constraint. A subcircuit with the hierarchical symmetry constraint may contain some devices together with other subcircuits with the common-centroid and (hierarchical) symmetry constraints. Similarly, a subcircuit with the hierarchical proximity constraint may contain some devices together with other subcircuits with the common-centroid, (hierarchical) symmetry, and (hierarchical) proximity constraints.

Most modern analog placement techniques optimize the placement in different hierarchical subcircuits simultaneously, instead of bottom-up integration, because the optimal placement of a subcircuit may not lead to the globally optimal placement. Most approaches apply **simulated annealing** based on topological floorplan representations such as **Sequence-Pair** and **B\*-tree**. The approach in this chapter adopts a fully deterministic approach based on the hierarchical **B\*-tree** (HB\*-tree) and **symmetry-island** formulations.

### 2.2 Preliminaries

#### 2.2.1 Symmetry Constraints

To reduce the effect of parasitic mismatches and circuit sensitivity to thermal gradients or process variations for analog circuits, some pairs of modules need to be placed symmetrically with respect to a common axis, and the symmetric modules are preferred to be placed at close proximity for better electrical properties.

The symmetry constraints can be formulated in terms of **symmetry types**, **symmetry groups**, **symmetry pairs**, and **self-symmetric modules**.

- **Symmetry type**: A symmetry type may correspond to a symmetry axis in either the horizontal or the vertical direction. Two different symmetry types exist with either the vertical or the horizontal symmetry axis.
  - For **vertical (horizontal) symmetry**: a symmetry pair with two modules of the same dimensions and orientations should be placed symmetrically along the vertical (horizontal) symmetry axis.
  - A **self-symmetric module** whose internal structure is self-symmetric must have its center placed at the symmetry axis.

**Table 2.1 -- Notations used throughout this chapter:**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $S$ | A symmetry group |
| $(b, b')$ | A symmetry pair |
| $b^s$ | A self-symmetric module |
| $b^r$ | The representative of a symmetry pair or a self-symmetric module |
| $n$ | Number of modules |
| $m$ | Number of symmetry groups |
| $(x_i, y_i)$ | The center coordinate of module $b_i$ |
| $w_i, h_i$ | The width and the height of module $b_i$ |
| $\hat{x}_k, \hat{y}_k$ | The coordinate of the symmetry group axis of the symmetry group $S_k$ |

**Symmetry group definition**: Let $S = \{S_1, S_2, \ldots, S_m\}$ be a set of $m$ symmetry groups whose coordinates of the symmetry axis (axes) are denoted by $\hat{x}_k$ and $\hat{y}_k$, $1 \leq k \leq m$. A symmetry group $S_i = \{(b_1, b_1'), (b_2, b_2'), \ldots, (b_p, b_p'), b_1^s, b_2^s, \ldots, b_q^s\}$ consists of $p$ symmetry pairs and $q$ self-symmetric modules.

**Symmetric placement constraints** -- for a symmetry group $S_k$ with the vertical (horizontal) symmetry axis, the following must be satisfied:

For vertical symmetry:
$$x_j + x_j' = 2 \times \hat{x}_k, \quad \forall j = 1, 2, \ldots, p$$
$$y_j = y_j', \quad \forall j = 1, 2, \ldots, p$$
$$x_l^s = \hat{x}_k, \quad \forall k = 1, 2, \ldots, q$$

For horizontal symmetry (Eq. 2.2):
$$x_j = x_j', \quad \forall j = 1, 2, \ldots, p$$
$$y_j + y_j' = 2 \times \hat{y}_k, \quad \forall j = 1, 2, \ldots, p$$
$$y_l^s = \hat{y}_k, \quad \forall k = 1, 2, \ldots, q$$

#### 2.2.2 Symmetry Island

The **symmetry island** concept addresses the effect of the symmetric device layout on the electrical matching properties of the symmetric devices.

Pelgrom et al. measured the mismatch between MOS transistors with various electrical parameters as a function of device areas, distances, and orientations. The difference of an electrical parameter $P$ between two rectangular devices is modeled by the standard deviation:

$$\sigma_{\Delta}^2(P) = \frac{A_P^2}{WL} + S_P^2 D_x^2$$

where $A_P$ is the area proportionality constant for $P$, $W$ and $L$ denote the respective width and length of the device, and $S_P$ denotes the variation of $P$ under the device spacing $D_x$.

The device dimensions of modules in a symmetry pair are assumed to be the same. According to the above equation, **the larger the distance between the symmetry pair, the greater differences between their electrical properties**. Therefore, it is of significant importance for the symmetric devices of a symmetry group to be placed in **close proximity**.

**Definition 2.1 (Symmetry Island)**: A symmetry island is a placement of a symmetry group in which each module in the group abuts at least one of the other modules in the same group, and all modules in the symmetry group form a **connected placement**.

Key distinction illustrated by Fig. 2.6:
- $S_1$ forms a symmetry island when all modules in the symmetry group are placed adjacent to each other (connected placement).
- $S_1$ does **not** form a symmetry island when the placement results in two disconnected components.

The symmetry island is preferred in analog layout design due to its better electrical properties (lower mismatch from the Pelgrom equation).

#### 2.2.3 Review of B\*-Trees

A **B\*-tree** is an ordered binary tree representing a **compacted placement**, in which every module cannot move left and bottom anymore.

- Every node of a B\*-tree corresponds to a module of a compacted placement.
- The **root** of a B\*-tree corresponds to the module on the **bottom-left** corner.
- For each node $n$ corresponding to a module $b$:
  - The **left child** of $n$ represents the lowest, adjacent module on the **right side** of $b$.
  - The **right child** of $n$ represents the first module **above** $b$ with the same horizontal coordinate.

**Coordinate calculation**: Given a B\*-tree, each module's coordinate can be calculated by a **pre-order tree traversal**. Suppose the module $b_i$ represented by the node $n_i$ has the bottom-left coordinate $(x_i, y_i)$, the width $w_i$, and the height $h_i$. Then for the left child $n_j$ of $n_i$: $x_j = x_i + w_i$, and for the right child $n_k$ of $n_i$: $x_k = x_i$. In addition, a contour structure is maintained to calculate the $y$-coordinates.

Starting from the root node (whose bottom-left coordinate is $(0, 0)$), the algorithm visits the root's left subtree, then its right subtree via this preorder tree traversal procedure (B\*-tree packing). This calculates all coordinates of the modules in the placement. Using a doubly-linked list to implement the contour structure, the **total packing time is linear** in the number of modules.

## Algorithms & Methods

### B\*-tree Packing Algorithm

The B\*-tree packing procedure computes all module coordinates from the tree structure:

1. **Initialize**: Set root module at $(0, 0)$. Initialize contour structure (doubly-linked list).
2. **Pre-order traversal**: Visit each node $n_i$ (module $b_i$) in pre-order.
3. **For a left child** $n_j$ of $n_i$:
   - Set $x_j = x_i + w_i$ (place immediately to the right of the parent).
   - Compute $y_j$ from the contour structure (find the maximum $y$ value in the range $[x_j, x_j + w_j]$).
4. **For a right child** $n_k$ of $n_i$:
   - Set $x_k = x_i$ (same $x$-coordinate as the parent).
   - Compute $y_k$ from the contour structure.
5. **Update contour** after placing each module.

**Complexity**: $O(n)$ time for packing $n$ modules using the doubly-linked list contour.

### ASF-B\*-tree (Automatically Symmetric-Feasible B\*-tree)

The chapter introduces the **ASF-B\*-tree** to handle symmetric placement of symmetry groups. Key definitions:

- **Definition 2.2**: The representative $b^r$ of a symmetry pair $(b_i, b_i')$ is $b_i$.
- **Definition 2.3**: The representative $b_R^r$ of a self-symmetric module $b_R^s$ is the **right (top) half** of $b_R^s$ in a symmetric placement with respect to a horizontal (vertical) symmetry axis.
- **Definition 2.4**: A representative B\*-tree is a B\*-tree containing only the representative nodes that correspond to representative modules.
- **Definition 2.5 (Mirrored Placement)**: The mirrored placement of the representative modules for a symmetry group $S$ is to place the nonrepresentative modules on the mirrored positions of the representative ones for each symmetry pair or self-symmetric module in $S$, with respect to its symmetry axis (axes). The representative and the nonrepresentative models of each self-symmetric module are not disjointed.
- **Definition 2.6**: A representative B\*-tree is **symmetric-feasible** if the mirrored placement of the representative modules can be obtained after packing the representative B\*-tree.

The ASF-B\*-tree can represent only **compacted symmetric placements**; in particular, there exists a unique correspondence between a compacted symmetric placement of a symmetry group and its induced ASF-B\*-tree which results in a symmetry island.
